## Te - A

The degree of productivity depends entirely on the situation and its demands. A clear example of this occurs in the context of teaching. When someone assumes the role of a teacher—or simply proposes to teach something—productivity is not measured by the amount of information transmitted, but by the extent to which the other person is able to absorb, understand, and elaborate on the content independently.

  

In this context, it becomes necessary to observe how far the learner’s knowledge can reach through their own effort, considering their capacity for absorption, comprehension, and intellectual elaboration. From that point onward, a genuine process of individual development begins, in which progress must occur without pressures that cause the learner to feel incapable, inferior, or cognitively blocked. Productivity, therefore, consists in recognizing the limits of the environment and fostering advancement from that final boundary.

  

This same principle applies not only to human relationships but also to processes. In this case, assessing productivity means identifying breaking points, bottlenecks, errors, and systematic failures so that the process can advance according to its specific demands and within established expectations. Errors that may be corrected in the future can, in fact, represent advances that were not possible at a given historical moment.

  

An example of this is the computational technology developed during the Second World War, such as the systems conceived by Alan Turing, which later served as the foundation for subsequent technological advances, including personal computers. Although technology has evolved, earlier systems met the demands of their time and were repurposed in new social and productive contexts. Thus, productivity concerns the ability to produce effects that remain efficient even after structural changes.

  

Efficiency, in turn, is directly related to the mode of use. A carpenter, for example, is unlikely to find practical value in advanced structural calculations, but will benefit significantly from proportional calculations. It is not possible to be efficient without understanding the proper use of a given object in relation to its specific purpose within a particular context.

  

Tools exist within a functional grouping and may be reused in other contexts, provided that such reuse does not compromise work efficiency. Quality, therefore, depends strictly on the correct use of the means and resources necessary to achieve a specific purpose without sacrificing the final result or rendering it contingent. Every process must serve the expected outcome, respecting established parameters and the ultimate objective that guides it.

Once the components and their respective functions are defined, consistency and individual responsibility from each member become essential for the project to function in a linear and progressive manner. If this does not occur, point A does not advance properly to point B; and if point B fails to proceed within the required time—performing inadequately the function assigned to it—it compromises an entire chain of subsequent processes. Thus, a snowball effect forms: an accumulation of problems generated by the failure of a single entity to fulfill its role.

  

The logic of work, therefore, consists in the functionality of a system that follows predefined parameters and conditions. Needs are anticipated at the outset, and all subsequent planning exists to ensure continuous progress and the prevention of damage across all processes. A serious error in any of the components will be quickly identified, since no process operates independently of the others. Even if the final outcome is incorrect, the error is rapidly located, allowing the circuit to be corrected and the system’s efficiency to be ensured.

As I stated in T-A and T-B, you must first make contact with the level of performance and the individual capacity to perform of each person who composes a whole; that is, you must understand how and why they do what they do, and what prior foundation they have for doing it. Accordingly, you subject the individual to an initial and progressively more difficult test. For example, in a test of 50 questions, 20% of the margin of error may come from calculations, while the remaining 80% of correct answers derive from practical–theoretical application. This indicates that the individual’s performance is oriented toward the implementation of methods in the field rather than toward calculations themselves. What must be done, therefore, is to guide the individual to understand how calculations can help them become more precise in their practical–theoretical work.

  

When an error occurs, one must first understand the inherent difficulty that accompanies the situation; otherwise, it is like pushing a 20-ton stone with weak arms—it simply does not work. As an instructor, you must create a specific foundation that combines the individual’s strength with their weakness, so that, as a rule, they use their strength to resolve their own weakness. That is, their Strength $(F)$ and their Weakness $(-F)$. The more strength they devote exclusively to learning their weakness, the less attention they give to their strength; however, if their strength operates in function of their weakness, both grow geometrically. When $F$ is used in function of $-F$, we obtain geometric—or moderately exponential—growth, given by:

  

$G(F,-F)=\alpha \times [F(t)\times A(t)]$

  

That is, when we understand that $F(t)$ represents the conscious use of strength over time, and that $A(t)$ represents the attention directed toward one’s own weakness, we can define $G(F,-F)$ as the joint growth between strength and weakness, amplified by a synergy factor represented by $\alpha$, whose condition is $\alpha>1$. This necessarily indicates that, rather than being worked on in isolation, strength and weakness can be functionally integrated. When strength is used as a means to overcome or sustain weakness, the resulting growth ceases to be linear and becomes geometric—meaning that progress accelerates over time.

  

If strength and weakness are treated separately, without interdependence or an integrated strategy, growth occurs only in a linear and slower manner

  

$G(F)+G(-F)\propto t$

  

In other words, true mastery—whether personal, intellectual, or technical—only occurs when strength is placed at the service of one’s own weakness, allowing both to develop mutually within a cycle of reciprocal acceleration and synergistic evolution.

  

For example, I had a friend in Engineering who was very poor at Physics and Mathematics. Essentially, he asked me for help because we were close friends, and the following week there would be an assessment of students’ knowledge of basic physics concepts. For him, it was obviously terrifying. However, I understood his situation: he had had very poor teachers and inadequate instruction. What I did—since he loved history—was to use examples of historical events to explain physics formulas and their manipulations (that is, their different manifestations) according to each case.

  

When I tried to explain to him the equation $F=m\times a$, I initially spent some time focusing solely on the mathematics, but clearly he made no progress. So I asked him whether he knew how Alexander the Great conquered the world, and he replied that he did. I then constructed the following syllogism:

  

(1) Alexander the Great began with a small army.

  

(2) With each conquest, he absorbed new soldiers, increasing his mass of combat.

  

(3) As he maintained the pace of movement and attack (constant acceleration), his total attacking force increased.

  

Thus, the force of Alexander the Great can be expressed as:

  

  $\text{Force (Alexander the Great)}=\text{mass (his army)}\times \text{acceleration (his military expansion)}$

  

Adaptability serves those who wish to learn it. Even without understanding the foundations of mathematics, it is evident that my friend learned in a simple and rapid way. In approximately 13 hours, I was able to introduce mechanics and electrodynamics to him so that he could understand them—using only historical concepts and adapting the explanation to his language.

Participating in the Brazilian Robotics Olympiad (with 300,000 applicants), becoming a Building Technician, earning in dollars working in IT, placing fourth in a federal university program, being a good fighter, having good physical conditioning, being intelligent—all of it seems like a gift combined with the extreme effort I put in (though I do not consider it extreme). There are people I see and even envy for their complacency and ability not to act; it seems impossible to me to conceive the idea of stopping my effort.

  

Until some time ago, I was unable to see how much energy I put into everything I do, and when I do not act, I feel like a loser. That is why, for me, what matters is not “not falling,” but never stopping getting up and walking—then trotting, and running, even wounded, with obstacles and fatigue. I believe this is a virtue.

  

# Ne Section

  

## Ne – A

  

Despite everything, what remains and endures through time—even after one’s first death—are the relationships built over the course of life. I say this because, as one matures, one realizes that people, possessions, material achievements, knowledge, health, illness—all of these come to an end. Therefore, everything one does is ultimately left for others. Thus, despite all our efforts, what gives life meaning is death itself: being able to live for those who cannot and, above all, to enjoy the quality of life that God gives us as a reward for our labor.

  

In the end, what remains as the result of effort is one’s nature, whether good or bad. For this reason, it becomes increasingly important to generate results born of attitudes, commitment to something, discipline, patience, and maturity—because we do not do these things for ourselves, but for others.

  

Thus, one’s second death occurs when others forget you, and this is our condition.

  

After converting to Christianity, I was able to see new perspectives on justice, values, ethics, repentance, future plans, and motivations; on health, nutrition, emotions, and faith. That is, if I die, if I am forgotten, or if I am remembered in a way different from how I would like, God will see me as someone who tried.

  

In other words, the meaning is to be remembered by God—not to be “perfect” in the eyes of others, or to try to be—but to be eternally remembered by Him as someone who never gave up trying, even while being incapable of changing their own nature.

  

This is of an individual nature, yet shared. Consider this: we can indeed be part of a collective, but that does not change the individual structure and experience—namely, one’s internal struggles, which may not be visible externally. That said, the only One who can know you more deeply than you know yourself and therefore never tire of giving you chances is God. For this reason, I believe that the effort and help of a group are important, but never more important than one’s individual relationship with the Father.

  

## Ne – B

  

It is the ability, no matter how difficult it may be, to rebuild one’s paths and try again and again and again. One cannot have potential without knowing where one should advance. To have potential, one must first structure what is lacking and what is desired; even then, it is not concrete—you are still acting in the abstract. The only way to have potential is to transform it into movement, that is, to make possibilities concrete. Otherwise, it is idealism.

  

One cannot believe oneself to be something and not act; both are like time and space. One must be while believing, and must continue believing even if one is not yet, because the possibility is being reduced into a plan. When one visualizes what must be done, what remains is to do it.

  

That said, a person has potential when they recognize the lack of it and, upon perceiving this, direct a possibility into a plan and then into execution.

  

I believe that anyone who is hopeless is a coward. Hopelessness arises as a defense against the belief in an eventual failure—and failure, in this case, is not the problem; the problem arises when one does not try. That is why I say there is more virtue in losing than in saying, “I could have done it,” because when one says, “I failed,” one acted—and acting is something most people do not do. They judge those who act and those who fail, yet lack the strength or courage to confront themselves. This is a weakness of character, a weakness of spirit, and a physical and emotional weakness disguised as superiority.

  

## Ne – C

  

That he is not. The secret to developing in any area is to treat oneself as an eternal student—to always learn. One cannot begin a Christian journey in heaven; it must be acknowledged that whoever seeks is not yet, and if they already were, why would they need to seek?

  

In any situation, one begins from the premise that, at present, one is incapable. The past no longer exists; the future is an unknown fruit of an unknown tree; and the present is the moment to do what must be done without thinking twice, because it may be the last moment. To believe that one already “is” is a blatant defense mechanism and vanity—a fragile ego that cannot bear learning and only wants to teach. But it is already said: want to be the best? Then start with the worst and never leave them.

  

What do I mean by this? In relation to your objective, you will always be worse, but never inferior, because the objective is never in relation to “the other,” but to yourself. Thus, considering oneself inferior can be a way to advance without defending one’s ego or superiority. If you surpass someone at a given time, circumstance, space, and activity, then in that specific moment you were better—and this is not timeless. Luck is what happens when opportunity intimately kisses discipline.

  

## Ne – D

  

By the truthfulness of facts, nature, well-being, and the real consequences it may have. We cannot fall into idealisms—only into certainties, whether through our trust in God, in the flow of life, or in the correct process and plan.

  

How can we rely on guesswork or assumptions? That is wrong and meaningless. Every idea, as a rule, must be realistic; beliefs, however, may or may not be. What matters is to make clear what one wants with an idea, whether it is contemporary or not. Progress depends on many factors. An idea may be good now but not applicable, just as it may be applicable yet terrible and idealistic.

  

## Ne – E

  

It is to understand how much of something applies in other forms that, at first glance, may seem contradictory. For example, when we look at reality as a whole—regardless of our existence—we see that its entire structure is ordered, logical, causal, and perfect in what it proposes to be. That is, within nature itself reside infinite symbolisms and meanings, both mathematical and existential–philosophical.

  

Consider this: the sea prefigures divine substantiality—that is, how God presents Himself to the human being. The light of the sun—the shallower the fish is, closer to the light, the more beautiful, radiant, and aesthetic it becomes. By contrast, fish of the deep sea, where light does not reach, are blind and sensitive to it—symbolizing sin, fear, omission, and indulgence. The deeper a fish is in the abyss, the more deprived of light and the more deformed it becomes, while beings of the same species possess the beauty that light grants them.

  

The same applies to human beings: the closer one is to the Divine, the more beautiful one becomes in soul, spirit, and body—purer, more virtuous, and more compelling. Those who choose to remain distant become increasingly blind and sunk in momentary materialistic illusions. This is the essence of the search for the Divine—not because He needs us, but because we need Him; and the closer we are, the purer and healthier we become, far from danger, clear as a sea of scarlet blue.
