# Section 8

# Feldman's Levels and Pereslegin's Quarcionic Quirks

_(A lyrical digression on Ya. Feldman's theory of levels and the astonishing adventures of Pereslegin's ideas)_

> _I have raised a monument to myself..._ [Pushkin, "Exegi monumentum" — trans.]

When you pick up this handsomely published book, you fill with hope that, having read it, you will learn something very important — something that will help you understand the human being better, for the subject is nothing less than a model of man. Let us see what Feldman writes about himself in the preface and further on in the book. Ya. Feldman wrote and published the book _Theory of Levels and a Model of Man_.

"I, Feldman Yakov Adolfovich, was born in Odessa (Ukraine, USSR) in 1957. According to eyewitnesses, I learned to read at three and a half. By my own recollection, I wrote my first poem at six. At the same age I drew up the first (and last) perpetual-motion-machine scheme of my life. Since then I have periodically lapsed into either poetic or philosophical creativity. I studied the violin, but in the senior grades transferred to a mathematics school. In 1973 I entered Moscow University (the Faculty of Computational Mathematics and Cybernetics) and graduated in 1978. I stayed in Moscow and worked as a programmer. Having convinced myself that the practical work of a programmer does not load the brain, I set about studying everything in sight. I wrote my first philosophical articles and showed up with them at the philosophy department of Moscow State University, where I was shown the door. At that time I found myself a philosophy tutor (A. V. Surin) and studied with him until (after about two years) I began writing articles again. Having convinced myself that the content of the articles contradicted 'the line of the Party and the government,' and not wishing to enter into direct conflict with 'the Party and the government,' I gave up philosophy — for good, it seemed. At the same time I again began writing poems and sending them to journals, receiving standard rejections."

**Commentary:** Of course, we do not now hold it against Feldman that the content of his articles contradicted "the line of the Party and the government" — though one might see something rebellious and anarchic in this — but, alas, it is hard to judge the actual content of the works written back then. Only one thing is clear: the author liked them. Let us proceed on the assumption that those articles were no worse than the book the author has offered us. The author's brief self-characterization creates the image of a person who is not only talented but many-sided. Moreover, the biographical data given in the introduction point to a high level of inventiveness and enterprise, which allows one to suppose the author's TIM is LIE — though I still think it is ILE. If so, the author's self-assurance about his fundamental rightness in any matter where he considers himself competent becomes understandable. But then, such are people.

Here are the author's reflections on the theme of model and law.

**Feldman:** _The school physics course contains a section on "the theory of gases." It begins with the story of how the laws of Boyle–Mariotte, Charles, and Gay-Lussac were discovered. All these laws established the dependence of certain observable quantities on other observable quantities under certain observable conditions. These laws show how some phenomena are connected with other phenomena; they are called phenomenological._

**Commentary:** It is commendable, of course, that the author remembers the school physics course and draws methodological conclusions from that knowledge. A wholly insignificant stylistic defect, which only a meticulous editor would notice, is the third repetition of the adjective "observable," here applied to the conditions. The point is that in such contexts one usually speaks of conditions as _given_, since a law is formulated with respect to _specified_ conditions.

**Feldman:** _For modern science, however, this is not enough. For every bouquet of phenomenological laws, modern science tries to build a model within which those phenomenological laws can be obtained as theoretical laws — that is, derived by logical reasoning from the simple principles (axioms, postulates, first principles) on which the model is built._

**Commentary:** The axiomatic approach to constructing a theory is not an invention of modern science; historically it was reliably used by Euclid to prove geometrical theorems. As for "bouquets of phenomenological laws," this is a rather strange expression, since the concept of a law carries a seme of fundamentality, and it is hard to imagine that any modern science contains bouquets of fundamental laws. If the author means empirical regularities, then of course that is what he should write, so as not to mislead the inexperienced reader and not to provoke the experienced one.

**Feldman:** _Science (Engl.) is the study of all phenomena and processes in which human participation is not essential (though not excluded in principle) — for example, geology or biology._

_Humanity (Engl.) is the study of all phenomena and processes where human participation is an essential factor (for example, archaeology or the history of science)._

**Commentary:** Here, on the one hand, it is completely unclear why the terms long used in Russian — "natural sciences and the humanities" — need to be replaced with Anglicisms. The answer apparently comes down to the author having studied the subject in America, so the Russian terms are simply unknown to him. From this one may suppose that other concepts used in Russian scientific methodology, as well as in the history and philosophy of science, are also unknown to the author.

**Feldman:** _Recognition-naming, then differentiation-demarcation — that is, the first and second levels. Before that there was also the zeroth level — falling into the inarticulate stream._

**Commentary:** Such is the stylistic improvisation — many beautiful and portentous words, each evoking a mass of associations, but where these words come from and what they mean the author apparently considers self-evident.

**Feldman:** _To trace one line requires the third level; to trace two lines in parallel, the fourth. But that is at the moment of perception. At the moment of writing, one had to trace, from without, from an external observation point, the existence of one system, and then of two systems in parallel — the seventh and eighth levels respectively._

**Commentary:** Reader, you understand everything here — while the author, it seems, understands everything too, yet cannot manage to explain it to the reader. Well, perhaps he will succeed later.

**Feldman:** _I will never agree with the neo-Kantians that science is built according to one set of laws and humanity according to another, "for such is the nature of these things." On the contrary, I have always held and still hold that science differs from humanity as a pie differs from raw dough — only in the degree of maturity of the cognitive process, the degree of our advancement along the path of knowledge, while the path itself is the same in either case. Moreover, to "bake the pie through," to bring humanity up to the level of science, is what I have set as my life's task._

**Commentary:** To put it simply: regarding the neo-Kantians I agree with the author, and I too believe that opposing the natural sciences and the humanities at the level of methodological requirements means capitulating before the difficulties of cognition. But the author's style of argumentation is of so intuitive-metaphorical a character that one cannot even be sure he will be able to bring his proofs up to the level normative for science.

**Feldman:** _If the work of the individual branches of humanity is not perceived merely as applied research for solving concrete problems (the sociologist helps a political party, the psychologist advises on career choice, the historian of science fits out a museum-apartment), but one imagines instead that all these particular studies are gathering facts describing the existence of one single object of knowledge — MAN — then everything falls into place. The facts turn out to be so numerous that astronomy and geology can only envy._

**Commentary:** Very optimistic — but where is the main idea?

**Feldman:** _To collate the data of such distant branches as the history of science, the history of literature, the history of art, political history, political science, psychology, sociology, philosophy, logic, and pedagogy, one needs a general model of the central subject of these sciences — a model of MAN. And it must be such that the data of any of these sciences can be interpreted on this model, for only such data will be of use._

**Commentary:** So the author, drawing on the concepts of scientific methodology, wants to build a model of man.

**Feldman:** _But here one more historical digression is necessary._

_I studied at university in 1973–1978, under Brezhnev, in the USSR. In those years every institution of higher education compulsorily taught and examined "the social sciences," a.k.a. "Marxism-Leninism": the history of the CPSU, dialectical and historical materialism, political economy, scientific communism. Properly speaking, this whole set was supposed to create in the student the illusion that humanity was no less advanced than science. But why was this illusion needed? "Children, don't go walking in Africa." If research in a certain direction is advancing successfully and its results are superb, then other directions are unnecessary, and the results can be neither improved nor called into question. Thus the illusion "this direction is successful" and the illusion "these results are true" propped each other up for quite a long time._

**Feldman:** _Life refuted Marxism in practice — it collapsed together with the Berlin Wall (November 9, 1989)._

**Commentary:** What was refuted was not Marxism but its perversion. Marxism was not one bit worse than the Western humanities, and science in Russia fully met industrial needs; but Russia's semi-literate rulers thought everything was fine with them and did not understand that the West was making enormous efforts to harm Russia, employing the full might of its science to that end.

**Feldman:** _Is science a thing beyond the power of a single person, as Marx thought? And not even one science, but exactly half of all the sciences — humanity? To demolish someone else's ready-made worldview, one person is enough, provided he thinks systematically (6) and paradoxically (7)._

**Commentary:** So we are offered a recipe for demolishing worldviews. Feldman thinks systematically and paradoxically; consequently, he is about to demolish some worldview.

**Feldman:** _I managed to supplement my abilities with the abilities of my wife. The result is before you._

**Commentary:** If what Feldman writes is what he considers a result, it is hard even to imagine what a lamentable state he was in before meeting his wife.

**Feldman:** _...the space of problems is inhomogeneous; it does not resemble a boundless plain. There exist within it, like oases in a desert or mountain peaks, special points, "points of condensation." The study of the problem space itself, detached from momentary practice, and especially the study of the "points of condensation" of this space (I shall speak of "points of aesthetic condensation," for their study satisfies my aesthetic sense above all) — this is the only way to leap beyond the proverb "generals are always preparing for the last war."_

**Commentary:** Feldman speaks like a sage — mostly in parables. Presumably so that people will understand. But so far I simply cannot grasp what he is trying to say.

**Feldman:** _A model of man. TO. Three subsystems._

_Our many years of observation indicate that a model of man must include, first of all, the following groups of parameters (subsystems):_ _1) Warm-up_ _2) Coding_ _3) Levels_

**Commentary:** Well, at last. Understood: first there will be a warm-up. As simple folk say, for warming up... Then we'll be coded, and brought up to level.

**Feldman:** _We shall devote most of the book to the levels, but without the first two subsystems the system will not work. Important to remember: warm-up acts over a short stretch of time; coding increases or decreases response time; levels manifest themselves over temporal (substantive) intervals longer than one action, one problem, one situation._

**Commentary:** Aha — everything will become clear at the very end...

**Feldman:** _While acknowledging the physiological nature of temperament and considering it very important for building a model of man, we believe this concept needs refining._

**Commentary:** Generally speaking, to refine something, one must first define it.

**Feldman:** _Whatever a person is engaged in, his activity at any moment of time has a definite intensity._

**Commentary:** No — he never did say what temperament is, but switched the conversation to intensity. That is probably how he coded temperament. In sum, as I understand it, for Feldman temperament is both intensity and warm-up. Somewhat unscientific, perhaps, but possibly very effective.

**Feldman:** _Intensity can be measured in various ways (by operations per second, if the operations are of one kind; by the number of errors, if errors are possible; by the speed and accuracy of movements; by the electrical resistance of the skin; by the adrenaline level in the blood, etc.)._

**Commentary:** Translated into ordinary language, this means that temperament, a.k.a. warm-up, can be measured by all the methods listed in parentheses. Psychology, as it happens, says nothing of the sort — but perhaps a discovery awaits us here.

**Feldman:** _However, whatever we measure this quantity with, if we plot it along the time axis ("the warm-up curve"), the following simple regularities will emerge:_

_1) the warm-up curve has a periodic character over the working day. If we discard from the time scale the intervals of sleep and deep rest, the curve becomes periodic. As befits the definition, all periods have the same shape;_

**Commentary:** Psychology says nothing of the kind about temperament... In any case, it is highly doubtful that temperament would change periodically over the course of a day. I have never heard such a thing.

**Feldman:** _2) the shape of the period changes little over a person's lifetime (excluding periods of ill health);_

**Commentary:**

**Feldman:** _3) if we examine the curves of different people, they fall into four groups (with random variations within each group). The average curve of each group corresponds to one temperament._

**Commentary:** If what is shown in the figure really is the graphs of temperaments-warm-ups, then either this is a discovery or some sort of misunderstanding. But it looks very much like the intuitive images of temperament in the author's imagination. Well then — a rich imagination...

_(Figure: "Working capacity" vs. "Time"; curves labeled 1. Choleric, 2. Sanguine, 3. Phlegmatic, 4. Melancholic)_

**Feldman:** _Unlike temperament (which is never "pure"), the warm-up curve can precisely convey the characteristics of a given person. There are only four temperaments, but infinitely many curves!_

_Note that the choleric and the melancholic ideally complement ((no!)) each other if they work sequentially (but not in parallel)._

**Commentary:** So it turns out I was mistaken: the picture shows not temperaments but warm-up curves — but then why are temperaments written on it? I am forced to state that the author's thinking is somehow blurry and poorly organized...

**Feldman:** _Different people perceive, store, process, and output information differently._

The opinion that this was first noticed by the school of Neuro-Linguistic Programming is inaccurate. According to Alfred Binet, the division of people by types of memory (auditory, visual, motor) was known already to his teacher J.-M. Charcot. Binet himself discovered "thinking in schemas" and "imageless thought" (he also knew the term "warming up").

**Commentary:** But what is the author's own opinion?

**Feldman:** _The "visuals – audials – kinesthetics" of NLP form the first triad of codings._

_There are five triads in all, and they often mask one another:_

_Triads_ _Channels of perception: Ear Eye Hand_ _Mode of storage and processing: Text Picture Schema_ _Mode of existence in culture: Book Film Computer_ _Stage of thinking: Images Meanings Scenarios_ _Kind of activity: Communication Reflection Action_

**Commentary:** The author has called his chosen triads "codings." Somewhat unclear. Codings of what? He is a programmer by profession — evidently the word is dear to him, and he is capable of investing it with various meanings. It seems the author is sharing everything he knows. And he knows quite a lot. He is, as it were, building his own private science. Still, it would be worth doing this on the basis of scientific methodology. The author has his own private methodology. Well then, let us see...

**Feldman:** _The final result of thinking is precisely action._

**Commentary:** This is plausible as an empirical fact — but after all, one thought can beget another, and so on. As with philosophers or writers. Indeed, the result of the author's thinking turns out to be thoughts, which he writes down in a book.

**Feldman:** _Action is the realization of a scenario. A scenario can be invented oneself (text–picture–schema) on the basis of perceptual data (ear–eye–hand), or it can be borrowed from "social consciousness" (book–film–computer). When a scenario runs, checking of conditions is inevitable within it. To check a condition, one must single out an object for checking (an image) and compute some function on that object (a meaning). The same images and meanings can be used in different scenarios; that is, they are relatively independent. Communication is the joint construction of scenarios-images-meanings. Reflection is their independent construction, seen from outside._

_Images – meanings – scenarios are discussed in [2.28]._

_Individual preferences and proportions for a given person exist in each triad. Knowing these preferences, one can say a great deal about a person. But the very knowledge of the triads as a system of concepts can itself explain many phenomena of our life._

_The striving, well known in American literature, to go beyond a single triad (multiple intelligences) is, in our conviction, merely one step on the way to a system of many triads._

**Feldman:** _There exist 10 levels in all, numbered 0 through 9 [2.2]. In practical life, however, we will almost never encounter levels 0 and 9. So if you hear that there are 8 levels, numbered 1,...,8, do not be surprised._

**Commentary:** Just like that — without definitions, without justification, without ceremony.

**Feldman:** _Levels exist objectively. Why — we shall learn later. "Why" is a level-6 question, and in our exposition we are so far working through levels 1, 2, 3._

**Commentary:** How interesting and mysterious...

**Feldman:** _Here are some "earmarks" of the levels, so as to recognize and distinguish them (i.e., to work at levels 1–2). Lining them up, we will reach (in our understanding) level 3._

**Commentary:** We do not yet know — do not even suspect — what levels are. The author possibly knows what they are but keeps up the intrigue. And he is not yet ready to give a strict definition — because the reader has not yet warmed up, and can only be told certain earmarks of the levels. The full answer the author promises at the end. Though possibly he himself will find it out at the end, or nearer to it.

**Feldman:** _Inarticulate stream of events. Recognition-naming, one object, a thing. Differentiation-demarcation, many objects, space. Ordering, passage to the limit, one process, time, striving toward a goal. Series, many processes, a role system, an ensemble, narration. Table-classifier, one map, free will, risk. Model-theory, many maps, causality, proof, truth. Contradiction-paradox, one system, an idea. Harmony, many systems, accounting for distant consequences. Optimal instantaneous action._

_Lengthening the lists of features takes us to the fourth level (series). Naturally, this list of features is not complete; the complete list will be accessible only at the 5th level of understanding (table-classifier)._

**Commentary:** It seems the reader has already passed the level called "Inarticulate stream of events" and is beginning "Recognition-naming" — "one object, a thing" — more than that we cannot yet manage, but the wait won't be long. So, reader, brace yourself — surprises may well lie ahead. But an experienced captain is leading you, and with him you will soon conquer "Differentiation-demarcation, many objects, space."

**Feldman:** _We proceed to level 4._

**Commentary:** Reader, prepare yourself!

**Feldman:** _Imagine a meeting of two sides (two processes, two styles of behavior). For simplicity, suppose each side has fixed (for the duration of the meeting) its level. What will their interaction (conflict) lead to? It turns out the consequences can be foreseen._

**Commentary:** Here it is! Now you will learn to foresee — well, not everything, of course. You still have to grow into that.

|#|Relation|Formula|Type|Result|
|---|---|---|---|---|
|1|Levels equal|M = N|Competition|Mutual pressure, unstable equilibrium|
|2|Levels adjacent|\|M−N\| = 1|Cooperation|The senior manages, the junior helps; cooperation voluntary and natural|
|3|Levels distant|\|M−N\| > 1|War|Aggression of the lower against the higher. The higher perishes, or flees, or changes level|

**Feldman:** _This table is not only explanatory and prognostic but of entirely practical character. However, for the theory to become practice, one must learn to see the opponent's level — and in response tune oneself to the level optimal in the given situation. To what extent is such self-tuning possible in principle?_ And of course it is hard to agree that the table exhausts all variants of human interaction — where, for instance, is collaboration? So we are being misled.

**Commentary:** Reader, remember this. Explanatory and prognostic is not yet practical, because you have not yet learned to see your own level. Patience — a little more, and you will be able to use the explanations and forecasts in practice.

**Feldman:** _Every person has his own genotype, which means his own particular structure of the cellular-hormonal system and his own particular brain proportions. This means that however you train yourself, one activity (A) will always come out better for you than another activity (B). What, then, can be done?_

**Commentary:** You see, reader? You didn't know you had "your own particular brain proportions," and that is why certain things will always come out better for you. Well, do you understand why? It's all a matter of brain proportions. Remember that.

**Feldman:** _One can ensure that any activity comes out no worse than a certain minimally acceptable level._

**Commentary:** A well-known truth, but delivered with such aplomb that it inspires hope. And the main thing is, all this can be done without changing one's brain proportions.

**Feldman:** _One can ensure that you are able to pass freely from activity A to activity B when you yourself want to or deem it necessary — not as the activity "wants." That is, to "become the master of your own actions." A nontrivial task — recall gamblers, smokers, etc._

**Commentary:** Well — he recalled gamblers and smokers, and so what?

**Feldman:** _One can discover that certain levels have been blocked in you by upbringing, while to certain others upbringing has assigned absolute value. Both push you toward suboptimal decisions and therefore require conscious correction._

**Commentary:** All perfectly clear — and no worse than the Delphic oracle.

**Feldman:** _He who is not acquainted with the theory of levels will not even be able to pose these tasks for himself. He who manages to pose them and begins solving them will improve himself and improve the world._

**Commentary:** But it is understood that nobody is acquainted with the theory of levels, since we are present at its birth. No, no... it has not yet been born, but it is already moving down the birth canal, and soon it will happen... And you will learn it, and everything will start working out for you.

**Feldman:** _Must be remembered: In every subject area all levels are represented and have a right to exist. However, in each area there are levels which (as of today) enter into the concept of "a professional in the given area." For example, in mathematics a professional must display level 6 (no mathematical fact without proof)._

**Commentary:** Whereas in Feldman's theory of levels, one may state facts without proofs — because he not only knows the theory of levels but is its creator.

**Feldman:** _Every level has civilized forms of manifestation in society and a subject area in which its manifestation is especially advantageous both to the person and to society. For example, team sports require level 4, individual sports level 3._

**Commentary:** Oh, this too must be remembered...

**Feldman:** _My observations show that every person has by nature three favorite levels at which he prefers to act. Until July 2003 we (Ya.F. and E.) held that these three levels are equal in the personality profile. But on August 3, 2003 we published the article "Shakespeare's Tragedies and the Levels of His Characters," where we formulated a new rule: One of the three levels is auxiliary — it chooses means; the other two are principal — they choose the goal (alternately and in equal measure)._

**Commentary:** You see: before July 2003 there was no such thing, and now there is — although the author conceals from the reader what a level actually is.

**Feldman:** _A pseudo-conflict in our model is the meeting of two sides differing in warm-up and/or coding. If these sides try to work synchronously on the same material, a moment comes when the impossibility of such synchronous work becomes obvious. Subjectively this is experienced as a conflict — sometimes quite an acute one. Yet such a conflict is easily avoided: it suffices to cancel the requirement of synchrony._

**Commentary:** Correct. For example, if some work by day, others can work by night. All the more so since many buildings go unused at night. This applies especially to administrative buildings: by day officials work there, and by night the same premises could be used as hotels, or for dry-cleaning. Or for who knows what else...

**Feldman:** _Why do some people make discoveries and others not? A (theoretical) discovery is a nontrivial mental step. Not everyone can take such steps. It is a step forward from a certain volume of initial data. Not everyone has that volume of data._

_I am convinced that many of my readers could take the step. It is simply that each life has turned out its own way, and the volume of initial data that appeared on my path did not appear on theirs (or they attached no importance to it). But this can be remedied. I will show the data I relied on as I went toward my discovery._

**Commentary:** Now the author will share the secret of how to make discoveries like the ones he made. And immediately afterward each of us will be able to make some discovery or other and write just such a book — maybe even a better one.

**Feldman:** _Cybernetics, as you of course know, studies control and communication in various systems — including the system "man," the system "computer," the system "state," etc._

_Cybernetics forces us (its students) to look anew at things simple and, it would seem, long and absolutely understood. Here, for example, are two systems: a doorbell and a machine selling carbonated water with syrup (there were such in the era of my childhood)._

**Commentary:** No — he did not share it. He told us about a doorbell and about the carbonated water of his childhood. Later he will tell us about beads — how they can be scattered, about their topology, about dots on a screen...

**Feldman:** _Imagine that the number of random dots on the screen is increasing. Imagine that a string of beads is depicted on the screen. Starting from a certain (large) number of random dots, we will see on the screen that the string has broken (though the real string is still intact). Let us now formulate two general rules:_

_For loss of complexity not to occur in a mapping, a certain minimal intrinsic internal complexity of the mapping system is necessary. The complexity of the mapping system is measured by the maximal complexity of the pre-image that is not yet lost in the mapping. It remains to define the grades of complexity of the pre-image (the levels)._

**Commentary:** Well, here we come to the definition. It is not very sharp, but still — something at least. So: levels are grades of complexity of the pre-image. Note — not of the object itself, but of its pre-image. Not even of the image. But the highlighted part of the text deserves closer analysis. Here a genuinely important concept of complexity is introduced — important for defining level. Yet the author gives no definition of it, reporting instead that "the complexity of the mapping system is measured by the maximal complexity of the pre-image that is not yet lost in the mapping." Note that what is at issue is not the complexity of the object but the complexity of the mapping system. It turns out that what matters to the author is not the complexity of the real object but the complexity of, say, the camera or computer that will display the object. Of course, we could guess on the author's behalf what he must mean — after all, we already know what levels of complexity are, because we were taught it and it is written about in other books.

**Feldman:** _A string of beads is more complex than scattered beads. But how many beads are scattered (10, 15, or 20) does not affect the complexity. However, if the beads are removed one by one, a moment will come when one bead remains. If there was one bead on the string, the beads cannot be scattered; if more than one — they can. This means that (for us, here and now) the number one differs qualitatively from the numbers two, three, etc., while the remaining numbers (2, 3, 4, ...) are qualitatively identical._

**Commentary:** With the bead example the author wants to introduce some concept important to him — but it seems he cannot cope even with the beads. He handles concepts so casually because _he_ knows what he means; us he does not tell, yet assumes we can guess. But I, for one, do not understand why the number of scattered beads does not affect complexity. I was taught, for instance, that complexity is connected with quantity. Nor do I agree that a single bead cannot be scattered — have you never dropped some small object and then had to search for it? That is the minimal case of "scattering," but it does exist, and there is no sense in neglecting it. To scatter one bead means it slipped off the string. But the author gives no definitions and can therefore mean anything whatsoever. This example shows that the author gets muddled even with a single bead — which means he cannot be trusted.

It turns out that this whole manipulation with beads was needed by the author to convince us of the qualitative difference of the number 1 from all the rest.

**Feldman:** _Here we come into contradiction with Hegel's famous formula "the transition of quantity into quality": in our opinion, a new quality arises in the transition from 1 to 2, but does not arise with further increase of quantity. The new quality is brought in from outside as an order relation that did not exist before!_

**Commentary:** So now the author fancies he has refuted Hegel. Reader, do not believe the author — he has not refuted the great dialectician; he is simply muddling something again. He gives no definition of quality, so the reader must guess for himself what quality arises in the transition from 1 to 2. Had quality been suitably defined, the author would have seen that all numbers have a quality, each its own — but the author has not yet made that discovery.

Perhaps I simply do not understand the author. Perhaps I am unfair to him, too much of a nitpicker. Perhaps readers will be found who will read his book with pleasure. Therefore I refer those who wish to the author's own text — let them appraise it themselves. It is quite possible someone will enjoy how the author, relying on his discovery, analyzes the characters of plays, or the stages of personality formation according to E. Erikson, or the child's mental development according to Piaget, or poems, English ones included, or chess — it is entertaining, and the reader can pass the time quite pleasantly. At the end of the book the author tells of his poetic work. And although this has no direct bearing on the FLs (Feldman Levels), it does vividly illuminate his talent as a poet. His poems, his translations in particular, are simply good, and his analysis of poetic works is not inferior to the best examples of that genre.

I do not wish to weary the reader further — my conclusion is clear. The author is by no means a stupid man; he knows a great deal, though superficially. There is much he wants to share with the reader, but he offers too many banalities — and outright disinformation besides. Tell me, how is one to understand the first line of the table "Layers of Being"? The zeroth layer is called "Non-being," yet its values are being, with the value criterion life? And the table "Elements" shows that the layer of being called "Non-being" is filled with the primal element "Ether," and the state of matter there is called "Void." So ether _is_ non-being? Has science proved that? I understand, of course, that the author undertakes to explain everything in his book — but what about common sense? Reader, do you know what a "chronotope" is? The meaning of the word is determined by the meaning of its parts: "chronos" = time + "topos" = place; that is, it can be understood as "a place in time," or "a stretch of time." The author calls chronotopes, for example, "the development of world literature." Well, in some metaphorical sense, sure, why not... His list of such chronotopes includes: the Individual Researcher, Science, Logic, Man, Humanity. With this term he equates concepts of entirely different scale and content. And here is what the author says about Hegel: "Hegel asserts that the following chronotopes are similar. Objective spirit = nature up to and including man." Yet Hegel simply could not have asserted anything of the kind — for the simple reason that he never used such a word at all. The author's text resembles a kaleidoscope: you are entertained with motley pictures, and this goes on for a long time. Yet all the while they are trying to convey to you one single, long-familiar thought: that everything in the world develops, growing more complex, passing through definite stages. A pity it is done so chaotically.

## Conclusion [on Feldman]

Reading this book, one is left with the impression that it was written by a person who knows a great deal, but whose knowledge is somehow loose — a little about everything, banal and effortless. The text of this book can in no way be called scientific — methodologically it falls short. It is closest to something philosophico-scientistic, popular-worldview-ish, science-domestic; one could even assign it to an original genre: a light retelling of literature once read, with elements of pattern-seeking. Something like "A Popular Introduction to Pre-programming" — that is, a mention of items of information that might raise a programmer's competence. Feldman is trying to give shape to the idea of levels, which he apparently senses intuitively at the empirical level but cannot articulate, remaining at the level of a vague image. He writes as though before him no one had ever spoken of or known anything about levels — whereas this is one of the most widespread themes in science and philosophy. References to predecessors — anything like a literature review — are absent. Instead there is a multitude of textual illustrations from philosophy, sociology, political economy, and other branches of knowledge, where it is said outright that these are levels. Nevertheless, the text stirs the imagination with hints of something significant and important — but this effect is connected not with real content but with a special linkage of words, well studied in NLP. The book lacks the key definition: what are the levels the author has in mind? He gives a large number of examples from various subject areas, from which one may conclude that in every subject area there are less complex and more complex objects — which, of course, is no secret to specialists in any sphere of science, technology, or economics.

Thus K. Marx speaks of a hierarchy of socio-economic formations: the primitive-communal system, to which corresponds a natural economy; the slaveholding system — an economy based on slave labor; the feudal system — an economy based on the labor of serfs; capitalism — an economy based on the labor of hired workers; socialism — an economy based on the labor of free workers receiving just pay for their work; communism — an economy based on the free realization of every citizen's abilities, when material provision covers all his cultural needs. Here, of course, we are dealing with qualitative levels of the development of human society. The hierarchy of chemical elements fixed in the periodic table, where each atom represents a definite level of the development of matter, formed under definite conditions. The evolution of the animal and plant world consists through and through of levels of the origin of species. Plato writes of the changing character of power in the state. The state is created by sages, and they are respected for their wisdom. But a time comes when the state must be defended, and heroes and generals come to the fore. And when the state is defended, it begins to grow rich, and the richest become the most influential. But wealth is distributed unevenly, and gradually a marked division of people along the wealth–poverty index takes place. The poor folk — the demos — begin to hate the plutocrats leading their unrighteous life, and begin to smash them. Democracy is enthroned — an unjust order in which the victorious majority rules for its own benefit rather than that of the whole society. In this description we see levels of social change. But in a settled society, sooner or later, a social pyramid takes shape. Those at the very bottom in Ancient India were called vaishyas, with whom it was improper to associate. Above them stood the merchant-shudras. Higher still the kshatriya-warriors, headed by the chief commander. But everywhere there was also the class of learned bookmen — brahmins — standing as it were above all and mediating between the Higher Powers and all the people in society. These, of course, are level structures.

Even household things have their evolution with fixed levels. A multitude of books have been written on these themes, so no signs of novelty can be detected in Feldman's "discovery." Yet for analyzing various situations he uses the concept of level with virtuosity, creating an _effect_ of explanation. Marx, explaining the succession of social formations, pointed to the cause behind it — the change of productive forces, which take shape as material means of production. The explanation Feldman offers is phenomenological: one ideal process is explained by means of another. Something like: people feud because they have different levels — as if people of the same level could not feud. But they feud not because of level, but in connection with concrete material causes. ## Conclusion [on Pereslegin]

Of course, Pereslegin's quarcionics is a theoretical joke. By the large account, it does not withstand methodological scrutiny. But the very idea of playing with three poles is interesting and deserves attention. Can three-pole systems exist in reality? Well, there is such a thing as three-phase current... But that is merely a technical refinement, and current itself has only two poles. As for Shchedrovitsky's triad, it is an empirical construct, made for the occasion. There exists no proof that the three categories of this construct form a complete set. And in the set Statics, Dynamics, Spontanics, the last category has been artificially attached — which means one could go on attaching further items. For example, some sort of Relaxonics, providing a smooth transition between statics and dynamics. Or, say, Virtuonics, transferring the other categories onto an unreal plane where they can trade places and completely change their essence — when, for example, statics becomes dynamics and dynamics statics. Of course, there are people for whom this is hard to imagine; but there are also those for whom it is elementary.

What matters is that the three-pole system be real, not invented — for everything built on the invented will itself be invented.

Well, all right — but what about the Trinity? Alas, that is a truncated set: it lacks a fourth element. It is there, but masked for special reasons. It is the Mother of God. In short, if you come up with a _real_ three-pole system, come to me — we'll discuss it.

On the whole, though, one must give Pereslegin his due — his creative potential proved to be at its height. But fantasy still needs to be controlled by reality. So that, you know, the result may be real. But I respect Balzacs all the same...
