# Section 1

## Yermak Objects to Gulenko — Gulenko Objects to Yermak, and Seryozha Savchenko Wanders in the Dark…

_(Fragments from V. D. Yermak's analysis of certain judgments by V. V. Gulenko, occasioned by the latter's assessment of the former's theoretical constructions — with my commentary.)_

> — Good health to you, kuma… — I was at the market…

The most serious attempt to justify the IAs [information aspects] and Model A was made first by N. N. Medvedev, and Yermak followed in his footsteps; trying to improve Medvedev's approach and slightly altering the "first building blocks" the latter had used, he in fact made Medvedev's attempt worse. Nor can Yermak's approach to justifying Model A be considered successful. Having partially drawn on ideas from systems theory, Yermak proved inconsistent in identifying the semantics of the functions — his version did not come out the way Aushra's did, but he apparently considered his variant impeccable and fully consistent with Aushra's. So from then on he relied on Model A as if he himself had constructed it in his own work. I discussed all of this in detail in [4], but my analysis received no academic response whatsoever, so I too am entitled henceforth not to reckon with Yermak's constructions, which I consider erroneous.

**Yermak:** First of all, I want to thank Viktor Vladimirovich Gulenko for the entirely correct assessment of my conception of socionics as systemic, positivist, structuralist… [1]

**Commentary:** What is interesting here is that one scholar accepts another scholar's generalized worldview-methodological assessment of his conception.

**Yermak:** I do indeed hold that the psychic functions are certain processors that process particular aspects of the information flow of the psyche's interaction (the psyche itself being a macroprocessor) with the surrounding world, on the principle "information flow — processor" (and not "raw material — tool," as V. V. Gulenko interprets it [1]).

**Commentary:** The categories "information flow — processor" and "raw material — tool" relate to each other as species and genus concepts, and there is no fundamental disagreement here: Yermak's construct is more concrete, Gulenko's somewhat more general. As for saying of the functions that they are processors, that is no more than a variety of explication in which one term is replaced by an equivalent term from another subject domain.

**Yermak:** Scientific and life experience (and not only mine) convinces me that models and modeling are, for now, the only effective toolkit for investigating the psyche and obtaining practically valuable results. And not only because the psyche is still intangible and there are no suitable measuring instruments, but above all because the psyche will long remain a supremely complex object with numerous parameters, hard to access for direct (let alone "descriptive," natural-language) study by the psyche of another human investigator.

**Commentary:** Here I see no contradiction with the use of the modeling method as established in socionics, but I want to warn at once that in the works of Gulenko and Yermak a substitution has occurred: Model A has been replaced by Models "G" and "E." The first of these leads to a model with a different semantics of the functions than Aushra Augustinavichiute's, while the second seriously distorts the very conception of Model A. As a result, a significant portion of socionists were misled for a long time — all the more so because both authors, having changed the content of Model A, kept its form and name, which is, in essence, a forgery.

**Yermak:** I am convinced that the highly dubious achievements (in any case, the absence of notable breakthroughs) in the study of psychic and social processes in the twentieth century are explained, not least, precisely by the separation of the humanities, which do not use models, from the natural sciences. To this day this separation is actively maintained precisely by the humanists, despite the fact that it became an anachronism half a century ago.

**Commentary:** Here I agree with V.D. almost completely, except for the claim that in psychology over the last 50–60 years models have supposedly not been used — one can read about this at least in [6] and [7], as well as in my book [4, chapter 4].

**Yermak:** Gulenko's critique of my conception was not conducted entirely correctly. Judging by [1], he dislikes it (the conception) because it is based on: — the natural-science paradigm; — the systems approach and, in particular, the concept of control as the starting position for identifying the structure of the psyche; — the principle of the information metabolism of the psyche, which separates the information processes in the psyche and in communicants from their carriers — the material and energetic components; — the subjective character of the human being's interaction with the surrounding world, from which follows the aspect structure of the information flow; though this last point V. V. Gulenko seems not to have noticed.

**Commentary:** 1) I adhere to the natural-science paradigm, which in my case consists in this: the world exists objectively, that is, independently of any subject's representations of it, and the individual psyche, being the bearer of subjectivity, adapts effectively to the objective world and, above all, to its most fundamental structures. However, I disagree with Yermak, and also with N. N. Medvedev, whose conception Yermak adapted to his own understanding of Medvedev's "first building blocks," as I wrote in detail in the 2nd chapter of [4]. There are logical errors in Medvedev's — and even more so in Yermak's — understanding of the "first building blocks," and without eliminating them it is not possible to preserve this, on the whole, beautiful approach.

2. The systems approach is a very strong position, to which, it seems, there is nothing even to oppose. However, in Aushra's reasoning there is from the very beginning a deeper approach — reliance on the thermodynamic image of the four-stroke engine, which Yermak seems simply to ignore. At the base of the entire phenomenology of the world lies, above all, energy. And the laws of energy's existence are formulated precisely in thermodynamics, and they precede systems phenomenology.

It must be specially noted that Yermak includes in the concept of a system, besides substance, energy, and information, also "signals of rhythmics," which of course have an informational nature — and this small but important detail indicates that logic is positioned not in the EGO block, and consequently Yermak's TIM is not LII.

3. Of course, one should write "the principle of information metabolism _in_ the psyche," but perhaps this is an editorial oversight. It is precisely IM that polarizes the physiological and the psychic, which must not be reduced to each other; rather, one must discover their specificity and their deep mutual connection.

**Commentary:** Here is a vivid example of practicing eclecticism. What matters to the authors is not the theoretical quality of the methods used but the possibility of selling them effectively. The main thing here is the product's bright packaging, its easy memorability, and elements of fashionable prestige embodied in the terminology. Yet this too has a certain relation to socionics, since the basic categories of Jung, which underlie it, are used.

4. I disagree with Yermak that the human being's interaction with the surrounding world is of a purely subjective character. Subjectivity is conditioned, above all, by a person's individual interests, which are of course closely tied to personal history, to circumstances of a person's life that are difficult to account for and may often appear accidental. A certain contribution to human subjectivity is made by the species adaptation of _homo sapiens_, but this contribution is nonetheless lawful and can be accounted for. The contribution to individual subjectivity from socionic typology is entirely lawful and controllable. As for the aspect structure of the information flow, it exists prior to any perception of it by any subject whatsoever and is objective. This is the fundamental difference between my position and the positions of Yermak and Gulenko.

**Yermak:** However, in his striving to refute the systemic-subjective conception of socionics, V. V. Gulenko made serious errors in his article. And this may reduce the effect of the discussion and disorient readers. It will therefore be necessary to analyze what is written in [1].

**Commentary:** It appears that here Yermak considers his own conception of socionics to be systemic-subjective, with which one can agree only in part.

**Gulenko:** "…Not with types, but with universals — …communicative functions… — the term 'aspect' does not suit me…"

**Commentary:** Here Gulenko poses an exceptionally important question about the beginning of the new scientific direction — socionics. And he answers it quite originally: his axiom is the primary existence of communicative functions. He begins directly with them. Here one can sense Gulenko's subjectivist convictions, which will inevitably lead to the appearance of systematic errors in his theoretical constructions.

**Yermak:** Meanwhile, from Aushra Augustinavichiute onward it has been held that socionics begins with the structure of the psyche, reflecting the interaction of its elements — the psychic functions — with the surrounding world (including the psyches of other people) in the process of solving the individual, social, and other tasks set before a person; the subjective structuring, by aspects, of the information flow of this interaction generates types of psyche, whose number and diversity are connected with the combinatorial character of the pairing "psychic function — information aspect." The types of psyche are represented by models of the types of information metabolism (TIM) of the psyche. Can it really be that this approach, laid down by the creator of socionics A. Augustinavichiute and today generally accepted in socionics, is being revised by V. V. Gulenko? Or is a new science being created, attempting to study the psyche from some other angle? Then what does socionics have to do with it?

**Commentary:** Here Yermak quite worthily disavows Gulenko's views. And in form I agree with him, although, as it turns out, Yermak and I understand the basic concepts differently. Also interesting is Yermak's remark that Gulenko is trying to create some other science; but after many years of observing Gulenko's scientific activity I am forced to conclude that this is precisely how he understands socionics — he cannot do otherwise, it doesn't come out any other way for him: his intellect has been captured by the intellectual viruses of relativism and subjectivism.

**Yermak:** Comparing the "elementarist" doctrine of consciousness of the second half of the nineteenth century with contemporary ideas about the structure and elements of the psyche is quite incorrect. One of the founders of psychology, W. Wundt, and the "elementarist understanding of communication processes" can have no "historical identity" [1] whatsoever, since the problem of the elements of communication arose half a century later (more than that, even!) and in a completely different field [3]. And as for rescuing the "…socionic structuralism suffering from staticness (?)" [1] by supplementing the principle of reflection with a "principle of projection" — that means not even suspecting the existence of direct and feedback links in any systemic interaction.

**Commentary:** A very precise exposure of Gulenko's superficial yet self-assured attitude toward his own knowledge in the field of psychology. Gulenko is an intelligent and inventive scholar, but he is, unfortunately, very undemanding toward the products of his scientific creativity. Alongside results in socionics worthy of a scholar — for which one may take one's hat off to him — he has implanted into the broad socionic masses several information viruses, which is tantamount to a serious ideological diversion.

**Yermak:** "Judging from these positions," one is no longer surprised by the strange definition:

**Gulenko:** "…a function is not so much a tool for processing the portions of information arriving at it as an intentional generator of meaning (?!), ordering the external world according to its specificity (?)" [2] _((the marks in parentheses are Yermak's))_

**Commentary:** This is one example of how Gulenko understands a most important psychic category. And here, in Gulenko's spirit — or, what amounts to the same thing, in the spirit of liberal anarchism — one could say that everyone has the right to understand everything as he sees fit. The quoted statement resembles a definition in meaning, but it violates the logical requirements for this procedure that are well spelled out in the methodology of science. In fact there are not one but two judgments here — one "concessive-negative" and the other "concessive-positive" — and both of these forms are unacceptable from a logical point of view, since they are hypothetical in character. The first part only partially denies the understanding of the SF [socionic function] as a tool for processing information — simply downgrading the significance of this process in the structure of the psyche. The author says, as it were, that although the function is indeed a tool for processing information, this is a secondary process. The main process — that is, the leading content of the function — is "intentional generation of meaning." The concept of intentionality, used in its time by Brentano and subsequently adapted by Husserl in phenomenology, means "directedness toward an object." The use of this term points to a connection with Husserl's phenomenological philosophy, long since disavowed by the development of philosophical thought in the time that followed. The introduction of the concept of intentionality constitutes an indirect, hidden acknowledgment of the existence of the object as such, combined with a principled denial of the possibility of knowing its true nature — a line going back to Kant and represented in more disguised forms in neo-Kantianism. Given the complex dependence of Gulenko's views on phenomenology, neo-Kantianism, and structuralist views, one may speak of cognitive distortions in his position, which is not at all easy to discern and account for by a person insufficiently trained in the methodology of science. From the quotation one may conclude that Gulenko inclines more toward a subjectivist understanding of the function as a generator of meanings, rather than as a psychic organ providing the subject's direct contact with objective reality. The analysis also shows that a scholar's development of natural-science subject matter is strongly influenced by his worldview presuppositions, and without analyzing these it is impossible to assess adequately the concepts he introduces.

**Gulenko:** "By what right should the methodology of the natural sciences be transferred to the humanities, which have their own specificity?" [1]

**Yermak:** …the question is posed in the spirit of the Marxist philosophers. Well, if only by this right: that over several centuries of dominance in the humanitarian worldview of the religious and Marxist dogma "man is the highest achievement of Nature, special in the structure of his psyche, in his position and place in the Universe, whole and indivisible…," the humanists have managed to do essentially nothing not only toward solving, but even toward plausibly explaining, the human problems that had become so acute by the end of the twentieth century.

**Commentary:** I do not agree with such a categorical condemnation of the "Marxist dogma." The dialectical ideas introduced into scientific circulation by Marx played a significant role in Soviet natural science, although under the conditions of the Stalinist dictatorship they were ultimately turned into their own dogmatic opposite. However, the healthy dialectical core must be returned to scientific methodology. Model A itself is permeated with dialectical polarities, and this fact deserves special investigation. Much attention was devoted to this question by the remarkable Ukrainian socionist I. Litvinenko [1st volume of this book]. Unfortunately, he did not avoid exaggerations and distortions of dialectical concepts, having begun to look for them where they are not.

**Yermak:** The natural-science approach has ensured and continues to ensure the rapid progress of technology, while on the problems of the informational inclusion of the human being in control systems the humanists can say nothing…

**Commentary:** One can understand the pathos with which Yermak speaks of the natural-science approach, but the grounds on which he discerns it in socionics are not clear. That socionics belongs to the natural rather than the humanitarian (?) sciences — this still needs to be proved. Yermak offers no such proofs, and consequently his reasoning on this subject is unsubstantiated.

**Yermak:** …on systems… Here a "holistic alternative" is proposed to the elementarist "understanding of the system by Yermak" [1]. Alas, no such alternative exists, since an attempt to define a system on the basis of treating the system as a whole (holism) immediately leads to an element (macroelement) of the supersystem and, consequently, to elementarism; this is the "ABC" of systems theory… And the "holistic definition" of a system given in the article evokes nothing but bewilderment…

**Commentary:** The attempt to tie the concept of a system to one pole — holistic or elementarist — is incorrect by definition. Any system unites both these poles. In the philosophical definition, a system is a wholeness uniting a multiplicity of parts. This is what dialectics teaches us.

**Yermak:** Having done much for socionics in 1986–93, in the last three or four years V. V. Gulenko, to my great regret, has been going backward, reviving the long-obsolete descriptive (natural-language) humanitarian approach to the study of the psyche. This discredits the shoots of the natural-science approach in socionics planted by Aushra Augustinavichiute, and disorients beginners with the illusion of the simplicity and ethicality of the philosophical-humanitarian approach to the study of the human being.

**Commentary:** I do not quite understand Yermak's reproaches to Gulenko regarding the descriptive approach. Does Gulenko not rely on a model just as Yermak does? It is another matter that both Yermak and Gulenko created their own models, differing substantially from Model A, yet present them as if they were identical to Model A, which misleads the consumers of this information. Certainly a model must somehow be described, and in this sense we will not escape the descriptive component; but there must also exist intelligible algorithms for interpreting the model, allowing descriptions to be unfolded. Neither Yermak nor Gulenko has this — they make this transition at the level of intuitive insights. In fact, this is how the overwhelming majority of socionists now proceed, apparently unaware of my development of the corresponding algorithms [4, chapter 11].

**Yermak:** One would like to hope that V. V. Gulenko will receive these remarks without prejudice and, in correct discussion with colleagues, will continue to develop and perfect socionics.

**Commentary:** Gulenko used as the epigraph of his article a quotation from the NLP authors J. O'Connor and J. Seymour: "Scientific theories are metaphorical stories about this world; they are not the truth, just one of many ways of thinking about the world, exactly as painting is one of many ways of representing a landscape." This can be understood to mean that he (Gulenko) accepts as truth the positivist methodological stance contained in this quotation — the impossibility of theoretical attainment of truth. What matters here is that the conclusions of philosophers can strongly influence the methodological presuppositions of particular scientists, and this must be taken into account when assessing the results of such scientists' work. Clearly, if Gulenko has adopted such a doctrine, then his own constructions too must be treated as having no relation to truth. One is forced to admit that instead of proving the truth of his results, he offers the socionic community a certain fragment of his own stream of consciousness, which is no more than a metaphorical hint at his contacts with the world. Clearly such revelations can be of interest to science only as empirical material for further investigation into the causes of their appearance. I believe that preaching such an attitude to science disorients scientists, turning their work into the production of metaphors unfit — and even dangerous — for practical use. Analysis of Gulenko's scientific output shows that alongside valuable results he has injected into the socionic community a certain quantity of harmful information viruses, which it will not be at all easy to get rid of.

**Gulenko:** (The Ontology of Communicative Functions) Where does socionics begin? Not with types, but with universals — the basic components of communication, which I shall call communicative functions.

**Commentary:** So thinks Gulenko. I think that socionics must begin with axioms acknowledged by the entire socionic community, from which all the basic socionic concepts will then be derived. Clearly, "communicative functions" are Gulenko's axiomatic limit.

**Gulenko:** The term "aspect," habitual for certain socionic schools, does not suit me because it contains an objectal, corpuscular semantics and has nothing of process in it. Moreover, this word belongs exclusively to the visual modality and therefore does not suit people with kinesthetic or auditory perception.

**Commentary:** The term "aspect" was quite correctly used by Aushra Augustinavichiute to concretize parts of the information flow, and no reasons for a negative attitude toward it are apparent, other than purely subjective ones. Of course there is nothing reprehensible in the fact that the semantics of "aspect" includes notions of objecthood and corpuscularity, and it is entirely unclear why it cannot be connected with process. After all, we are talking about aspects of the information _flow_, which is of course a process. Can V.V. really not understand this? If so, one must reflect on Gulenko's real scientific competence. Clearly, with such statements he misleads practicing socionists who are not versed in modern philosophical intricacies and do not always understand that an uncritical choice of worldview scheme is fraught with serious changes in practical and intellectual behavior.

Furthermore, from the quoted fragment it is evident that Gulenko absolutizes the sensory modalities of NLP, turning them into impassable barriers obstructing scientific communication. There is no doubt that all modalities are accessible to all normal people, who are capable of fully using all the words of their native language, albeit with certain individual preferences, and it is absurd to think that the word "aspect" is inaccessible to certain scholars owing to its visual orientation. But it is also obvious that Gulenko has become so programmed by prejudices from the sphere of NLP that he seriously distorts reality — even though NLP itself is aimed at correcting distortions of reality arising for various reasons, including the relative dominance of some sensory modality in the sensory system.

**Gulenko:** The concept "aspect," first employed by E. Shepetko, is treated in the works of the Vilnius school of socionics, as well as a number of Kyiv schools, as "element."

**Commentary:** There is nothing reprehensible in the fact that the concept "aspect" may be treated by someone as "element" — synonymy does exist, after all, and an information aspect may be understood both as an information element (IE), in which case we would speak of elements of the information flow. What is the problem? Of course words must be used in accordance with the rules of combinability. And if IA points to a part of the information flow, then IE presupposes the non-decomposability of that part within the chosen level of generalization. If these shades of meaning are taken into account by scholars, then what can they be reproached with? But it seems Gulenko sees some defect in the usage he has indicated. Which one? What is he trying to talk the socionic community out of?

**Gulenko:** I want to recall that the dichotomy element/function or, what is the same, aspect/function has a long history. The elementarist understanding of the communication process is historically identical to the structuralism program of W. Wundt, who sought the building material of the psyche and its structure.

**Commentary:** Of course, the pairings "element/function" and "aspect/function" are not identical for socionists, and it is unclear why V.V. tries to identify them. SFs process IAs. This is generally accepted, and it suffices. What elements is V.V. talking about? Why introduce needless confusion? That Wundt in his time sought the structure of the psyche and the elements of that structure deserves a positive assessment, but he spoke precisely of elements of the _psyche_, not of aspects of the information flow. Wundt did the part of the work accessible to him, and we can only be grateful to him. Socionics completed his work, having found the structure of the psyche and the elements of which it consists.

**Gulenko:** The first to come out against Wundt's program was the Austrian psychologist F. Brentano, founder of the doctrine of psychic functions. The intentional act (the psyche's directedness toward an object), according to Brentano, works not with elements-aspects but with functions-processes. Later the name functionalism became attached to this anti-structuralist current. The head of the US functionalists, W. James, asserted that the psyche is not a "chain of elements" but an undivided "stream of consciousness."

**Commentary:** Brentano adhered to the concepts of neo-Kantianism, and accordingly sought a description of the psyche that would bear no traces of the "thing-in-itself" — that is, of notions of the object as existing independently of its perception by the subject. And it apparently seemed to him that by shifting the emphasis from the structure of the psyche to its functioning he had found such a solution, since functioning is directly experienced by the subject and is easy to assess as intentional — that is, inherent in the subject and at the same time directed at the object. It is not always easy for a scholar of materialist orientation to understand what all these contrivances are for, but the neo-Kantian orientation demanded it, imposing methodological restrictions on the scholar's work. At present, inadequate worldview presuppositions belong to the category of cognitive distortions, which explains why scholars obtain different results when trying to understand one and the same phenomenon. The example of W. James is a vivid confirmation of this. A different worldview position makes it possible to understand the stream of consciousness as structured, incorporating elementary objects — though these objects may be variable, which of course complicates the picture but by no means makes it unknowable. The discovery of socionics showed that the presence of a functional structure in no way contradicts the existence of an elemental base. Here we once again invoke the dialectical requirement to take account of both poles of a phenomenon. Structuralism and functionalism should not be torn apart — they complement each other, and used jointly they yield a fully adequate result.

**Gulenko:** I consider that the principle of reflection habitual for structuralists (the movement of information from object to subject) should be supplemented by the principle of projection (the movement of information from subject to object). Then socionic structuralism, suffering from staticness, will sooner or later acquire a more active form.

**Commentary:** First V.V. rigidly tied socionics to structuralism, and then he begins to fight against this — whereas no real structuralists ever invented socionics; and it turns out that this quite decent term is turned into a label that is pinned on socionics and turns it into what it is not — a limited dogma denying processes and dynamics.

**Gulenko:** Judging from these positions, a function is not so much a tool for processing the portions of information arriving at it as an intentional generator of meaning, ordering the external world according to its specificity.

**Commentary:** Precisely — "judging from these positions." That is how the neo-Kantian Brentano could assess a function; but a materialistically oriented dialectician has no need to absolutize such an approach and to deny the understanding of the socionic function as the process and result of information processing. As for the generation of meaning, this question must be investigated more thoroughly rather than dispatched with a reference to the original but far-from-contemporary thinker Brentano. Moreover, to say of a function that it orders the world is to exaggerate its conceptual scope. A function may order our _representations_ of the world, but not the world itself in its infinite diversity. Gulenko assesses the concept of the function from the positions of subjective idealism, though perhaps without being aware of it.

**Gulenko:** Naturally, every author is entitled to introduce the basic components in his own way.

**Commentary:** V.V. does not understand (has he not matured to it, or is it on principle?) that scientific methodology presupposes unified foundations of a scientific discipline — and not at all as a recommendation, but as a normative requirement flowing from deep penetration into and understanding of the deep structures of the world. The multiplication table can serve as an example here: one may relate to it in different ways, but its essence does not change because of that. As for socionics, the authors of its technological adaptations, Gulenko included, did just that — they changed the understanding of the basic components "to suit themselves," that is, as best they could understand them. But for socionics to become a science, it is necessary to achieve not merely a unified understanding of the basic components — that could be done by "an order issued to socionics" — but an understanding that corresponds to the methodology of science.

**Gulenko:** In this article I will dwell on four ways of interpreting the communicative functions — natural-scientific, psychological, everyday, and philosophical. They will represent four aspects of a point of view on how socionic functions can be introduced into use. In other words, our task is to grasp their ontology.

**Commentary:** It is strange that the experienced socionist Gulenko calls all SFs communicative. In Model A only one function is communicative (the CPF — communicative-probing function = the 2nd according to Gulenko), which carries out communicative contact with the environment; all the other functions have different semantics and perform different, no less important work. And it is of course strange to call attempts at various justifications of the SFs "ways of interpretation." From an editorial point of view, a substitution of some concepts for others takes place here.

**Gulenko:** The natural-scientific aspect requires introducing the functions through physical correlates — matter, energy, space, and time, which is what most of Aushra's followers do. As a result, the spirit of positivism has taken firm root in contemporary socionics. The "father" of this epistemological current, A. Comte (1798–1857), dreamed of rebuilding the humanities on the model of physics. Fortunately, he did not succeed.

**Commentary:** Of course, the natural-scientific aspect in this case requires nothing. It presupposes that the psychic functions could be explained with reliance on physical entities. But this is only a hypothesis, and it still needs to be argued. As for the set of these entities proposed by Medvedev, although it seems sufficient, closer analysis reveals a defect contained in it [4: chapter 2], and consequently it should not be used for deriving the IAs from it, since in that case the error, in one form or another, will remain. As for the "spirit of positivism," this is a general defect of the contemporary humanities, connected with a truncated understanding of the methodology of science, and the reference to Comte merely shifts the emphasis. An attempt to justify or derive the IAs in one way or another in no way contradicts scientific methodology — the only question is how correct such attempts are. I think Comte would have been quite pleased — perhaps even surprised and pleasantly delighted — had he learned of the possibility of a deductive construction of a new humanitarian science, socionics; and such work has already been done.

**Gulenko:** The positivist attack on socionics, though strong, has plans and results that are quite vulnerable to criticism. By what right should the methodology of the natural sciences be transferred to the humanities, which have their own specificity? Reductionism in socionics cannot be justified: it stems from narrowness of thinking, whereas a socionically literate person must possess a broad outlook.

**Commentary:** V.V. confuses concepts. He does not, in fact, disclose the meaning of positivism in socionics, and what he calls the positivist attack has an entirely different nature. Positivism in psychology is realized in the physicalist reduction of psychic and psychological phenomena to physiological concepts, such as GSR (galvanic skin response), heart rate, or the electrical conductivity of nerve pathways, and their derivatives. In socionics such attempts also exist — for example, the Voll method for determining TIM — but these are isolated cases outside the socionic mainstream. Yet it is Gulenko himself who demonstrates positivist presuppositions, though apparently without being aware of it.

**Gulenko:** The nomothetic (model-based) and idiographic methods of investigation, whose equal standing and specialization were long ago substantiated in the Baden school of neo-Kantianism (Windelband, Rickert), should not be opposed to each other. They are better combined.

**Commentary:** I agree with Gulenko that methods of investigation in the humanities, even ones developed more than a hundred years ago, must be respected; but that does not mean they are not subject to improvement and modification. Socionics presupposes symbolic modeling of the structure of the psyche, and there was nothing of the kind in the Baden school; moreover, neo-Kantian concepts may differ significantly from socionic concepts in their worldview semantics. Thus, the illegitimate identification of concepts from different epochs and worldviews is a typical example of the disease of inexperienced scholars, and the essence of this disease lies in the hasty identification of theoretical units from different subject domains and worldview schemes.

**Gulenko:** Why does socionics, which studies the quantum nature of the psyche, not consider the active role of the observer of the communicative act? For it is precisely he who introduces the aspects into the information flow. Where is the special anthropological function? In this article it is important for me, as a relativist, to occupy a meta-position, which opens up when you turn into an observer of observers.

**Commentary:** Here Gulenko openly designates relativism as the basis of his worldview, which means that all who understand the limitedness and the fundamental defect of relativism must make corrections for the inevitable systematic error in Gulenko's constructions and reasoning. It is also curious that V.V. evidently considers the IAs a product of the subject, cutting off the objective differentiation of the information flow, which exists prior to the subject's contact with that flow. Incidentally, an analogous story took place with the concepts of light and color. For example, in the Christian religion white light was considered a primary indecomposable essence, and colors were regarded as an external coloring of white, in the way that white cloth is dyed. Only after Newton's instrumental decomposition of white light into the color spectrum was the reality of the independent existence of colored varieties of light acknowledged. So, just as the spectrum of colors exists objectively, so too does the spectrum of IAs exist objectively. And no bugbear of positivism, especially a wrongly understood one, works here.

Also interesting in this passage is how Gulenko constructs his thought: what is the connection between the quantum nature of the psyche and the active role of the observer? Seryozha writes that "Criticism in socionics is as absent as a ghost in a barracks" — but in reality nobody ever forbade it: if you want to criticize, go ahead, only preferably in a qualified manner. And then Seryozha, starting from the definition of socionics given by Yermak — "Socionics is the science of the structure and mechanism of the psyche" — makes a discovery for himself. He writes: "That is, there exists a psyche. What is it? Unknown." On the one hand, it is hard even to believe this — after all, we are all bearers of a psyche and must surely have at least some idea of it, all the more so a cultured and highly educated historian like Savchenko. The simplest definition of the psyche is to understand it as a system of subjective reactions. And how do _you_ define the psyche?

On the other hand, official science does not know what the psyche is. To picture the attitude to this problem in Soviet and post-Soviet psychology, it suffices to read the relevant quotation from the dictionary "Psychology" [8]: "The understanding of the psyche (P.) as reflection makes it possible to overcome the false posing of the problem of the relation between the psychological and the physiological, which leads either to the divorce of the P. from the work of the brain, or to the reduction of psychic phenomena to physiological ones, or, finally, to the simple statement of the parallelism of their course. The disclosure of psychic reflection as engendered in the process of the material subject's interaction with objective reality excludes the view of psychic phenomena as purely spiritual, isolated from the corporeal cerebral processes by means of which the transition of the reflected reality into psychic reflection takes place. However, the characterization of the subject's activity cannot be directly derived from the physiological processes realizing it, since it is determined by the properties and relations of the objective world, to which it is subordinated and to which, correspondingly, the psychic reflection arising in the subject's brain is also subordinated."

From this quotation it is clear that the proposed problem is assigned to the category of pseudo-problems and considered definitively solved — well, perhaps except for some technical details, which can be refined in a working order. Although, on the other hand, everything said in this quotation can be regarded as pure nonsense. Think into it, read into the reasoning presented, and at some moment you will understand this, if you did not understand it from the start. Let us begin with the concept of reflection. How does the author of the quoted text understand reflection? As in a mirror, or what? Perhaps somehow differently? But how? Of this the quoted text says nothing. Apparently, in the times when this dictionary was compiled, it was considered that everyone understood this word as they should. In any case, reflection is a certain action, a certain function — and there is no function without an organ. So within the framework of what organ does this reflection take place? That organ is the psyche. And the reduction of the psyche to a totality of physiological processes is nothing more than the most vulgar reductionism. Vulgar reductionism rests on reducing a phenomenon to the nearest factors by which it can be somehow explained. But scientific explanation must go further and find factors adequate to the phenomenon under investigation. So I must apologize to Seryozha for doubting his doubts, for the problem of the psyche in fact remains unsolved in official science, although the chief psychologists are certain that _they_ at least know what the psyche is.

**Commentary:** The differences between the functions enumerated in the previous commentary are objective and can be assessed by an expert.

**Savchenko:** And what if the functions were not numbered?

**Commentary:** See the previous commentaries — the functions have no numbers as it is.

**Savchenko:** Speaking of the first four functions, we speak of the zones of maximal competence and confidence — the dominant function; of the search for similarity and difference — the second and third functions; of the zone of maximal uncertainty — the repressed function.

**Commentary:** All the functions, not only the first four, have quite definite semantics, and there is no point in confining oneself to the first four.

**Savchenko:** This is not socionics! Out of the hall! At him! To the stake!

**Commentary:** Seryozha, one must defend one's point of view professionally. And if you run into boorishness, intolerance, dogmatic obtuseness, that means there can be no talk of science here. See the first three rules of scientific communication:

1. If an error has been found in a scholar's reasoning, he must correct it — up to and including the elimination of the construct. If a scholar refuses to correct errors, he is, by definition, not a scholar, and his quasi-scientific activity need not be reckoned with.
2. Argumentation in science must have a through character — from any point down to the very foundation.
3. If you are conducting a discussion with a scholar, you must know his works and refer to them correctly.

**Résumé (Savchenko):** The question of the meaning and content of the aspects has not been solved and is a delayed-action mine for socionics.

**Commentary:** The question of the content of the aspects has been solved and awaits scientific discussion.

**(Savchenko):** Either Jung served only as a catalyst — and catalysts do not enter into the composition of the substances obtained, in which case socionics is connected neither with Jung nor with the dubious offspring of "Psychological Types," type-watching — or socionics is somehow based on Jung. Then the question: how?

**Commentary:** Aushra would not have discovered socionics without Jung's discoveries. Jung could not have made his discoveries had Freud not been before him. Freud could not have carried out his revolution in psychology had he not relied on the enormous psychological experience accumulated by humanity before him. Between Freud and Aushra lies a large number of ego-psychologists, each of whom made his own unique contribution to the understanding of personality. The future, scientific socionics must become the completion of this grandiose work. So: the aims are clear, the tasks defined — to work, comrades!

**(Savchenko):** Disregard of elementary logic in the development of socionic theories does not allow socionics to become what it could be. More than that: today's socionics is in general little capable of being anything decent.

**Commentary:** The leading socionist-maîtres, in creating their technological variants of the use of the classical core created by Aushra, commit not only logical errors. In socionic improvisations there are present, in large quantity, methodological and cognitive errors. Of the existence of the latter, many maîtres simply have no suspicion.

**(Savchenko):** The absence of a socionic organization in itself substantially hinders the development of socionics as a science, to say nothing of its social status.

**Commentary:** It has been proved in science studies that key discoveries are made by talented lone scholars, while the most productive units in scientific terms are small groups — 7–8 people with a precise division of labor. Large organizations are good only for solving large state programs with large financing, but even there intrigues, undermining, and the whole set of defective human relations that reduce the productivity of scientific labor are possible. Yet even without any organization we see how ineffective the interaction of scholars can be when ambitions and earnings are at stake.

And to this résumé Yermak let loose the following tirade: "The 'résumé' of S. V. Savchenko's opus, even against the background of his entire 'critical analysis,' looks simply indecent. What 'resists criticism' is not so much the content of the résumé's points (the content is simply absent!) as the categoricalness and peremptoriness of the declarations. And thank God that at such a level of knowledge there is as yet an absence of a 'socionic organization' à la S. V. Savchenko — otherwise all socionists would surely be marching in formation, professing one single 'correct truth'!"

**Commentary:** Comments are superfluous.
