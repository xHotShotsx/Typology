# SECTION 5

# THE SEMANTICS OF INFORMATIONAL ASPECTS — WHAT, EXACTLY, DO THE AUTHORS HAVE IN MIND?

_(an example of a complex and conscientiously argued error)_

**Commentary on the work of L.A. Kochubeeva, V.V. Mironov, and M.L. Stoyalova**

_[Translator's note: this is Section 5 of S.I. Churyumov's commentary. Abbreviations kept as in the original: IA = informational aspect; ARP = Augustinavichiute–Reinin trait; TIM = type of information metabolism; SF = socionic function; PF = psychic function; IP = information flow; DP = differentiating trait; PR = Reinin trait; IMEE (Rus. ВМВС) = internal model of the external environment; UChK (УЧК) = Churyumov's book "The Smile of the Cheshire Cat".]_

The St. Petersburg Working Group has presented the results of its three years of research in the book _Socionics: THE SEMANTICS OF INFORMATIONAL ASPECTS_. The authors have carried out a large and interesting piece of experimental work. However, their theoretical premises, from the standpoint of modern scientific methodology, contain a number of inaccuracies that cast doubt on part of the results they obtained. The analysis of this work is especially instructive because the authors understand methodology and quite consciously formulate their positions. Their combined methodological complex naturally divides into experimental methods and general scientific methodology. I personally believe that with their experimental methods everything, or almost everything, is in order — which is easily explained by their belonging to the St. Petersburg scientific school. As for general methodology, unfortunately, most domain scientists who directly produce scientific results do not always have the opportunity to rely on the achievements of the leading edge of methodology. The cause of this is a certain inertia of the scientific mentality. Scientists in the main tend to hold to what is tested, established, and officially approved. This can explain the still-remaining vestiges of positivism in the scientific milieu, although advanced philosophical thought long ago overcame its one-sidedness. It is also plausible that various forms of positivism, entrenched in the West as the methodological basis of scientific research, are quite deliberately being implanted into the consciousness of the bearers of post-Soviet science. The scientist is immersed in a disciplinary paradigm, and any paradigmatic novelties are met by him with distrust. But, as we now understand it, at the base of socionics lies a paradigm that differs significantly from the one that has established itself in science over the last 100 years. This is one of the reasons why official psychology has taken a negative position toward socionics. The main components of the socionic paradigm are the notions of the essential role of information in metabolic processes at any scale, and the concept of a unified field, without appeal to which any scientific research and its results lose their primary foundation, remaining scattered empirical findings of a recipe-book kind.

As for the overall characterization of the work under analysis, it deserves any positive dissertation-level assessment, despite the significant number of methodological deficiencies it contains. Moreover, it is the authors' personal affair whether they find it possible and necessary to respond in some way to my analysis. What matters to me here is only that future generations of socionists have the opportunity to compare different points of view on one and the same theoretical phenomenon.

I will also note that the authors — owing to their psychological literacy — are a pleasure to read, unlike certain "pure" socionists who are almost unacquainted with the fundamental psychological literature. Nevertheless, I diverge from the authors in the understanding of what an informational aspect is — a construct that occupies a substantial part of their study.

**Authors:** _For three years the Working Group (WG) on socionics (formerly the working group on socionics at the laboratory of interdisciplinary research of the Institute of Biology and Psychology of Man, St. Petersburg) was engaged in research on informational aspects, analyzing and describing the results of a large and painstaking effort._

**Commentary:** Everything here is impressive: the duration, the group character of the work, the laboratory, the institute, the subject matter, and the painstaking attitude toward the work.

**Authors:** _Work on the project went hard. Only enthusiasm and an unquenchable research interest allowed us to put the final full stop in this book (though not in our work)._

**Commentary:** After more than a quarter century of work with socionic subject matter, I have several times had to state at conferences that socionics is both complex and difficult to understand, despite its obvious popularity and seeming transparency. Alas, it is transparent only for beginners, who chirp with ease in its blossoming crown, "without the slightest doubt" re-discovering what has already been discovered by others whom they have not read, and proposing to completely remake it in accordance with their treetop-level notions. Whoever has reached the deep levels of socionics feels the resistance of its far-from-simple material.

**Authors:** _We must give due credit to our predecessors, whose works describing the speech peculiarities of the socionic types helped us considerably in our research._

**Commentary:** This is right — to give predecessors their due and to cite them properly.

**Authors:** _At the same time, our approaches and the approaches of our colleagues differ substantially in the criteria on the basis of which the dictionaries were compiled._

**Commentary:** This is an important statement. It is not excluded that the authors will reach the paradigmatic level and propose their own variant of understanding socionics.

**Authors:** _The dictionaries of aspect lexicon previously encountered in the socionic literature were based on such methods as direct observation (collection of empirical material) and certain theoretical premises (ideas about how exactly representatives of particular types ought to express themselves). This state of affairs corresponds to the primary stage of development of any new science or scientific paradigm. At the same time, we believe that this stage is ending, and the stage of sorting data and testing previously advanced hypotheses and assumptions is beginning. This is one of the tasks we pursued in our work. The second is connected with the search for adequate methods of experimental research, which, in our opinion, can be worked out only by taking into account the experience of adjacent sciences._

**Commentary:** It appears the authors are insufficiently acquainted with the works of other researchers of socionic theory, who long ago passed the primary stage and proposed a fundamental grounding of socionics (see the works of Bukalov, Prokofieva, Filimonov, Churyumov).

**Authors:** _Undoubtedly, this study is another step toward the establishment of socionics as a scientific discipline, where one of the criteria of scientificity is the reproducibility of experiments. To this end we have described in detail the methods and results of our work._

**Commentary:** It would be interesting to know whether the authors' result is reproducible if other focus groups are used.

**Authors:** _The focus group (resonance group) for an aspect itself checked the lexicon; if a word or phrase caused difficulty or dispute in the group, such lexicon was not taken into account in further work._

**Commentary:** The focus group is perhaps the main peculiarity of the authors' method in the attempt to sort out the concept of the IA. However, let us note at once that one such group, or a small number of them, is entirely insufficient for obtaining reliable results. After all, people are used here as empirical bearers of the language, and their production will, of necessity, have merely the character of opinion, in no way binding for others. Here, as always in humanities empirics, statistics and the requirements imposed on a statistical experiment must be of essential significance. But the main thing here is that the "resonance" methodology is merely a beautiful idea, which can be used at the level of a hypothesis but which cannot be considered a reliable source of truth; it would be reasonable first to ground and develop it experimentally and theoretically. Without this, accepting the results obtained with its help as absolute would be premature.

**Authors:** _Inviting colleagues to a discussion and proposing that those who wish repeat our experiment, we nevertheless note the necessity of observing safety procedures as a guarantee of the psychological health of the participants of the resonance groups._

**Commentary:** In any case, I am ready to take part in the discussion, and I accept the authors' challenge.

**Authors:** _For the first time in the history of socionics we give definitions of such generally accepted and commonly used terms as informational aspect and Reinin trait, and we also define a number of the Reinin traits used in the work._

**Commentary:** I do not even know how to take such a claim. As if that very history had never existed, or as if it consisted solely of the authors' impressions of socionics as it happened to come to hand. As if it were not Aushra who discovered the IAs, and as if her works did not contain implicit definitions of these entities and attempts at their deductive construction. As if the works on the grounding of the IAs carried out by Medvedev, Ermak, Shepetko, Ritchik, Filimonov, Sedykh, Gut, Prokofieva, and finally Kalinauskas had passed the authors by. And, of course, my own [3]. These should at least have been analyzed in a literature review. And yet a few paragraphs earlier the authors spoke of their readiness to give predecessors their due.

As for the ARP (Augustinavichiute–Reinin traits), the initial phase of their development by both Reinin and Aushra unquestionably deserves the closest attention, to say nothing of respect for authorship. Perhaps the authors have made some stunning discovery refuting all their predecessors — but then this still has to be proved. It is also not excluded that the authors have discovered something quite different but do not yet know it, though they spent three years of creative time on it. Columbus, too, thought he had merely discovered a route to India…

**Authors:** _The semantics of aspects in TIM diagnostics…_

**Commentary:** And by the way, do the authors even know who first formulated the problem of the semantics of the IA, the SF, and Model A — or do they consider that inessential?

**Authors:** _A fundamentally important point in diagnostics by speech is the principle voiced by G. Reinin: "not in awareness — not in language."_

**Commentary:** Evidently the authors ignore the psychoanalytic observations going back to Freud, connected with the manifestation of subconscious tendencies in speech and in nonverbal reactions in the form of erroneous actions, slips of the pen, slips of the tongue, and speech forms employing specific lexicon that points to unconscious factors. Did G.R. Reinin really formulate his principle in exactly this way — without taking into account the rich clinical experience of the psychoanalysts? As I understand it, such a principle could sound quite different: "elements whose content is not conscious may be present in human speech."

**Authors:** _…speech reflects the peculiarities of the psyche, including typical characteristics._

**Commentary:** But also subconscious tendencies, which in no way contradicts the manifestation of typological markers — for these, too, are not fully controlled by consciousness and, in people untrained in typology, are for the most part not only not conscious but possibly absent from the subconscious as well, which is of course hard to verify owing to the absence of reliable methods for working with the subconscious.

**Authors:** _The study of aspects in the broad sense of the word is an area where the socionist's foot has practically "never trodden," and over which much work still lies ahead._

**Commentary:** As an editor, I note that the phrase "in the broad sense of the word" should be set off with commas — otherwise it has quite a different meaning from the one the authors presumably intended. But of course the authors are right when they say that the topic of aspects needs work; however, references to predecessors — at least to Medvedev, Shepetko, Ritchik, Filimonov, and Ermak — must be made. And as for the "socionist's foot" — an obvious exaggeration. So I find myself wondering, after at least two "dashing" claims by the authors, whether exaggeration is the basis of their intellectual stylistics.

**Authors:** _In many cases the content of the aspects is derived from the model and verified by nothing: it is presumed that this is how it must be._

**Commentary:** Here we are dealing with a subtle point concerning the relation between theory and practice. Aspects, unquestionably, must be theoretically grounded — that is, after all, where they came from. In order to verify something, a scientist must have at least some image of the construct being verified. The construct itself is always the result of some generalization of empirics, though such a generalization may have been made by someone else and be present in the research field at the level of a hypothesis, a prejudice, or something taken for granted. Moreover, I have proposed (see UChK) a scheme for the deductive derivation of the aspects, which secures their theoretical status. But, naturally, their concretization must be provided by experimental work, which in turn must correspond to the theoretical notions. It is precisely such theoretical-empirical iterations that must, in the end, ensure the full validity of this construct — and not only this one. The authors' claim is simply unsubstantiated — yet another exaggeration. Apparently the authors do not understand that empirics is not reducible to experiment — there is also clinical and everyday experience, which may be quite sufficient for introducing primary concepts. Aushra Augustinavichiute introduced the concept of the IA as the basic partition of the information flow (IP); but, of course, someone might wish to replace her construct with his own, in which IAs are understood differently — for example, as arbitrary taxonomic subdivisions of information — but for that they must first be introduced. And not simply introduced: their full spectrum must be obtained.

What, then, could such presumed taxonomic subdivisions of information be? How, in general, can information be divided into units that are maximally general and yet more concrete than the very concept of information? For that, first of all, one must know what information is. Not at the level of intuitive insight — at that level everyone knows what information is — but at the level of a logical concept. Without this, any work in this direction will be a wandering in intuitive darkness. I fear that precisely this is happening with the authors.

We know that a large number of informational units exist, but in socionics we are talking about a high level of generalization. To such a level belong the informational types or, what is the same thing, the types of information metabolism. But the set of informational types is not limited to the number 16. It is arranged on levels determined by powers of two. Aushra chose the level 2³ = 8 (the third level), which corresponds to the number of functions in Model A and by whose symbols these functions are denoted. This last remark is important, since the choice of the third level for fixing the set of IAs corresponds to the requirement of necessity and sufficiency for the constructive support of the socionic model of the psyche. Possibly, for other purposes, other levels might be needed, but in each such case a justification and the introduction of corresponding definitions are required. An understanding of this is absent from the authors' work.

**Authors:** _Meanwhile every author writing a book on socionics gives his own description of the aspects, but practically none of them refers to experimental data._

**Commentary:** Here we have a situation entirely characteristic of the initial stages of the "wild" formation of a scientific direction. One should not lament this — one should make use of it. Scientists who write books express in them both their theoretical notions and their own, often weakly grounded and almost unreflected, empirical findings. The subsequent conscientious criticism of these is a form of diachronic dialogue and a pledge of the further development of the science. However, from the methodological point of view, one ought to sort out the taxonomy of experimental data. After all, this also includes the scientist's empirical experience of everyday language use. We are all bearers of the language and know a thing or two about it, and for primary formulations that may suffice. How else could Aushra have formulated the idea of the IA, fundamentally new for science? Here her brilliant intuition did the work.

**Authors:** _The task of the present work is to order and supplement the existing knowledge on the semantics of informational aspects, testing it against factual material._

**Commentary:** …you see — to order and supplement; so there is, after all, something to order and supplement, and yet so much was said… The stated intention is a good one; however, as the authors' further text shows, it destroys the theoretical supports already proposed by their predecessors. This does not mean that the achievements of predecessors must be accepted as dogma, but it does mean that one must reckon with them and respond to them, at least in the form of conscientious analysis and criticism, rather than by a voluntarist replacement with one's own theoretical impressions.

**Authors:** _The personality, as a totality of innate and acquired traits, manifests itself in activity and through activity, in the ways it organizes the environment and contacts the environment._

**Commentary:** A very correct remark — however, one resting on pre-socionic notions of "activity," which was an ideological bogeyman of Soviet philosophy and psychology. No, activity is not at all a bad category, provided it is not tied to the Soviet ideology that turned it into one of the arguments for the enslavement of citizens by servile labor. In socionics the concept of "activity" corresponds to the IA of extraverted thinking, but here this concept is given a fundamental status not through ideological manipulations but thanks to a precisely found axiomatic base corresponding to the scientific notions of the unity of the world and the possibility of adequately reflecting its fundamental invariants in consciousness.

**Authors:** _Speech is a kind of activity. No one disputes that it reflects the peculiarities of the culture (and subculture) to which a person belongs, national, gender, and age peculiarities (the speech of a child and the speech of an adult differ — an obvious fact), psychic norm and pathology, etc._

**Commentary:** Here we have a subtle substitution of the concept of "speech," connected with pre-socionic notions of it. In socionics, speech is linked with the IA of introverted thinking, its primary objectivation, where its products — algorithms of possible activity — undergo a primary check at the level of the orienting reflex, whereas activity is a category of extraverted thinking, that is, of the real (extraverted), and not ideal (introverted), purposeful transformation of reality. Here we are dealing with the opposition of word and deed: words can contain only the idea of a purposeful transformation, while deeds — that is, activity — are the transformation itself. Failure to distinguish these is a standard methodological error. Clearly, a distortion of the theoretical notions of speech is fraught with both overt and finely camouflaged errors in further theoretical and experimental work.

**Authors:** _"It is precisely the linguistic personality (i.e., the human being in his capacity to perform speech actions) that has become the integral object of numerous spheres of the science of language, which have formed the various fields of anthropocentric linguistics."_

**Commentary:** Here, in the concept of the "linguistic personality," one can observe the initial stage of the dismemberment of the integral concept of "personality" into fragments convenient for study in the subject area of psycholinguistics. As a working device this is normal; however, one must not forget that after such fragmentary study the integrity of the concept must be restored. But the authors evidently do not understand that "speech actions," although they can become the basis of activity — that is, of a real purposeful transformation of the environment — do not by themselves transform the environment and therefore are not activity.

**Authors:** _It would seem that in socionics too it is legitimate to apply the methods of psycholinguistics to increase the accuracy of diagnostics and the understanding of the worldview of each TIM._

**Commentary:** One can fully agree with this, adding the requirement of introducing socionic corrections into psycholinguistics — which will in no way destroy its disciplinary purity but will connect an already established science with new and highly effective socionic ideas, especially in their methodological aspect. Without taking into account the socionic contribution to the understanding of the human psyche as a structure of information metabolism, psycholinguistics may simply turn out to be inapplicable to socionics.

**Authors:** _Traditionally in socionics it is considered that each informational aspect processes its own part of the information flow entering the psyche._

**Commentary:** That is what the authors think about the socionic tradition — while using the words incorrectly. IAs do not process anything — functions process. But the authors have a "new vision" of the IA and the ARP — a non-traditional one. They said what corresponds to their understanding of Model A. And this means that, strange as it is for scientists of such caliber, they have not understood Jung's main discovery — the positioning of the functions in the psyche. But, as I have understood, most socionists do not understand this, and one may say that it is traditional in socionics. Moreover, Jung himself did not fully reflect on this side of his typology. The positional semantics of the functions — answering the question "what place does a function occupy in the chain of intrapsychic information metabolism and what does it do?" — was extremely approximate in Jung: a leading function, a subordinate one, an auxiliary one. He worked mainly with aspect semantics, which answers the question "what information does the function process?" And herein lies Jung's primary contribution to the semantics of the IA, though he never knew it. In this contribution of his, the theoretical and empirical sides of research are present in syncretic form. Of course, his rich clinical experience can in no way be ignored as the empirical foundation of his understanding of the meaning of the psychic functions. And, of course, he relied on the theoretical and experimental groundwork of his predecessors, who likewise cannot be discounted. All this creates the prehistory of the semantics of the IA and is subject to consideration and adaptation. So no "traditionality" in the understanding that "each informational aspect processes its own part of the information flow entering the psyche" has yet taken shape.

**Authors:** _The question of whether the division into aspects is internal or external seems to us conceptual. That is, is information initially divided into certain segments, or is its differentiation (in particular, into aspects) a product of the work of certain psychic mechanisms? On this question we hold to the subjective conception, within which it is presumed that in nature there is no aspect division, and that all division is performed by our psyche (brain) to facilitate the perception of information. In modern psychological science it is considered axiomatic that our psyche not only reflects but also transforms reality._

**Commentary:** Here the authors demonstrate a mature methodological position: they understand the fundamental character of the problem of the IA, formulate it precisely, and consciously make their choice in favor of the "subjective conception" — that is, they proceed from the premise that "in nature there is no aspect division, and all division is performed by our psyche (brain) to facilitate the perception of information." There is no point in trying to dissuade people who act consciously. Therefore the following arguments are not for the authors but for the reader.

First of all, in the reasoning about the division of information "into certain segments" there is a subtle logical error connected with the axiom of identity. When the authors speak of the "initial division of information," they thereby, by default, acknowledge the "initial" existence of information. What does "initial" mean? Apparently — before it is perceived by a subject. Let us note that some scientists do not acknowledge the existence of information before it is perceived by a subject. In that case it is appropriate to ask: where, then, does it come from? This misunderstanding points to the underdevelopment of certain theoretical points in information theory. And if the authors acknowledge the existence of information prior to its perception by a subject, they will have to acknowledge its initial division as well. If, however, they take the position that information does not exist without a subject, then they cannot speak of the "initial division of information" without violating the axiom of identity. Therefore, if we speak of an information flow which is apparently directed from outside toward the subject and is perceived by the subject, there are no grounds to suppose that this flow has no divisions; and these divisions, too, will be perceived by the subject — and, of course, there is nothing surprising in the fact that they will be perceived subjectively. But the flow is an informational one — that is, it is the flow that carries the information.

On the other hand, the concept of "wholeness" should not be used as a bogeyman by insisting on the indivisibility of nature. That would amount to an admission that nature is a single undifferentiated mass — which may appeal to certain atavistic components of the scientific consciousness but certainly does not correspond to the conclusions of integral human practice, which deals with an environment that is a relatively organized multitude of heterogeneous objects. The unity of the world is a lofty point of view. But the world is one at its very foundation, while at the level of the physical world it is obviously manifold — let us not forget the lessons of dialectics.

As for whether the IAs are objective entities or subjective impressions, here we are dealing with a misunderstanding. The fact is that Aushra from the very beginning considered the IAs, first, subjective images of certain ontological entities (field — body, etc.) and, second, real components of the IP as well, denoting both hypostases of the IA by the same symbols — though they could have been distinguished by introducing some index; but this is not a matter of principle, even though we do have here a case of terminological synonymy.

The decision partially adopted by Aushra is explained by difficulties of interpretation, but this state of affairs should not be perceived as overly complicated. In the end, behind both of these entities stands one and the same archetype. And in time this could be sorted out in more detail. So let us, at last, sort out what the subject perceives and what the subject adds that was not in the object.

Let us begin with the definition of the ARP. The ARP is, first of all, the fundamental set of differentiating traits that make it possible to distinguish some TIMs from others. This set is characterized by completeness, consistency, necessity, and sufficiency, although the requirement of independence does not hold on it — something that should be specifically stipulated and recognized. All this means that apart from this set there exist no other DPs of the same level of generality. The ARPs — that is, the DPs entering into this fundamental set — are elementary entities, i.e., indecomposable at this level of generalization. However, unlike the ARPs, in personality psychology a large number of DPs of a lower level of generalization have been identified and are studied, which allow one to distinguish, with a high degree of detail at the everyday level, one personality from another and, in numerous typologies, one type from another. In socionics, the DPs by which one TIM is distinguished from another are precisely the Augustinavichiute–Reinin traits (ARP). They are maximally generalized traits, features, properties of character. As applied to socionics this is easy to understand if one considers that a TIM is precisely a generalized character. The three terms — traits, features, and properties — are synonymous here. In socionics this generalization is based on the fact that socionics deals with maximally generalized functions. Character traits, in psychological terms, are parameters which, with a suitable choice of terms, can be identified with elements of behavior. If, for example, we single out the type of brave people, this means, in behavioral terms, that in certain situations they will act in defiance of danger. At a high level of generalization these are frame parameters setting the broad boundaries within which the behavior of the personality is realized. Such parameters in socionics are extraversion and introversion, intuitiveness and sensoricity, rationality and irrationality, the leading or subordinate role of thinking or emotionality, and so on. The bearer of any TIM acts within the limits of each of these poles and never crosses its constraints. Each of these poles can appear in two hypostases: objective and subjective. Can arbitrary DPs — and in particular the ARPs — be redefined as functions? For this one must return to the definition of a function as the process and result of the work of some part of the psyche. Can, for example, extraversion be defined as a function? Is extraversion the process and result of the work of some psychic organ, however generalized or utterly concrete that organ might be? Extraversion is a psychic quality, and as such it is the functional realization of an informational program recorded in one way or another on a psychic carrier. By now there is no longer any doubt that there exist psychic and physiological mechanisms for reading such programs off their carriers and functionally realizing them as a behavioral component. Of course, it would be splendid if experimenters managed to identify these considerations in the laboratory. The understanding of DPs — and in particular of the ARPs — given here is sufficient to allow them, if necessary, to be interpreted as functions. Incidentally, one can add here also the functional interpretation of relations, where the function turns out to be the combination of two functions belonging to different partners. But refinements are needed here.

Objectively, the ARPs are generalized parameters of the object, while subjectively they can be understood either as generalized standards by which the subject recognizes a generalized parameter of the object, or as a generalized psychic function (PF) that extracts from the IP a quite definite part of the information and processes it. This understanding of the PF rests on the definition that a psychic function (PF) is the process and result of the work of a definite structure of the psyche. From this point of view, an ARP is a PF. For distinguishing this information the subject possesses subjective standards which were developed both in the course of the evolution of the animal species and in the process of phylogenesis. The study of the process of formation of such standards in the course of a species' adaptation to its habitat constitutes an important scientific problem. The work of the ARPs, which are PFs of a high level of generalization, is easy to interpret as the comparison of an objective signal with a subjective standard.

In connection with what has been said, the question arises: "what, in reality, corresponds to the subjective standard?" Since we are dealing here with generalized functions, generalized traits of behavior, and generalized subjective standards, we should turn to the problem of the existence of what in logic are called general concepts.

It was laconically and vividly expressed in his time (3rd century BC) by the little-known Greek philosopher Prodicus. He said: "A horse I see, but horseness I do not see." This problem divides philosophers to this day. And it has a direct bearing on the problem of the IA. One may say that the IA is Prodicus's maximally generalized "horseness." It is the "aspectness" of everything there is in the world. A modern Prodicus might say: "Objects I see, but their aspectness I do not see." As we can see, the old problem of the existence of general concepts becomes frankly topical in socionics. And a single understanding of it for all socionists — let alone for all philosophers — does not yet exist. Yet an artist, with one stroke of the pen, drawing a wavy line imitating the contour of a horse from muzzle and mane to tail, thereby invites us to see pure horseness without a horse — and even small children who have ever seen a horse will not confuse it with anything. Thus one can state that horseness is a trait, or a set of traits, by which we recognize a horse. If we acknowledge the real existence of traits as a combination of an object's parameters, then we should have no problem acknowledging that horseness exists really — and, by analogy with it, aspectness really exists as well. Let us not forget, all the same, that people, owing to the contiguity of phenomenon and concept, easily conflate the trait as an objective parameter and the trait as a criterion of distinction behind which stands a subjective standard. The first is objective, the second subjective. Let us turn to concrete examples. When a person sees the color red, this is a parameter of the object perceived by the person in subjective codes. Red in nature corresponds to an electromagnetic wave of a definite length, and thus the object's parameter is coded in natural codes — in this case, in the modulations of electromagnetic radiation. The generalized frames of subjective standards correspond to generalized frames in the modulations of the signal. It is precisely such generalized frames that the IAs are — but the subject will perceive them in his own codes, in the capacity of which the ARPs act. Thus, if a parameter of an object belongs, for example, to the IA of introverted sensorics (s) — meaning that behind it stands a resource necessary to a living being — then in subjective codes it will be perceived as an experience of usefulness, comfort, aesthetic pleasure.

Thus, in socionics this problem of the existence of general concepts is solved thanks to the adoption of the informational paradigm, according to which information, as a modulation of a material-energetic carrier, exists really and not subjectively, and is capable of really influencing the behavior of people and other entities that are non-transparent to information (see the section on truth in the humanities). The names and classes of objects are informational entities, and they are real in the sense stated above.

However, without recourse to the informational paradigm (see the section on truth in the humanities in this book) it is difficult to sort this out. According to the informational paradigm, information exists in two hypostases — objectively, as the set of modulations of all media coexisting in the world, and subjectively, as the perception of these modulations in subjective codes. Correspondingly, two definitions of information must be given: an objective and a subjective one. Objectively, information is the codes of form in the modulations of any material-energetic process, which in this case is understood as a signal process. Subjectively, information is that which changes the state of the subject. Failure to understand the dual nature of information leads to all manner of misunderstandings, above all philosophical ones. Precisely such a misunderstanding is the opposition of materialism and idealism, each of which copes somehow with solving problems within the limits of its own competence.

Any object perturbs the medium, or media, in which it is located. These perturbations exist independently of the subject perceiving them. These perturbations are represented in the medium as its modulations. These modulations, in turn, are codes of the object's parameters — its dimensions, position in space, the material of which it consists, and so forth. It is precisely the totality of all the medium's codes that constitutes the objective hypostasis of information as a "coded reality," or encoded reality. Thus the real world and the real objects in it are present simultaneously in material-energetic and in signal-code form. The subject, in his adaptation to the habitat, acquired specific organs allowing him to transcode the modulations of the medium into subjective images, whose essence, nonetheless, does not in principle differ from the objective coding modulations. The differences here relate only to the media whose modulations code the objects. The psychic medium of the subject, in its modulations produced by the organs of perception, contains the image of the object — but, thanks to its psychic nature, it is also aware of it. The recognition, in accordance with the informational paradigm, of the objective existence of information entails the necessity of recognizing also the objective existence of the classes into which objects are divided. And this means that to the materially represented set of objects there corresponds a class of these objects encoded by the modulations of the medium. The codes carrying information about classes are frame subsets of the sets of codes carrying information about concrete objects. The same can be said of the IAs, since they are maximally generalized classes of objects, processes, phenomena, and any other wholes objectively existing in our world. Refinements: lexical units are not only words but also their meaningful parts — roots, prefixes, suffixes, endings — as well as their combinations, up to and including texts of arbitrary length. A concept is the functional specification of a denotatum. Proceeding from what has been said, the question must be posed: where in Model A is perception located — or what in Model A corresponds to perception? The distorted notions of the semantics of the functions current in socionics mask the sensory input in Model A, calling the corresponding functions the normative (or role) function and the suggestive function, respectively. True, the concept of suggestion can quite well be connected with input influence — but it reflects the negative part of such influence, connected with possible manipulation. As was shown in [3], the ERF is the function connected with the expectation of a resource and the capacity to assess its parameters adequately for correspondence to needs. Simply put, this function evaluates the resource. But if one manipulates the resource, one can turn this function into a suggestive one.

The ANF is likewise an input function, but it works with the load-bearing influence of the environment and generalizes the means of withstanding this influence. Yes, in this function one can detect the semantics of normativity as the necessity of conforming to the demands of the environment — but this part of the semantic field has a secondary character, derivative from the necessity and capacity to withstand external pressure. It is easy to understand that the pressure of the environment forces the organism to adapt to it. And only afterward can the parameters of adaptation act as normative constraints.

Now the question can be posed: how, then, do the other functions perceive information? Here, it seems, it would be more correct to speak not of perception but of reception: all the remaining functions receive information from the preceding one in the chain of information metabolism. And this is not some intuitive-theoretical fantasy, but a conclusion from the deductively grounded notion of the semantics of the functions of Model A.

Let us see how, from the point of view expounded, the functioning of the models of individual TIMs looks.

The ILE (Don Quixote), who has in the ANF the aspect S, responsible for withstanding the pressure of the environment, perceives the world as dangerous; while by the ERF, where he has the aspect s, the world is for him a source of resource. Such a dual perception of the world launches two informational-metabolic chains. The perception of the world as dangerous is passed to the CCF (conservative-critical function), where Don Quixote has the aspect e — that is, an introverted emotional assessment of something as unacceptable. But, owing to the ambivalence of symbolic constructs, this same function also demands of the subject himself a correct attitude toward the object, even if it proves a source of danger. An imperative of this kind, formed by Don Quixote's CCF, requires of him a careful attitude toward the habitat; and for this he must have formed an adequate internal model of the external environment (IMEE). This is precisely what his PPF (program-potential function) with the aspect I is engaged in. Here extraverted intuition provides cognition of the laws of functioning of the objects of the external world. This information is passed to the CPF (communicative-probing function), where Don Quixote has the aspect l, responsible for the verbal-logical structuring of the regularities discovered at the previous stage and for their translation into the subject environment in the form of messages, teachings, theories.

In the TIM kindred to Don Quixote — Huxley (IEE) — this chain looks somewhat different. S(anf): Huxley, like the Don Quixotes, perceives the world by this function as a source of danger, and this is passed to his l(ccf), which, unlike the Don Quixote's e, assesses the world as "incorrect" — but one that must be handled correctly, for which his I(ppf) must work out an adequate IMEE. The corresponding information is passed to Huxley's e(cpf), which forms the means of adequate communication with the subject environment. These are means answering to the creative, virtuosic use of introverted-emotional information, to which belong all varieties of non-logical charm — which provides the bearers of this TIM the capacity to establish contacts with partners quickly. Analogous interpretations of the symbolic formulas connected with Model A can be offered for all the TIMs.

**Authors:** _Phenomena of which a person is aware may be described by statements from different aspects that are equivalent in sense but not in meaning. Thus the semantic fields of individual aspects may contain both unique meanings and overlapping ones._

**Commentary:** Here there are two judgments formally connected by a cause-and-effect relation, as the operator "thus" testifies. However, it seems that such a consequence is not supported by the content of these judgments. To convince ourselves of this, let us examine the two judgments. In the first assertion the authors use the distinction between sense and meaning introduced in his time by G. Frege. The meaning of a statement has an objective character and is connected with the denotatum — or, what is the same, the extension — or, simply put, with the object to which the corresponding sign points. In the present case, these are words. Sense has a subjective character and is connected with how the subject relates to the denotatum. Proceeding from this, one can partially agree with the first statement, since in speech there can exist statements having the same sense expressed through reliance on different denotata. Thus one can say "yes," "I agree," or "it's a deal." However, a variant is possible in which different senses are expressed by one and the same denotatum. Thus, by means of the word "yes" one can express very different senses, up to and including opposite ones. Remember the joke: "When a diplomat says 'yes,' it means 'maybe'…"

Returning to the quotation from the authors, one must admit that the second judgment in no way follows from the first — which is what the connective "thus" was supposed to indicate.

**Authors:** _The language of the "sensorics of sensations" gives greater possibilities for describing bodily states and processes. The semantic field of this aspect includes a multitude of unique meanings for their fine discrimination. However, these same bodily processes can be approximately described also in the language of the "logic of systems" (the "geometry" of the body: "full up to the roof," "legs wet up to the fork"), and in the language of the "ethics of emotions" (when something inside "thuds," "skips," "rises," and "yelps"), and in the intuitive language, metaphorically ("fog in the head"). In other cases, using the language of introverted sensorics, the language of corporeality, one can describe, for example, a geographic map or the movement of the planets around the Sun._

**Commentary:** The authors use jargon lexicon — "sensorics of sensations," "logic of systems," and the like — which is unacceptable from the editorial point of view, which takes into account the normative lexicon of the Russian language. These homegrown jargonisms, like a psychic contagion, were irresponsibly thrown into socionics under the pretext that "it is better for practice" — they are, supposedly, easier to remember and more comprehensible to the end consumer. Only people who — strange as it is — never understood the meaning of the scientific terms introduced by Jung can motivate this defective terminology in such a way. However, after many years of work with socionic material, I have understood that, despite its seeming simplicity, socionics is difficult — its complexity is masked — and therefore many intelligent and very intelligent people have fallen into the trap of light-minded self-assurance and their own self-importance as connoisseurs and "maîtres" of socionics. However, there is an explanation of why people can successfully earn money on socionics while not fully understanding its basic concepts. The names of the TIMs, their more or less felicitous characterizations, and the relations between them — that which constitutes the core of socionics worked out by Aushra — are easily learned and become the basis of a socionic understanding of people. It does not matter that people do not fully understand what extraversion and introversion and the other differentiating traits are; at the level of words they are not hard to use — the more so as the others, after all, are also at the level of approximateness. Teaching this is not difficult, and the first teacher in any field easily becomes a respected figure, and on this ground it is easy to assert oneself. Along with self-assertion comes self-assurance, and in some — conceit.

To show the divergence in senses between the Aushra–Jungian terminology and the homegrown terminology of the "maîtres," I will give just a few examples.

Introverted sensorics the maîtres replaced with "sensorics of sensations." A cultured bearer of the language feels the defect of this term, since it consists of two words with the same meaning. In editorial practice this is called pleonasm — the pattern of "buttery butter." Extraverted thinking the maîtres replaced with "business logic" — which is reminiscent of the program of courses for businessmen, while all the varieties of productive activity remain in the shadows, resting on an enormous number of varieties of technological processes, from knitting on needles to the smelting of iron and steel, from cooking borscht to building space rockets. Whereas "business logic" is something about how better to "spin" stale goods or "palm off" a doomed project on a partner. Perhaps the official programs of such courses will contain no such items — but experienced instructors will certainly devote a significant part of the class time to precisely this.

**Authors:** _Overlaps can also arise in the sense component — the content of the aspect — as happens in the theme of "foresight" in the extraverted and introverted intuitions. (For details see the section "Overlaps of Aspects.")_

**Commentary:** Here we have come to a very important point, connected with the independence of the aspect lists. From general methodological considerations it should follow that if the semantic fields of the IAs intersect, then the peculiarities of such intersections should be uniformly represented for all the IAs. But here we touch on the vast topic of set-theoretic operations, which goes beyond the bounds of my interests connected with the IAs.

**Authors:** _A scrupulous analysis of the entire vocabulary obtained allowed us to single out the main themes and subthemes for each aspect; moreover, between adjacent aspects (i.e., aspects having a common scale — see Table 1) parallels were clearly noticeable._

**Commentary:** What, then, is the methodological toolkit of the "scrupulous analysis" performed by the authors? It appears to be the same old "intuitive peering" into words and reliance on the intuitive insights arising in the process. This is the common basis of any scientific method — but concrete research techniques proper to the concrete subject area are also necessary.

**Authors:** _In some cases the following effect was observed: one and the same word could be recognized as belonging to different semantic fields._

**Commentary:** There it is! So the "effect was observed" after all! And consequently there is no guarantee that this effect does not extend to all words — that is, that all words are polysemous and can be connected with each of the IAs.

**Authors:** _Therefore it is important to listen not only to what a person speaks about, but also to how and by what means he does so. In this way we substantially broaden our possibilities for diagnostics. It is not necessary to ask targeted questions about saucepans, about illnesses, about whether he likes fantasy and how he feels about tourism. There is less risk of taking a personal history for a typological structure (and an ESE who does not cook and is not ill, but goes hiking and reads fantasy — for an LIE). One can ask any questions, converse with a person on any topic that interests him (be it politics or a second cousin's aunt), noting in his speech either patterns relating to the poles of the trait scales, or those same traits in "folded" form — that is, the lexicon of the aspects._

**Commentary:** The authors draw an important conclusion relating to socionic diagnostics with reliance on projective methods, as counterposed to methods relying on the analysis of verbal answers. It is only a pity that the authors do not cite the predecessors who drew this conclusion many years ago (Gulenko, Bukalov). Unfortunately, this conclusion has in the authors a spontaneous character and is not built into a systemic assessment and comparison of diagnostic means. Otherwise it would be clear that projective methods should not be too rigidly counterposed to content analysis, since verbal content also contains projective components within itself. And is not the work under analysis devoted precisely to the detection of such components in verbal content?

## Conclusion

The authors have carried out a large and interesting piece of work, although many of the concepts they hold to either contain logical errors or are dubious in worldview terms. They attempted to develop a new notion of the IA and to construct the corresponding semantic fields on that basis. However, their definitions of the IA and the ARP lack sufficient grounding — and therefore do not deserve trust. It is entirely incomprehensible why the clear and correct concepts of the IA and the ARP proposed by Aushra and Reinin should be replaced by unsubstantiated conjectures. I have discovered and pointed out various kinds of inconsistencies in the authors' elaboration of this concept. If I am right, then any superstructures over a defective construct, however plausible they may seem, will necessarily contain the defect within themselves. Therefore I do not wish to examine their work further, since, without agreement on the basic terms, there is no point in building far-reaching conclusions.

I will draw attention to just one theoretical circumstance. The fact is that all the basic concepts of socionics are abstractions of a rather high level. For example, we cannot equate extraversion, in level of generality, with such psychic qualities as, say, tidiness, cheerfulness, decisiveness. And all the ARPs are categories of that level of generalization. But, apparently, their semantic fields may include senses and objects of any level of concreteness whatsoever.

The work is unquestionably unfinished and unquestionably must continue, and the results already obtained need careful analysis — both from the methodological and from the socionic point of view. The authors — psychologists by education who have managed, as far as possible, to work their way into socionics — have undertaken a work that has deep foundations in philology. Therefore such a work cannot do without philological expertise. Without such expertise it will of necessity have a preliminary, agenda-setting character.

Let us note that the authors, relying on socionics as a methodological instrument, undertook an attempt to organize linguistic material in accordance with the socionic notions of the IA. However, this very notion is insufficiently clarified in their work. They try to introduce their own concept of the IA, representing it as a composition of linearly dependent ARPs — evidently without fully understanding that the ARPs can be expressed through the IAs, which leads us to a circle in definition. The authors must also be reproached for the fact that the divisions of concepts within the arrays tied to concrete IAs are of that same empirical character.

The authors must be thanked for boldly intruding into a weakly developed theoretical area and exposing themselves to the point of methodological criticism. And, of course, one must not think that their text consists of nothing but errors. These are intelligent people, and professionals besides. Unquestionably, much of use can be extracted from their work — but this will require additional effort.

The hypothesis that an IA unites quite definite objects was first voiced by the Vilnius group of socionists — Shepetko, Filimonov, Ritchik — and it was they who presented the first lists illustrating this hypothesis. Later Ermak supported this hypothesis with his expanded lists. Evidently, behind this hypothesis stands the tacitly accepted conviction that the IAs have an ontological nature — that is, that real objects stand behind them, and not the subjective impressions of human individuals. The authors of the work under analysis have quite definitely declared their position with respect to the IAs, considering them subjective categories performing the role of classifiers that operate at the moment of a person's perception of the IP.

As far as I have been able to understand, the authors in their work proceeded, consciously or subconsciously, from the premise that there exist typological verbal markers closely connected with the IAs and manifesting themselves in speech in connection with the IAs most actualized in the work of the functions of a concrete bearer of one or another TIM. And this, presumably, can be used in socionic diagnostics.
