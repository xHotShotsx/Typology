## 1. Description of intertype relations on the basis of the principle of duality of types and relations

In 1991, at the conference in Vilnius, I put forward the principle of duality with respect to TIMs and intertype relations (ITR). This principle was fortunate: V. V. Gulenko, who had heard my paper, some time later began developing on its basis the ideas of relational socionics, in which the foundation of the theoretical constructions and practical reasoning is not the TIM — the basic object of "classical" socionics — but the ITR. In his article [3] Gulenko gives a perfectly effective formal description of the ITR, distributing them along the Jungian axes and the Augustinavičiūtė–Reinin traits. He writes: "There is no need whatever to invent some new, special distinguishing traits for the ITR. It is far simpler to make use of the ready-made binary traits of the socionic types included in the Augustinavičiūtė–Reinin list [3]." However, such a conclusion can be drawn only on the basis of the parallelism of intertype relations and socionic types, and work of that kind requires theoretical justification. Such a justification could have been a reference to the principle of duality of TIM and ITR, but in his article Gulenko "forgot" to cite my paper, which is unquestionably a violation of the ethical aspect in science. Without such a reference, Gulenko's constructions lose all meaning, since it remains unclear where the parallelism of types and relations comes from. And — what is theoretically important — such an assertion must have sufficient grounds, but V. V. Gulenko adduces no such grounds, and this turns the assertion quoted above into an arbitrary assumption or a "brilliant guess".

By virtue of the principle of duality of types and of the relations between them, and by virtue of the high level of abstraction of the socionic model, it appears possible to describe each ITR directly, using the corresponding TIM models. In this case the description of a relation acquires the same analytical character as the description of a TIM, rather than being built on an intuitive description of the links between the functions of the models of the interacting types. Of course, such a conclusion is justified only if the principle of duality of types and relations corresponds to reality. It is also interesting that, relying on this same idea, the talented socionist A. A. Nemirovsky constructed an alternative classification of the ITR. In his article [4] A. A. Nemirovsky undertook an attempt to change the terminological and semantic basis of the description of the ITR, which can only be welcomed, since it broadens the conceptual basis of socionics.

The formulation of the principle of duality with respect to TIMs and ITR is extremely simple: **the structure of a TIM is isomorphic to the structure of an ITR, and between the set of TIMs and the set of ITR there is a one-to-one correspondence.** From this it follows that all correct assertions employing the concepts of TIM and ITR will remain correct if each of these concepts is replaced by the other. Therefore, if we accept the truth of the statement that between two TIMs an ITR arises, then we must accept the truth of its converse as well — that between two ITR a TIM is formed. This can be understood as something like a holographic picture arising as a result of the intersection of two information flows associated with each of the ITR. Without doubt such an image is hard to grasp, since human perception is oriented above all toward extracting a figure from a ground, and it is not easy to imagine that the figure may turn out to be the result of interference of certain energy fields that are part of the ground. In any case, it is quite unclear how the image proposed above could be interpreted in physical terms. Nevertheless, the heuristic character of the principle of duality points to the existence of a meaningful interpretation of the formally constructed last phrase.

Concerning the introduction of the principle of duality of TIM and ITR, the following may be added. In a private conversation at the XXII conference, O. Karpenko expressed confidence that the principle under discussion follows obviously from G. Reinin's mathematical derivations concerning dichotomous traits of a sixteen-element set, and that on these grounds one ought to cite him. This struck me as strange, and I carefully re-read what Reinin had written about the traits. I found there no hint whatever that he had assumed a duality of TIMs and ITR. Moreover, it later turned out that when Reinin learned of my proposal, he expressed doubt about the applicability of the principle of duality in socionics. What has been said is quite sufficient not to suppose any involvement of Reinin (and he himself never claimed any) in this principle. Subsequently I came to the conclusion that the principle of duality can also be applied to the relation between the Augustinavičiūtė–Reinin traits (ARP), the ITR and the TIMs. However, this conclusion could in no way have been drawn from Reinin's work, if only because Reinin considered only fifteen traits. Only the introduction of the sixteenth Reinin trait — arrived at independently by O. Karpenko and by the present author — created the necessary and sufficient condition for speaking of a principle of duality. But even then it was still necessary to conclude, and to prove, that the set of Reinin traits is strictly ordered. And only after I had carried out this work was it possible to formulate the extended principle of duality, establishing a one-to-one correspondence between TIM, ITR and ARP. Obviously, intuitive declarations on the subject alone would have been insufficient, and only the demonstrative theoretical work carried out gives sufficient grounds to speak of an isomorphism of the three sets indicated.

The correspondence between TIMs and ITR has exactly the same nature as the principle of duality of lines and points in geometry, which asserts that all true geometrical statements mentioning lines and points can be replaced by analogous expressions in which these terms have exchanged places. Into the subject area of socionics this principle can be carried over on the grounds that the form, accepted in mathematics, of representing binary relations as a Cartesian (rectangular) table — with the interacting elements on the margins and the relations at the intersections of rows and columns — may, on the basis of the principle of duality, be replaced by a nomographic form "of aligned points", in which the interacting elements are located as points on straight lines (in the particular case, parallel ones), and the relations between them are represented by straight lines connecting the corresponding points. It was precisely by this scheme that Aushra established the links between functions with identical information content in the "Yu" model, and the character of these connections, their structure, enabled her to identify the relations and describe their features. It is precisely on the basis of the principle of duality that one may assert that, just as there exists a model of the TIM, there must also exist a model of the ITR, isomorphic to the model of the TIM.

We make use of descriptions of relations, including, for example, the description of duality, proposed by Aushra Augustinavičiūtė. She made these descriptions on the basis of an analysis of the transactions that must exist between identical IAs in the models of two different TIMs. For instance, for duality the scheme of transactions using the "Yu" models is presented in Fig. 12.01. Strictly speaking, the configuration given in Fig. 12.01 may be regarded as a model of a relation, since it makes it possible to introduce the concept of a relation and to bring out certain features of relations. However, this model looks incomplete, since it does not represent or use information about all the functions.

The aspects he designates by capital letters in alphabetical order. The resulting configuration is presented in Fig. 12.02.

In this model the letters conventionally designate the IAs admissible in the model, and in this way the necessary generalisation is achieved, whereby not a single relation between two concrete TIMs may be considered, but the relation between any TIMs. However, it remains undefined how a transaction is established if the IA is not designated. The author proceeds from the assumption that he knows in advance in which positions of the models interacting in the given relation the identical IAs are located. From a methodological point of view such an approach appears insufficiently grounded, since the author assigns the type of relation in advance, presupposing a fixed position of the IAs, and then draws the transaction lines — with differing completeness for different relations — and interprets the configuration of transactions. For example, the configuration for the relation of activation is presented in Fig. 12.03. In Medvedev's model of a relation one can see a hint of the permutational character of the operator of a relation, but this theme is not reflected upon by him.

In models of this type it is accepted that in an ITR the interacting functions are those with identical IAs. Although this looks plausible, it is nevertheless necessary to make at least some explication here, rather than take it as self-evident — all the more so since it is not at all difficult to justify the interaction of functions with different aspects. The simplest idea on which one may rely in justifying the interaction of functions with identical aspectual content consists in this: that such functions work on identical subject matter and use identical codes or carriers of these codes, and this presupposes that it is "easier" for them to understand one another. This sounds plausible. A somewhat different justification of the assumption under discussion might rest on the notion of vibrational-energetic fields associated with the ITR, in which case one could say that such functions "work in the same frequency range". This explanation is sufficiently different from the previous one, but also sounds plausible — with one limitation, however: such fields still have to be identified. However, socionics deals not with concrete relations between people but with types of relations, where it is unimportant what the aspectual content of the functions is. What matters here is only which function is in contact with which, not which aspects are in those functions. The semantics of relations is determined by the semantics of the interacting functions, not by their aspects. Therefore it is sufficient to construct combinatorially all variants of interaction of functions in order to reveal all possible types of relations. Of course, one must take into account the systemic constraints existing in the socionic structure of the psyche, and what we know from the scheme of construction of Model A. As for the distribution of IAs among the functions, then, from the standpoint of the theory of relations, it may be treated as a heuristic hint which in its time helped to arrive at the idea of types of relations, but which can be defined from entirely different considerations.

In describing relations by TIM-like models one should bear in mind that the semantics of the corresponding symbols will shift toward the content of relations. In any case an exact verbal interpretation of the symbolic combinations here is extremely difficult and stands in need of elaboration and refinement. Let us note also that the analytical description of ITR by the corresponding models differs somewhat from their descriptions made on the basis of the contacts of functions in the "Yu" model, since in the former case we have more data and parameters for evaluation and interpretation, and consequently there is a more developed basis for obtaining not only a more detailed but also a more reliable description.

It can already be said now that socionic relations (SR) and the everyday relations (ER) that unfold between people in daily life are not at all identical, and that with one and the same SR between people there may arise, become established and develop ER that in no way correspond to them (E. S. Filatova develops thoughts somewhat close to this — see the literature). Duals may quarrel, mock one another, hate one another, either one-sidedly or mutually. Socionic conflictors may passionately love one another, actively help one another, activate and amicably criticise one another. A socionic supervisor may hopelessly fall in love with his supervisee and suffer on that account — though of course in this pair there may also be mutual love, deep friendship and selfless support. A socionic benefactor may understand his beneficiary perfectly, while the latter may turn out to be incapable of catching the fine shades of his partner's thought. So do SR correspond to nothing at all? They do correspond — but not in that literal and straightforward form in which it appeared to socionists at the early stages of socionics' development. In this connection one may say that SR, first, serve as a shell within which ER develop; and second, that what is described as SR is only types of relations, whereas real relations between people, like socionic types, have a multitude of shades, the totality of which may be defined as the character — no longer of a type, but of a relation. For one and the same type of relation, conflict for example, may be stormy or calm, soft or harsh, protracted or short, deep or superficial, and even — however strange it may sound — pleasant, affording deep emotional and aesthetic satisfaction. Conversely, a dual may be unappealing to you, and relations with him may be tense, tedious, burdensome, dangerous — and nevertheless there will be present in them a dual component that will lend a quite definite shade to all the facts of the relation established in the pair. In particular, dual relations afford the partners good access to one another's attention.

Socionic relations may develop in accordance with several generalised scenarios, the principal ones being stabilisation, realisation and degradation.

It must also be said that the intuitive description of the ITR by the contact of "Yu" models is somewhat accentuated — that is, certain of their features are sharpened, so that the impression is created that, for example, dual relations are exceptionally good, while conflict or supervision, on the contrary, are exceptionally bad. Almost two decades of work with socionic material have shown that matters stand somewhat otherwise.

Socionic relations only create the background against which real relations between people develop. They are a kind of overtone of the field of relations established between two people. Moreover, it can now be said with confidence that in any pair, if the relations have stabilised and are maintained at a certain level, all the shades of socionic relations are present — from identity to supervision. If between arbitrarily chosen partners there were absent even the minimal moment of identity (for instance, the use of a common language), they simply would not be able to talk about anything or, under other circumstances, to maintain any relations at all. Belonging to a common social group, social status, level of culture or education, a common sphere of interests, finally common circumstances or the situation in which the partners find themselves, and so on — all these are moments of identification.

In just the same way, in any pair there is always at least some degree of dualisation — a natural distribution of duties, work, roles, the presence of cooperation, mutual aid, interdependence — and if this is absent, the relation becomes unviable and will fall apart. Both partners request something of one another and in some way accept requests from the other; both supervise one another and in something submit; in something people limit one another, feel awkward, do not venture or never say certain things to one another, and so forth. Therefore in a real pair it would be important to reveal the whole spectrum of relations and their quantitative characteristics, and this would make it possible to evaluate the partners' relations more precisely and, if necessary, to carry out psychological correction.

Proceeding from the assumption that a relation has the same model as the corresponding type, one may say that every relation, besides a type, possesses also a character. Thus one and the same socionic scheme of an ITR may yield as great a variety of concrete pictures of relations as the boundless variety of characters that fit within the sixteen socionic types. In this way socionics sets psychology only a scheme for the description of relations. Summing up the foregoing reasoning, one may say that SR represent communicative shells within which two basic types of ER develop — cooperation and confrontation. In describing the ITR we shall adhere to this scheme.

Let us recall that in Lyashkyavichyus's table the correct sequence of types is distorted: in the fourth quadra the LSE and IEE dyads are transposed. Let us also stress that V. V. Gulenko, in our view, constructed the correspondence of TIMs and ITR almost correctly, determining it by the relations of the ILE to all other types; he did not, however, indicate the grounds for the arrangement of the types in the socion, which could have been reduced to a reference to the "Churyumov square", where such grounds are presented in sufficient measure. Besides this, it must be taken into account that to the types Hamlet and Gorky correspond the relations of beneficiary and supervisee, and to the types Stierlitz and Dostoevsky the relations of benefactor and supervisor.

A large, interesting and needed work on relations has been written by P. Tsypin [8] — a talented and promising researcher in socionics. However, many of his constructs and assertions bear a problematic and hypothetical character — as, incidentally, do almost all the remaining concepts of socionics at the present stage of development of this new science, and, of course, mine as well.

Consider the following reasoning of Tsypin's: "The most important thing in intertype relations is the law of perception of an information signal. It states: the human psyche is capable of catching and processing only signals identical to those present in its structure" [8]. Here identity apparently includes also the notion of the sign of a function to which the author adheres. Doubt arises at once that what is at issue is "the most important thing" in ITR. It turns out that the most important thing is not the content of the ITR but some formal trait that has taken the researcher's fancy. Besides, the formulation of the whole phrase has apparently not been edited, and it is not easy to understand what in fact is being spoken of. The formulation of the "law" itself is obviously[^1] incorrect, since the psyche, as is known from psychological research, perceives — at both the conscious and the unconscious level — an enormous quantity of units of information in no way identical to those "present in its structure", since a person perceives not only what is known to him but also what is unknown, and the unknown is not identical to what is known to him. Further, it is unclear why this should be called a law. The author incorrectly qualifies the assertion he advances by designating it with this term. If this assertion is to be considered a law, then it is unclear from where, from what empirical data, this "law" is derived. At best, it might be a hypothesis whose reliability still has to be proved. In essence, however, the proposed construct is nothing more than a rule for constructing and interpreting a model of a relation, on which its author orients himself in his work. It is an assertion that the author accepts as plausible. Therefore, when the author ties this assertion to the interaction of functions with identical signs, the rule he has taken on faith turns into the ground of the truth of his conclusions. But plausibility is not yet truth, and it would be worth warning of this the reader unversed in logical subtleties. And since all the interpretations concerning the interaction of functions with both identical and different signs can be made without appealing to the semantics of the sign, the rule used by the author looks not only doubtful but superfluous. If one takes into account that the explication of the "socionic" signs of functions, invented by V. V. Gulenko at the very "dawn" of Kiev socionics — that is, when the Kiev scholars were only beginning to acquaint themselves with socionics — has no socionic semantics but represents an empirical guess not satisfying the requirements of completeness, consistency, necessity and sufficiency, then, presumably, the defects contained in it will lead to distortion and to the appearance of a hidden systematic error in all subsequent conclusions.

And here is another construct proposed by Tsypin which personally arouses in me strong doubts. He considers that functions differ, among other things, in that some of them work continuously and others discretely. He writes: "Some of the channels of Model A process information continuously; others, on the contrary, emit signals from time to time." This assertion looks very plausible, only it is unknown what its empirical grounds are. Tsypin considers that "the acceptant channels (functions 1, 3, 5, 7) constantly scan the surrounding world", while "the productive channels (2, 4, 6, 8) work with interruptions — for they are functionally dependent on the information of the acceptant channels" [8]. Here the reference to the productive channels being "functionally dependent on the information of the acceptant channels" is apparently regarded as an argument in favour of the discrete working of these channels. But if the acceptant channels work continuously, then information from them must likewise arrive continuously in the productive ones, and consequently the latter must not only receive it continuously but also process it continuously. But of course such reasoning is pure logic. Yet to logic I answer with logic. As for how matters stand in reality, this must be sorted out first of all factually and only then logically, so that logic orders the facts rather than imposing formal possibilities on reality.

It is unclear why productive work must necessarily be discrete. True, it is meaningless to speak in this context of discreteness in general without giving any explication of this concept as applied to socionic functions. Can one, for example, consider such a productive process as the growth of an organism to be a continuous process? That depends on the size of the time step we take, for cells do not divide continuously, and if one takes a sufficiently small interval of time then one can find some moment when not a single cell is dividing and hence there is no growth. To make this reasoning more obvious, let us choose a phase of the organism's development when its embryo consists of a very small number of cells. Then it will be possible to discover that growth — change in the size of the body — occurs discretely.

And yet this is a superficial observation, since it does not take into account intracellular processes, which cannot be ignored in analysing this process. And since intracellular processes proceed continuously, then… And what can be said of the continuity of sensory processes? Their assessment from this point of view likewise depends strongly on the choice of discretisation step. If we take vision, for example, then only from the position of the perceiving subject may it seem continuous. Possibly not everyone knows that the human eyes continuously perform so-called saccadic movements — small rapid displacements of the eyeball — and that the eye sees[^2] only at the moment of its stopping, that is, discretely. Further, there exists a blink rate which does not hinder subjective perception, but it is obvious that the eye does not see an external object while closed. No — of course it still sees, but not that which is being observed. And finally there are the phases of sleep and waking, when the eyes are now switched off as an organ, now switched on. There are also deeper physiological processes, when the renewal of the light sensitivity of rhodopsin takes place in the cones and rods of the eye. This process is very fast but nevertheless makes its contribution to the discreteness of vision. So one may speak of the continuity of the processes of perception only conditionally.

In just the same way, productive processes, for many reasons, cannot be continuous. They have their own regime of discreteness. But just as some factory, from the point of view of its working regime, works discretely, while from the point of view of macroeconomic indicators it works continuously, so too the work of all the functional systems of the organism may, from different points of view, be assessed now as discrete, now as continuous. Therefore, in assessing the work of such systems, one must first set the criterion of that assessment. So how, after all, do the macrofunctional systems of the human psyche work? Alas, from the point of view of "macroeconomic indicators" — or here it would be more apt to say, from the point of view of macro-informational indicators — the macrofunctional systems of the human psyche work continuously. All of them — both acceptant and productive. But from the point of view of working regimes, in assessing the work of the functional systems one will have to choose an entirely different term: they work _cyclically_. There exists an introverted part of the cycle and there exists an extraverted part of the cycle, but on the whole, from the point of view of macro-informational indicators, this work is continuous. Therefore Tsypin's attempt to introduce the distinction under analysis for acceptant and productive functions does not appear well grounded. And consequently all the conclusions he draws from the assumptions he has adopted will also be doubtful.

Nevertheless, Tsypin's description of the ITR is of great interest, since it is carried out with reliance on the contact of all the functions of Model A, and not merely two functions, as was done using the "Yu" model. It is unclear, however, why there is no reference to [9], where this approach was first proposed.

More difficult to analyse and to criticise constructively is Tsypin's conviction that "the informational interaction between two Models A is unidirectional, that is, the stronger function gives a signal to the weaker" [8]. Hence it turns out that the stronger function "supplies with information" any weaker function of the partner's Model A, while the weaker function "is capable only of perceiving informational signals from outside" [8]. However, another view of this problem is also possible, in which the interaction of functions is understood as mutual, going in both directions: from the strong to the weak and from the weak to the strong. Only this influence is different. The influence of the stronger function is active: proposal, intervention. The influence of the weaker function on the stronger is passive: attraction, expectation. Such, for example, is the relation of a nursing infant to its mother — its needs are imperatives for the mother, and this influence upon the mother is very strong. Expectation, or expectancy, is regarded in psychology as a perfectly real influence of one person upon another. Let us note also that the idea of a one-way influence from "the stronger to the weaker function" is apparently the result of a suggestion arising from the designation of transactions by unidirectional arrows.

There is also a structural heuristic prompting the recognition of the mutual influence of any pair of functions upon one another. Thus, if one draws up a "tournament" table of the interaction of functions (see Table 12.01), only by taking this into account can it be filled in on both sides of the left diagonal. Otherwise the whole space above and to the right of this diagonal would have to remain empty. As for the table of interaction of functions itself, at the intersections of columns and rows it indicates the relations in which the corresponding functions interact. This is important, since the semantics of the interaction of functions must be obtained as a composition of the semantics of the interacting functions and the semantics of the corresponding relation — which here, incidentally, acts as a heuristic hint, since the semantics of the relation must itself be derived from the semantics of the functions.

In order to prepare the ground for deriving the semantics of relations from the contact of functions, let us represent Model A in the form of a cube (Fig. 12.04). In this model one can discover several variants of structural links between functions. From a purely structural point of view, functions may be **adjacent-horizontal** (PPF–KZF), **adjacent-vertical** (PPF–IVF), **diagonal-horizontal** (PPF–ANF), **diagonal-vertical** (PPF–MMF), and **diagonal-vertical-horizontal** (PPF–ERF). Of course it is necessary to take into account that there is a zero structural link of any function with itself. All these variants can be reduced to four types. Empirical observations within the socionic paradigm show[^3] that the gradations of the energetic quality of contact in the interaction of the partners' functions are determined by their structural links. Taking the foregoing into account, the following gradations of the energetic quality of contact of functions may be distinguished:

**1.** An **adjacent-vertical** link between functions manifests itself in real contacts as "cautious understanding", which is met with in the mirage relation and in full opposition (PPF–IVF). This contact may be called **passively synergetic**. To it, by structural criteria, one must also assign duality and semi-duality, where diagonal-vertical-horizontal links are present. Within this group of relations the synergy decreases in the direction DU – PD – MI – PP.

**2.** **Adjacent-horizontal** functions are in immediate connection, and therefore when such functions meet in the contact of partners, active confrontation takes place. To this group belong the mirror and supervision relations (PPF–KZF) on the one hand, and conflict and being-supervised (PPF–KKF) on the other. These relations may be assigned to the category of **active antagonism**, but its degree increases in the direction ZE – PK – KN – KF.

**3.** **Diagonal-horizontal** pairs of functions in the ring of the model are separated from and connected to one another through an intermediate function. Such functions themselves are in different blocks, which, it seems, makes their confrontation "deferred" — for antagonism to switch on, additional conditions are needed. In real relations this manifests itself in restrained, or passive, antagonism. Precisely this is what we observe in super-ego and business relations (PPF–ANF). To this group, both on structural considerations and by the results of empirical observations, one must assign identity and kindred relations. The level of tension in this group grows in the direction TZh – RD – DE – SE.

Let us consider Tsypin's assertion about the order of strength of the functions. These assertions are bound up with the intuitive insights of socionists and stand in need of logical elaboration. When Jung spoke of the leading and the subordinate function, one might see in this an implicit indication of their differing strength. However, such a transition from a judgement about subordination to a judgement about strength is only plausible; strictly speaking, it is not obligatory. The leading element need not necessarily be stronger than the led. This is well known in cybernetics, where it is said that a weak informational influence may switch on powerful energy flows. Thus a light press on a button switches on a powerful mechanism. Therefore, in speaking of the strength of functions, one must give a precise definition of what is understood by this, rather than take it as self-evident, since in doing so it is easy to commit the error of identifying strength and influence. Strength is a projection of the archetype of energy, whereas influence is a projection of the archetype of information. With this one may agree if, of course, we accept that strength is the capacity to perform work, while influence is the capacity to change behaviour. Therefore when socionists speak of the strength of a function while thinking of the character of its influence, we have here nothing other than a homonymic shift, in which one and the same word is understood differently by different people. As a result there arises confusion and mutual incomprehension, up to and including rejection and repudiation.

So then, it is not at all difficult to suppose that all the macrofunctions of the psyche are potentially of equal power. In a real bearer of a type, by virtue of his individual features, the energetic power of different functions may be different, but this by no means signifies that his program-potential function will turn out to be the strongest. It is plausible that the energetic power of the functions is built up in the process of the individual's self-realisation. Nor does their influence remain constant, which is well noticeable when comparing people who have realised themselves with those who have suffered existential defeat.

It would also do no harm to clarify what this is — "the energetic power of a function". The simplest supposition is that it is the capacity of a function to pass through itself an energy flow used by it for performing the specific work corresponding to its basic semantics and informational orientation. Despite the plausibility of such a definition, it arouses certain doubts. The point is that, presumably, any function works not in isolation but jointly with others. If we consider that the sequence of functions represents an informational conveyor — and such a representation was formed by Aushra and is widely disseminated among socionists — then it is natural to suppose that all the functions "sit" on one informational-energetic flow which they process and whose ultimate goal is the survival of the living being. Then it is unclear why and how functions can have different power, and why the notion of their energetic hierarchy arises. The simplest answer follows from the analogy with appliances connected to one grid but consuming different power, corresponding to their design parameters. However, this analogy does not entirely correspond to the notion of a functional conveyor, for there what is at issue is the processing of information as a kind of raw material, whose primary mass will at best remain constant, though it is not excluded that, owing to technological costs and waste, this mass ought even to decrease. True, with respect to information one may formulate a law of non-conservation, which might state that information is capable of arising out of nothing (creativity! anti-entropy!) and of vanishing without trace. Although, on the other hand, "manuscripts don't burn".

Such reasoning gives occasion to consider three possible cases. The quantitative measure of informational material in the process of its processing on the functional conveyor: 1) remains constant; 2) decreases; 3) increases. It is not excluded that in reality all three cases occur, but it would be valuable to establish the technologically neutral case, that is, what obtains under normal conditions, and what perhaps is precisely for that reason the norm.

Consider the first case. What might it mean that the volume of informational material remains constant in the process of its processing? First, it must be taken into account that information is always located on some carrier. Therefore one may speak of the complex carrier–information. Presumably, not only energy-informational complexes circulate in the psyche; it is obvious that psychically active information may also "sit" on matter. In any case hormones, unquestionably psychically active substances, have a perfectly definite molecular structure, to say nothing of all other neurotropic substances. We shall not go here into the details of the transfer of a control signal from a molecular carrier to a purely energetic one — what interests us here is the question of the volumes of energy-informational substrate processed by the generalised functions of the psyche. And if we turn to the analogy with the processing of raw material in a technological process, then one may imagine that, ideally, the volume of material in passing from raw material to end product may remain constant. For example, the quantitative measure of clay in a pottery article may be exactly equal to the quantitative measure of the lump of clay from which it was made; whereas in making a Pinocchio out of a log there will be a significant percentage of waste. It is clear that there are many nuances here, but at present we are considering this problem at the most general level. What matters to us is that a case is possible in which the quantitative measure of the initial substrate will be equal to the quantitative measure of the end product. This ought to mean that through each function of the informational conveyor there passes approximately the same volume of energy-informational material, which ought to correspond to approximately identical energetic power of each function. So on what, then, are the ideas about an energetic hierarchy of functions based?

On the other hand, from physiology we know that the physical power of separate organs may vary strongly both within one organism and in comparing different organisms with one another. It is obvious that in a weightlifter separate muscle groups may differ very greatly in power from the corresponding muscle group in a sprinter. This fact is easily linked with trainedness, but behind it, unquestionably, there also stands innate constitution. In connection with this analogy one may speak of the different trainedness of different functions in different people. But whence could a basic difference in the energetic power of the functions in one individual arise? It is clear that, for example, as a result of illness the locomotor apparatus, the nervous system or the gastrointestinal tract may become energetically weaker. But on what grounds may one suppose basic differences in the energetic power of its eight physiological systems? At the same time, in socionics the notion of precisely a basic difference in the powers of the generalised psychic functions is stubbornly maintained.

Let us turn to another analogy. Consider the functional conveyor engaged in digestion in the organism. Let us distinguish in it several blocks: seizing food (the oral cavity), its preliminary treatment for further processing (chewing and moistening with saliva), digestion proper (treatment by the enzymes of the stomach, duodenum, pancreas and liver), assimilation of nutrients (in the intestine, liver and kidneys) and, finally, elimination of the unassimilable residue. Of course, the end product of this process must be considered the assimilable substances, not what is not assimilated. In this case it is obvious that the end product, that is the assimilable substances, constitutes only some part (sometimes a very small one) of the initial substrate. In this functional chain one may see the same scheme of a productive four-stroke thermodynamic process that Aushra used in describing the maximally generalised scheme of information metabolism. Of course, in considering a concrete process it is necessary to adapt the terminology to the semantics of that process. First stroke: accumulation of potential — detection and seizing of food. Second stroke: preliminary preparation of the substrate for further processing — chemoreception, determination of the enzyme complex necessary for digesting the food seized. Third stroke: obtaining the end product — digestion of the food. Fourth stroke: removal of the end product from the working zone and its transfer to transport agents whose task is to deliver the nutrients to the target consumers, where utilisation, or assimilation, of the useful substances will take place. In this scheme we see several functional blocks whose energetic capacity is different, but it is determined by the parameters of energy consumption of each block, which corresponds to its norm. But can one, in the case of a normal regime, speak of an energetic hierarchy? For each block consumes as much energy as it needs, and not in dependence on how much is "allocated" to it in accordance with its place in a hierarchy.

Nevertheless, in observing people the impression is created that some of their psychic functions are more energy-intensive than others. For example, the function of self-assertion in representatives of some types often seems the most noticeable. But possibly here different things are at issue — for noticeability and energetic capacity are not necessarily equivalent.

Proceeding from the analysis carried out, preliminary conclusions may be drawn. 1. Possibly, in socionics the concept of the noticeability of a function and that of its energetic capacity are not distinguished. 2. If this is so, then possibly no energetic hierarchy of functions exists at all. 3. Each function has its own normative energetic provision, which may, however, fluctuate within wide limits depending on innate individual features of the personality and on its personal history, reflected in the differing trainedness of the functions.

Then why does the idea of a hierarchy appear? Perhaps it is a hierarchy by some other criterion? For example, by the criterion of parametric dimensionality, the idea of which belongs to A. V. Bukalov. This is a very productive idea, which may be used to explain the level of instrumental universality of the functions. A. V. Bukalov shows the difference in instrumental universality using the example of the manifestation of the IA of functions of different parametric dimensionality. Thus, one-dimensional introverted logic remains within the bounds of formal logic with its extremely limited correctness. Two-dimensional logic rises to "common sense", subject to the current banality of rationalism, where an excessively straightforward logicality may be compensated by situational interest. Three-dimensional introverted logic is the logic of invention, the logic of finding a way out of a situation and of sophisticated argumentation. And four-dimensional introverted logic is the logic of discovery, of complex theoretical construction, the logic of high abstractions inherent in mathematics or in the best examples of philosophy with its complex reflections. Such an approach would fully explain the hierarchy of functions, but further justification of it is necessary.

A substantial influence on Tsypin's interpretation of relations is also exerted by his understanding of the semantics of the functions. Thus, considering the 7th function to be supervising, he describes its interaction with the 3rd function as "a peculiar benevolent supervision of the partner's achievements in the 3rd function" [8]. "Supervision" is because the author ascribes such semantics to the 7th function, while "benevolence" is because such a contact occurs in the relations of duality and semi-duality, in which these functions meet. That is, the author implicitly understands these relations as benevolent — but their semantics ought to emerge from the semantics of the functions, and not be presupposed as known beforehand because the author learned of it from other sources. As for the "peculiarity", it has to be added as a kind of intuitive coefficient in order to compensate for the socionic understanding associated with the relation of supervision. For what is at issue is the unmentioned duality and semi-duality, not socionic supervision. However, understanding this function as imperative-temporal, which, according to Aushra, not only imposes nothing on others by its IA but imperatively submits, in its subject matter, to the informational state of the environment, we give an entirely different interpretation of the interaction 7–3, or IVF–ANF. The point is that the ANF responds, by its IA, only in tense situations, while the IVF simply imposes nothing; and so it turns out that two "non-imposing" functions are interacting, which is precisely what sheds light on the semantics of duality and semi-duality. Thus there is no question here of any "peculiar benevolent supervision". What is at issue here is rather a "peculiar" considerateness, in the sense of not imposing anything on anybody. Besides this, Tsypin considers that the 7th function is stronger than the 3rd, and that influence, in his opinion, has a one-sided character; therefore he adds that "constructive criticism and the provision of information necessary for the effective work of the aspect of the 3rd is possible". Here it is clear where Tsypin's "criticism" and "constructiveness" come from, but since the given assessment has a very generalised character, it is not so simple to analyse. For, no doubt, constructive criticism and the provision of information are possible from the PPF, from the KZF and from other functions too, so that such a characteristic is not differentiating for the contact 7–3, although possibly it does not contradict its semantics either.

As one more example, let us consider Tsypin's interpretation of the link 2–3 (KZF–ANF), which is formed in conflict and in being-supervised. He considers that this contact "forces each of the participants to mobilise his forces in the aspect of the normative function, constantly to work on himself so as not to fall in the partner's eyes". Since in assessing the ANF he places the accent on its normativity, one can understand the requirement "constantly to work on oneself" — this is in order to correspond to norms. However, it is quite unclear where the fragment "so as not to fall in the partner's eyes" comes from. This is some sort of tertiary thematic interpretation, out of place in the basic format of a primary interpretation. All the rest in no way follows from the semantics of the functions he adopts. It is unclear why a communicative function should require mobilisation. The communicative function is tuned to all-round discussion, and in contact with a "normative" one it might discuss the norms. However, the normative aspect of the ANF constitutes only a secondary part of its semantics — the result of adaptation to the environment. The core of this function is confrontation with the pressure of the environment. From this one might understand the requirement "to mobilise one's forces", but Tsypin has a different semantics, from which this does not follow. Proceeding from my own understanding of the semantics of the functions in question, I propose an entirely different interpretation: "all-round discussion of the partner's behaviour in a tense situation". In a tense situation one must act, not analyse the partner's behaviour. Understanding such a contact as discussion instead of joint action corresponds to the semantics of the relations of conflict and being-supervised, in which the contact under analysis appears.

I consider that the semantics of a contact of functions is determined by the semantics of the functions entering into it and by the order of their address to one another. Therefore, for example, the contact 1–2 will be interpreted taking into account the concepts standing behind the corresponding functions — program (PPF) and communication (KZF). Thus, in the given case, one function offers a program (I), that is, something fundamental, while the other perceives this program merely as a communicative pretext or as an opportunity "to talk about something", to socialise, and, among other things, "to look at the proposed theme from different sides, to subject it to logical analysis, to determine its place in the hierarchy of values (l)". On the other hand, the contact 2–1, taking into account the same key words, may be understood in such a way that one function "simply wants to talk about something, to socialise (l)", while the other is preoccupied with fundamental things (I) and can offer nothing else. From this the semantics of the mirror relation becomes clear.

One more example. 3–7 = extreme situation + cooperation. An expanded interpretation may look like "expectation of support in extreme situations". And the contact 7–3 may be understood as "an offer of cooperation in extreme situations". Both these possibilities describe, from different sides, part of the semantics of duality and semi-duality.

### Principles of interpretation of relations

1. The semantics of relations must be obtained as a superposition of the semantics of the functions meeting in the concrete relations and the semantics of the synergy or antagonism characterising the structural links between the functions.
2. The semantics of a function may be partly represented by key words characterising the process of work or the end product of the function.
3. Address is possible from any function to any other, but it is unambiguous for any pair of TIMs.
4. In order to unify the primary interpretations, we shall begin the reading with the symbol of the first partner (in parentheses).
5. A primary interpretation is the result of substituting, in place of the symbols of the archetypes, their speech equivalents relating to a concrete subject area and mutually concordant according to the rules of the language. The simplest interpretation is the substitution of key words and key expressions in place of the corresponding symbols. As a result we obtain nuclear judgements.
6. Secondary interpretations are correct semantic transformations of the nuclear judgements.
7. Tertiary interpretations are the result of further correct transformations of already existing secondary interpretations, with a transition into other subject areas and thematic spheres.
8. All primary interpretations must be sustained in a single stylistic and semantic key, and must also belong to one level of generalisation. Thus, by choosing different stylistics, different semantic frames and different levels of generalisation, one may obtain different sets of primary interpretations.

**RTF – KKF (DU, MI):** the product obtained by one partner is assessed with understanding by the other. Passive synergy. Compatibility by discomfort.

**RTF – ERF (KF, KN):** the product obtained by one partner does not correspond to the resource the other needs. Basic incompatibility. Active antagonism. Sphere of compatibility: law, discussions, negotiations, or by necessity.

**RTF – MMF (RD, SE):** the product obtained by one partner does not stimulate the other. Passive antagonism. Compatibility by comfort; free-time activity without obligations or by virtue of personal interest.

**RTF – IVF (ZE, PK):** the product obtained by one partner distracts the other from the solution of his own problems. Basic incompatibility. Active antagonism. Sphere of compatibility: law, discussions, negotiations, or by necessity.

**RTF – RTF (TZh, DE):** the product obtained by one partner is counterposed to the product produced by the other. Passive antagonism. Compatibility by comfort; free-time activity without obligations or by virtue of personal interest.

### CONCLUSIONS ON THE WHOLE SET OF FUNCTION INTERACTIONS (secondary and tertiary interpretations)

This relation may quite naturally be called the relation of identity, since it is a relation of identical TIMs and identical functions come into contact in it. However, people with the same TIM may possess strongly differing characters, which may greatly alter the picture of interaction in a concrete pair. Socionics deals with generalised characteristics of relations, without going into the concrete details that depend on concrete circumstances.

When bearers of identical types find themselves in one another's communicative field, they perceive one another with interest but, as a rule, avoid showing initiative, subconsciously expecting it from the other. Both partners subconsciously feel an attraction to one another and display curiosity and readiness to become better acquainted with someone who is so strongly like them in something not always comprehensible. However, the attraction here does not have an emotional character but rests on belonging to one class of objects. Identicals may render one another help and perform work natural to them that the other needs. They may quickly adapt to joint work in one group, including simply as a pair. In this case there is good mutual understanding, especially if they work under the direction of some third person. If, however, owing to the circumstances that have arisen, one of the partners must take on the direction of the joint work, then the work will proceed successfully only if the second consciously takes on the corresponding part of the duties — that is, if they are able to come to an agreement, for which there are typological grounds.

Each of them has his own world, his own affairs, which they cannot sacrifice; but if their concrete life attitudes, goals, ethical norms and interests turn out to be compatible, they may perfectly well unite their efforts. If between them there are differences of view on matters of principle, it is difficult for them to reconcile these, and after clarifying the state of affairs (for example, after lengthy discussions and arguments) they prefer to remain each with his own opinion and not to try to convert the other; but they are able to accept one another as they are. When identicals find themselves in immediate proximity and intuitively, without verbalising it, recognise their identity, they begin to examine one another with interest, as it were probing, checking and clarifying the partner's characteristics. In this process the logical, formal and evaluative side outweighs the emotional, which undoubtedly may be present in this relation, as indeed in all others, but plays a passive role here. A man and a woman of the same type may of course fall in love with one another, but such love may quickly pass into friendship, which is also not bad, or ripen to a state in which the people are capable of parting with dignity and respect, which will likewise correspond to the TIM.

Thanks to the effect of sufficient mutual understanding predominating in such a pair, it is relatively easy for identicals to exchange mutually useful and, especially, information new to one of the partners — not necessarily but predominantly verbal, which is, incidentally, coloured by the IA of the KZF. Of course, they may transmit to one another information about dancing, dressmaking and sewing, cooking, or talk about new theoretical conceptions, but the verbal, demonstrating, explaining, sign component plays the predominant role in this case. This state of affairs contributes to an identical being, in comparison with other TIMs, the best teacher.

If the flow of new information dries up, identicals fall silent and may calmly increase the distance, since the relation in this case is maintained precisely by the exchange of information. As Aushra says, "to an outsider observing silent identicals it may seem that they are sulking at one another, but they simply have no topics for conversation at the given moment." For the identicals themselves silence may become tense, since it looks like doing nothing, and the partners may begin to be burdened by one another's presence, hurrying off to occupy themselves with something useful.

Besides this, identicals may subconsciously regard one another as members, so to speak, of one clone — that is, as a set of fully identical subjects — although between them, given identity of socionic types, there may be very great differences in character.

At long distances identicals, within the bounds of their competence, easily perceive information generated by another bearer of the same type in statements, books, works of art, political activity.

However, between identicals competition, rivalry, struggle, confrontation are potentially possible. At short and at long distances identicals may wage a struggle among themselves, exert force pressure on one another, but always within the bounds of ethical limitations comprehensible to them, going beyond which they count among "forbidden methods". Very characteristic in this case is discussion aimed at clarifying concepts or positions.

Volitional pressure from one of the partners evokes corresponding pressure from the other, but all this fits within the framework of functional equality, assessing the partner's behaviour as admissible and comprehensible, which is formalised in the semantics of the corresponding IA. Thus do wolves, grizzly bears, deer or black grouse fight among themselves — it is a duel within the bounds of basic equality and species limitations.

When in any form of confrontation (competition, contest, rivalry) one of the identicals outstrips the other, wins or gains an advantage, the other is capable of understanding and accepting this as a worthy victory. Nevertheless, the emotional reactions may be arbitrarily strong and have any colouring, from sympathy and admiration to hatred and contempt, but in them there will always be present the element of an attitude toward an equal.

Both partners cannot bear limitations in the sphere connected with their attitude to building relations, but the basic identity of this sphere does not permit them to criticise one another's ethical attitudes, although they are able to assess critically the other's critical attitude to ethical attitudes in general. The way in which one partner tries to establish relations with the other does not evoke rejection in the latter, since their basic standards of integrity are the same. However one of the partners may act, for the other it will be self-evident, since, owing to the coincidence of their basic notions of integrity, they cannot impose ethical limitations on one another's relations and behaviour. They are, as it were, embarrassed by this, and skirt round those moments that fall within the sphere of their mutual psychological or social incompetence, feeling that it is absurd to make the other remarks of this kind, since everything is self-evident anyway and applies equally to you personally. Incidentally, they do not cross the barriers natural to them. In the case of identity, it as a rule simply does not occur to the partners to make one another remarks of a generalised ethical character, even if they do form the impression that some act is inappropriate — they may think that the other has sufficient, though perhaps unknown, grounds for it. Between the partners, in the sphere of incompetence common to them, a tacit agreement establishes itself of its own accord, by virtue of which they do not overstep tabooed limits, and a violation of such an unspoken compact is perceived by each of them as the use of a "forbidden method".

Identicals have a common zone of expected, presumed or actual resource, which may constitute both the basis of an alliance and interaction and the basis of competition. Identicals are tuned to analogous life resources, and this may both unite them in achieving common goals and become the basis of rivalry. Identicals are capable of becoming for one another a source of life resource, and this could constitute the basis of the well-being of such a tandem. However, this is possible only given a conscious attitude to the common cause, when one of them consciously takes the position of leader while the second helps him to arrange the provision of the life resource, thereby dualising his activity.

Since identicals have similar notions of comfort (compatibility by comfort!), it is not difficult for them to be together and successfully spend time under conditions where one can occupy oneself with something interesting, relax, or where there is no need to exert oneself — that is, where their weak functions are not subjected to loads.

Identicals have similar emotional reactions, and they perceive one another in a similar emotional key. Their primary emotional reactions to one another are to a significant degree equivalent, and they require identical stimuli in order to switch into working mode.

The emotions of identicals have similar parameters. They intuitively understand one another's emotional states. As a result a peculiar emotional stupor may arise, gradually passing into emotional satisfaction from the recognition of the known. Emotional mobilisation in identicals occurs under similar conditions, and they may jointly spend time connected with entertainment or endure circumstances under which significant working loads fall upon them. However, at the basis of their relations lies, after all, not emotional attachment — which may nevertheless be very strong — but informational, cognitive, communicative-logical interest.

Identicals structure their time analogously; they have a similar attitude to the expenditure of time, a close style of acquiring life experience; they may easily take part in similar sequences of events, travel together, go together to lectures, to a club, take up sport.

Identicals may jointly perform work corresponding to the social request they intuitively perceive. This work may be perceived by them as needed by themselves or by someone else; they may work in one brigade, group or team, when they jointly work out the rules of working behaviour or submit to a third person, and they thereby understand the sense and purpose of the work performed in the same way.

Identicals make up stable and capable groups directed at achieving goals whose character corresponds to the IA of their EGO block, especially when such a group is headed by an identical, a dual or an activator.

The background and content of identity relations improve when the partners do not shut themselves up in a narrow circle, do not impose rigid limitations on one another, do not count on a strong emotional bond, and have common goals.

Love and marriage between identicals may contain all shades from eros to pragma (Meged, Ovcharov), and may turn out to be sufficiently stable on condition that their weak functions are covered by some third party, for example parents, or that they have consciously divided the duties. The partners rather easily accept the other's experience and way of life.

Sometimes in such cases an illusion of complete well-being may arise; for example, the everyday standard of living of two SEEs may be very high — good housing conditions, well-arranged processes of nutrition and rest — but their spiritual level, viewed from outside, may seem primitive. In the case of two ILEs, however, with joint purposeful work, training, attendance at all manner of seminars, domestic life may turn out to be completely neglected. In these cases a subconscious dissatisfaction may accumulate which in the end leads to the cessation of the relations. Identicals may leave one another with full understanding that their union has exhausted its possibilities and that they must continue their further life path alone or with someone else.

An identical may become the best teacher for another because of the readiness to perceive and the ability to understand one another. The information flow coming from an identical is comprehensible, well perceived (often because of the identity of representational systems) and prompts reflection. Between identicals clarifying discussions and topic-developing debates easily arise. They feel and perceive the topic in a similar way and strive for its optimal development. Here in the foreground is the search for truth.

A large role in the character of such communication is played by the similarity of attitude to the problems connected with the KKF. However, in the case of an intersection of interests, competition and a struggle for power with intrigues and manipulations may easily unfold between them. Yet even so they jointly avoid using methods that are considered forbidden for them, that is, connected with the subject matter of the fourth function. If in competitive struggle one of them gains the upper hand, the second accepts this as the result of real superiority.

The Augustinavičiūtė–Reinin traits inherent in the relation of identity allow us to refine certain parameters of the interaction.

The relations of identicals gain if they are open and take place in an environment of numerous friends and acquaintances. Identicals empathically support in one another the confidence that one must take life lightly and look at things simply. The best regime of relations for identicals is creative cooperation. Spontaneity, freedom, ease, scope, the ability to accept the other as he is, the ability to yield. In the relations of identicals the humour available to them works well. Identicals may permit themselves straightforwardness in judgements and statements. Everything must be constructive, logical and unobtrusive. It is important that they have joint prospects and goals of a strategic order. The stability of their life situation has a positive influence on the identicals' well-being.

### CONCLUSIONS ON THE WHOLE SET OF FUNCTION INTERACTIONS

Duals are able relatively easily to become a source of life resource for one another. What in an identical pair can be achieved only given a conscious attitude to the common cause, in duals comes about by virtue of the natural division of labour in accordance with the IAs of their first blocks. Duals, by providing one another with a resource, help to unfold one another's potential possibilities.

However, in order for the effect of dualisation to set in, the dualisation ring must switch on, and for this specific conditions must arise — the joint overcoming of discomfort, the essential components of which may be described in terms of the IAs corresponding to the duals' EGO blocks. In this sense one may say that duals are compatible by discomfort. One of the forms of such a condition is rendering help in performing work that is difficult for the other partner. Duals receive emotional satisfaction from communicating with one another, are emotionally mobilised and feel emotional security in one another's presence. The emotional reactions of one partner provoke communicative attention toward him on the part of the other. Communication is accompanied by an emotional exchange and by the arousal in one another of positive emotions.

A state of volitional mobilisation on the part of one partner is perceived by the other as important life experience. Under a loading influence of the external environment duals plunge into a state of working concentration on eliminating the real or possible threat. They spontaneously allocate and spend the time necessary, by their notions, in such cases and jointly organise the sequence of their own events in resisting negative loads, performing as required the work adequate to the possibilities and abilities of each of them. In such a pair joint resistance to the pressure of the environment, joint struggle against life's difficulties represents a vitally important event; the vitally important events in this pair are filled with a joint struggle for existence. If external pressure is absent, life in a dual pair may become stagnant.

Each of the partners in this pair perceives positively the other's ability to perform productive work in which he is competent. In a critical situation this may serve as an occasion for each partner to take on that part of the necessary work in which he feels competent. In this pair an attempt by one partner to establish relations with the other does not evoke rejection if it is based on rendering help in performing work difficult for the other. A dual is ready, even in contradiction with his own convictions, to perform work for his dual and to give him support in that sphere of life where the latter is insufficiently competent, limited in time, or simply in need of help. By changing the modality of the relation, one partner may influence the features of his partner's end product or his attitude to productive activity.

Although the essence of dual relations is emotional comfort, it is nevertheless not attained automatically. For this the relation must be tuned, which presupposes the ability to come to an agreement with the partner. According to the model, a dual is ready to respond to the corresponding proposals of his partner, since a priori they must correspond to the subject matter of the IA of his ERF. Still, in this way only a necessary but by no means sufficient condition is formulated. Incidentally, various kinds of expectancies on the part of one of the duals may sometimes be perceived by the other as a burden and evoke a desire to increase and maintain distance, which in turn may aggravate the emotional state of the other.

The feeling of emotional cover that arises in the partner's presence is expressed in a special sense of security and is apparently due to the closing of the informational rings linking the EGO and SUPERID blocks of the duals. This reduces to a minimum the possibility of informational confrontation with the corresponding possible negative emotional tension. In simple words this is expressed in the fact that what one of the partners offers from his EGO, the other expects and accepts by his SUPERID.

Once relations in such a pair are established, they lead to a mutual emotional feeding which gives the partners the strength necessary for mobilisation and the achievement of joint goals. Since emotionality in the given case is connected with the work of the KZF, it above all has a positive character; however, if negative moments arise, this function may also work in a negative key, using sophisticated manipulative moves. Still, since in a dual pair the KZF addresses the MMF, this leads only to the partner's emotional mobilisation and does not entail hostile embitterment and estrangement, as might be the case with an address to the KKF.

Duals jointly resist the pressure of the environment, performing as required the adequate work corresponding to their possibilities and abilities. In this pair each partner has an imperative impulse to spend his time supporting the other in tense situations. However, since each of the partners has his own spectrum of what constitutes problems for him, while the second partner is precisely a specialist in solving problems of just that kind, the pair turns out to be capable of withstanding a double set of difficulties. This is one of the causes contributing to the stability of the pair. However, the reverse side of stability may turn out to be stagnation and the loss of prospects.

A dual gives his partner the possibility of freeing himself from performing work that slows self-realisation, and this contributes to unfolding the potential possibilities of each of them. The resource provided by one partner to the other contributes to inspiration and the appearance of original ideas in the second partner. Dual relations are comfortable, which is expressed in the natural division of duties, labour and spheres of responsibility, in the absence of typological reasons for the appearance of negative emotional tensions, in the absence of compulsory duties, when there is no need to establish who is in charge here, while possible labour loads and duties are distributed naturally. Each partner himself takes on the manageable part of the work corresponding to his typological competence. Comfort is the potential possibility and the program of this relation; however, this is only a precondition which will become reality other things being equal.

If the need arises, duals are ready to play the role of intermediary in their partner's affairs, and do this selflessly and effectively, at the level of performing work in which they are competent.

These relations can truly unfold their possibilities only given close emotional communication on the basis of practical mutual aid, and on condition that the duals manage to teach one another complete trust — but in doing so one should act spontaneously, relying on improvisation, and this does not always come off.

The partners are potentially a source of life resource for one another, and in the broad sense of the word each of them is a resource for the other. One partner is ready and able tactfully to render help in the corresponding specific sphere in which his partner does not tolerate limitations but proceeds from his own notions and interests.

In this pair both partners perceive one another's life programs with interest; for them this is a positive life resource and they find one another interesting. Communicative acts directed from one partner to the other evoke an emotional elation that gives strength for solving life's problems. Each of the partners mobilises and resists the pressure of the environment differently, and is ready to use his life experience to help the other. This leads to the partners feeling secure in one another's presence, and they do not strain one another. An area in which one of the partners is not competent, or to which by the features of his way of life he cannot devote sufficient attention, may relatively easily be taken under control by the dual. Each of them does this relatively easily, although a preliminary tuning or training may be required in order to realise the importance of performing the corresponding section of work for the other or for joint survival. A dual does this tactfully and naturally, so that the other partner experiences no counteraction or feeling of becoming a debtor.

Each partner expects the corresponding information and the corresponding communicative acts containing potential possibilities important for him. When this happens, a person experiences deep satisfaction and quickly restores his expended strength, and his psychic state is harmonised.

The relations develop optimally if the partners spend more time in a narrow circle, act spontaneously and unconstrainedly, place the accent on the emotional side of the relations, and strive to achieve concrete pragmatic goals.

A high level of positive emotionality is a potential possibility of these relations; the ideas and proposals of one partner, saturated with potential possibilities, evoke emotional elation in the other.

If this side of their relations acquires a positive shade, then correspondingly their life too is filled with positive content and meaning. If, however, their relations acquire a negative shade, it will likewise be difficult for them to part with one another, and they will just as strongly, as in the positive case, excite one another emotionally. In this case such emotionality will also bear a toning and mobilising character, but this will now be a struggle with one another, and each of them will seek new arguments to prove his rightness. Yet in the depths of their souls they will regret that the conflict prevents them from reaching out their hands to one another.

The partners, in a regime of volitional mobilisation, are capable of jointly performing the necessary work directed at obtaining the end product. If problems arise, of any complexity and any scale, the partners instantly switch into work in order to solve them, and do this persistently and stubbornly. The volitional mobilisation of one partner stimulates the working capacity of the other. This is also perceived as the performance of responsible and necessary productive work aimed at overcoming difficulties connected with a loading influence, with the pressure of the environment, with the features of the situation, or with the conscious achievement of the goals set. One partner has the possibility of changing the other's well-being, which opens to him the path to direct informational influence upon the latter. Since kindred types have similar notions of comfort (compatibility by comfort!), it is not difficult for them to be together and successfully spend time under conditions where one can occupy oneself with something interesting, relax, or where there is no need to exert oneself — that is, where their weak functions are not subjected to loads. For kindred types similar conditions and situations are comfortable; they may spend time together in comfortable conditions when there are no aggravating circumstances.

At first it is not even clear how the partner's work can evoke tense emotions and wariness; but in the case of kindred relations the partners do not understand one another when one of them tries to perform physical work. This puts them on guard and strains them. It seems to one partner that the other is too unsuited for such work, that he would do better to occupy himself with something else.

The partners try so far as possible to entertain one another, to teach and to learn from the other what he can do.

If the development of events in this pair takes on a negative character, the partners may enter into a struggle of force in which any means may be employed — mockery, insults and even slander — but this is always very witty. It is very difficult for the partners to trust one another completely, since each of them has his own notions of what must be done and how one should behave. Any actions of one partner, if they are in one way or another directed at the second, may be assessed as manipulative.

If the partners' programs coincide, they consider it their duty to defend one another, to help in structuring space and information, and here they may attain heights of mastery.

The partner's professional activity, his special abilities, may evoke a restrained or tense emotional reaction. The partners expect from one another the performance of practical work directed at improving well-being and conditions of existence, but they cannot fully provide this for one another. They may easily take part in similar sequences of events, travel together, take part in club activity — everywhere they can feel sufficiently light and unconstrained.

The partners try to inspire one another, providing each other with information connected with life experience, but very often behind this stands manipulation.

In order for this relation to develop optimally, the partners should spend more time among other people, in the presence of acquaintances and relatives, be spontaneous, not plan joint actions, not demand pragmatic returns from joint actions, and appeal to the other's emotions and emotional experience.

The reasoning given makes it possible to divide all relations into four structural groups: 1) TZh, DE, SE, RD; 2) DU, MI, PP, PD; 3) AK, ZK, KV, PZ; 4) ZE, KN, KF, PK.

As it is not difficult to verify, the result obtained fully coincides with the result presented in Table 12.05.

## INTERPRETATION OF THE CONTACTS OF FUNCTIONS IN THE RELATIONS OF TIMs

### TZh (identity relations)

If the addresses of the functions in each ITR are expressed graphically, tying them to Model A, we obtain the schemes presented in Fig. 12.06. As we see, each relation has an individual pattern of such addressing. Examining these schemes, it is not difficult to notice that in TZh and DU the order of address of the functional half-strokes has a form that may be called parallel. In AK and ZE the order of address has a pattern mirror-symmetric to the pattern of TZh and DU. However, between these patterns there is also a similarity, which is precisely what is revealed by the mirror symmetry. The same may be said of the pairs ZK, KN and DE, MI; KV, KF and SE, PP; RD, PD and PZ, PK — which unquestionably unites these pairs of relations into quadra groups. In such groups of relations the vital and mental rings turn out to be pairwise identical. Presumably these identities point also to a semantic commonality both in the groups of relations and in the groups of types corresponding to them.

In the relations ZK and KN the order of address begins at the end point of each ring, while its direction is the same. In the relations KV and KF the order of address begins at the end point of each ring, is directed to the opposite side, and finishes at the starting point. Analogous observations may be made in connection with all the models of relations. One would like to hope that the schemes of the order of address have a definite heuristic sense which still awaits its interpretation.

The interpretation of the models of relations proposed above should be regarded as preliminary and admitting various refinements, which is connected with the insufficient unambiguity and elaboration of the mechanisms of interpretation. As these mechanisms are perfected, the possibility will also appear of refining the decoding of the models of a relation.

Let me note that of all the descriptions of relations known to me, the most brilliant in the literary sense belongs to V. Shlaina [7]. Superb poetic language, refined metaphors, subtle comparisons, three-storey epigraphs — all this produces an irresistible impression and speaks of an extraordinary literary talent. This gives grounds for foreseeing the unconditional success of her texts with prospective readers. However, from the point of view of logic, everything written in V. Shlaina's article follows from nothing. That is, partly it follows from everything that others, and of course Aushra, have written on this subject. But Shlaina has no justification of her own for the structure of the relations, and therefore the descriptions she proposes are perceived as beautiful but of little reliability. Despite this, however, the very reading of such a text has a therapeutic significance. Read, for example, the description of duality, and you will feel a surge of strength and inspiration, and your faith in people will grow stronger. And that is worth a great deal.

## Conclusions to the twelfth chapter

1. A principle of duality with respect to TIMs and ITR has been proposed, according to which the structure of a TIM is isomorphic to the structure of an ITR.
2. A review has been made of part of the existing work in socionics on the theory of relations.
3. Two types of models of the ITR have been presented and their essential identity shown.
4. Structures of the ITR expressed through the contacts of functions have been constructed.
5. Certain principles of interpretation of relations have been formulated.
6. Preliminary primary interpretations of all the functions of the ITR have been made, and secondary descriptions of all the ITR proposed.
