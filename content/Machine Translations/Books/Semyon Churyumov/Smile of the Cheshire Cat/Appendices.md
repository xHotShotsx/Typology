## Appendix 2. Description of the cover picture

The cover design uses a motif from John Tenniel's illustration to _Alice in Wonderland_. That picture, and in particular the image of the Cheshire Cat, were agreed with Lewis Carroll himself. As applied to the cover of the present book, the picture may be understood as follows. In the foreground we see the questioner, represented by a conscientious schoolgirl open to the apprehension of truth. She may also be understood as a representative of the _tonal_ — that is, of that part of reality which is more or less accessible to human understanding.

The Cat, perched on a tree branch, looks rather odd. But what else would you expect from the characters of Carroll's tale? Recall the Mad Hatter, the Dormouse, the Rabbit, the Duchesses and the Queens, who are thoroughly not of this world. As for the Cheshire Cat, one should note also that in the original the word used for his smile is "grin", not "smile" — and the first word carries the sense of a bared-teeth expression, a smirk; although, given the situation of the tale and the character of the Cheshire Cat, the translators were right to render it as "улыбка" (smile). Nevertheless the Russian-speaking reader catches the seme of the smirk of an agent who knows something his insufficiently informed interlocutor does not. There is no arrogance in this smirk. It is, rather, the equivalent of an understanding of complexities. The Cheshire Cat on the cover is a representative of the _nagual_ — that is, of that part of our world which can be grasped by human reason only with difficulty, if at all. The image created by Tenniel corresponds well to the spirit of such representation. However, in the scene depicted on the cover there is also a third agent. This is the Light, the Light of Knowledge. This Light is at the same time an allusion to the presence of Aušra and her enlightening mission.

The cover of the first book, preserving the original fawn colour range of Tenniel's drawing, corresponds to morning, when the conversation is only beginning. On the cover of the second book, where more complex and more paradoxical questions are discussed, a saturated green is used, which together with the Light breaking through the foliage creates the atmosphere of afternoon in the depths of the forest. For the cover of the third book, where predominantly general-theoretical and methodological problems are discussed, along with problems of interdisciplinary interaction, blue was chosen, which together with the Light symbolises late evening beside a campfire in the forest, when the conversation is drawing to its close.

The placement of the schoolgirl with her back to the reader also sets the position of the reader himself, who together with the schoolgirl is trying to penetrate the secrets of the new science. The Light is the true bearer of meaning, while the Cat is merely its keeper and guardian.

## Appendix 3. Generalisation of the concept of a model of the psyche

At the foundation of socionics and of socionic models lies the methodological principle of _intuitive dichotomisation_, which is not recognised by empirical science, since it is considered speculative (read: intuitive) and is rejected by materialistically oriented ideologies as idealistic. This is done on an ideological basis and a priori. Once ideological prohibitions have been lifted there is no sense in preserving and adhering to this absurd a priori restriction. However, as is clear from general theoretical considerations, and as Schneiderman showed in his lectures at the Kiev House of Scientists, dichotomy is not the only principle for generating the polarities on which the systems existing in the world are based. Apparently three-pole, five-pole and similar structures are possible and are realised in our cosmos.

On the basis of results achieved by modern science — mathematics, physics, systems theory, logic, methodology, epistemology and others — the following general scientific postulates may be advanced:

1. Any object or system possesses energy levels inherent to it.
2. Any object or system can be described within a chosen paradigm by ascribing to it the number of energy levels characteristic of that paradigm.
3. Paradigms, or their subsets, rest on an empirically or theoretically identified number of energy levels.
4. Each energy level, by virtue of its external functioning, turns out to be linked with a definite informational aspect; the system of these aspects is generated by the object's external functioning and is complementary to the system of energy levels.
5. The qualitative characteristics of an object or system are determined by the combinatorics of the pairings of informational aspects with the energy levels of the object's or system's functioning.
6. The relations between objects within a concrete paradigm are determined by the combinatorics of the connecting lines joining the various energy levels to the corresponding informational aspects.

In science and for practical purposes, wide use is made of the model of describing binary relations in the form of a "tournament table", such as V. A. Lyashkyavichyus's table, which vividly presents the relations between all the types. This model corresponds exactly to a rectangular system of Cartesian coordinates. Such a system, by its construction, is a configuration linking straight lines and points to one another. On the basis of the principle of duality it can be transformed into the configuration dual to it, likewise consisting of points and lines which, in a certain sense, have exchanged places: in it the mutually perpendicular coordinate axes will now correspond to parallel line-axes, by means of which not points but lines of the plane will be determined, and these lines will connect points on the axes obtained as a result of the duality transformation. This corresponds exactly to the configuration for establishing intertype relations in socionics, where parallel rows of points are joined to one another by straight lines.

The reasoning set out above makes it possible to conclude that models for establishing binary relations have a general mathematical foundation and are not restricted to any given number of inputs; and this makes it possible to generalise the dual model of binary relations on parallel lines to any number of inputs, for which one may take the energy levels of a system's functioning and the informational aspects corresponding to them.

The socionic model "Yu" is built according to definite rules, which may be formulated as follows:

1. There is an ordered set of functions to which different energy states of the TIM correspond.
2. Four functions are linked with four informational aspects in such a way that different functions correspond to different aspects, and no aspect is repeated twice.
3. Aspects belonging to the rational and irrational axes are placed symmetrically with respect to the middle of the model.
4. If the first aspect is extraverted, then all the rest are introverted, and vice versa.

By varying the set of rules of this kind, and also by changing the number of functions and IAs, it is easy to obtain a large variety of informational-energetic models.

As examples of paradigms with differing numbers of energy levels and informational aspects one may cite Berne's three-level model (PAC: Parent–Adult–Child); astrology, where the planets act as the energy levels of functioning and the signs of the Zodiac as the informational aspects; or spiritual teachings with various sets of energy centres — 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 12, and so on.

In the astrological model there is only one corresponding rule. Any of the twelve functions, whose role in astrology is played by the planets, may be matched with any IA — here the signs of the Zodiac — in arbitrary order, so that any number of planets may be located in a single sign, while in some signs there may be no planets at all. In this case one may speak of a _free_ combinatorics of functions and informational aspects. In the case of socionics, the corresponding combinatorics has a _bound_ character.

Generalisation of the socionic model may proceed not only along the number of aspects and functions, but also along the peculiarities of the constraints imposed.

A great deal of work in this direction has been done by the remarkable Moscow astrologer P. Maksimov.

## Appendix 5. The court deck in the PSTIM

The court deck was studied at length and fruitfully by the remarkable Ukrainian socionist S. Savchenko. He showed that a mutual correspondence can be established between the court cards of the Tarot and the TIMs. Here I merely wish to draw attention to the configuration of the court cards as applied to the _periodic system of types of information metabolism_ (PSTIM), examined in detail in Chapter 15.

In accordance with the theorem on the strict ordering of the socion, the court cards arranged themselves so that the Knights fell into the first group, the Queens into the second, the Kings into the third and the Pages into the fourth. It turned out that the semantics of the court cards corresponds in a surprising way to the semantics of the typological personifications. Don Quixote is the Knight of Knights, or of Swords; Hamlet the Knight of Kings, or of Wands; London the Knight of Pages, or of Coins (Pentacles); and Huxley (Don Juan) the Knight of Queens. Dumas is the Queen of Queens, Gorky the Queen of Coins, Dreiser the Queen of Kings, and Gabin the Queen of Swords. Hugo is the King of Kings, Zhukov the King of Knights, Napoleon the King of Queens, Stierlitz the King of Coins. Robespierre is the Page of Coins, Yesenin the Page of Queens, Balzac the Page of Knights, and Dostoyevsky the Page of Kings. The correspondences obtained shed additional light on the semantics of the socionic types. Thanks are due to Sergey Savchenko for having drawn attention to this remarkable psychological achievement of those who tried to make sense of the intricacies of human character many hundreds of years ago, at the dawn of human civilisation. The presence of such semantic correspondences makes it possible to apply the corresponding psychological observations of the ancients to the modern description of the types.

The suits of the cards may be expressed either through their attributes (Wands, Swords, Cups, Coins) or through their ranks — Kings (Wands), Knights (Swords), Queens (Cups), Pages (Coins). If numbers are assigned to the suits by seniority (K=4, Kn=3, Q=2, P=1), then the socionic square turns out to be _perfect_ with respect to the arrangement of suits, since in it not only the sums of the columns and rows are equal, but the sums of all the diagonal directions are equal to the same value (10). It remains unclear, however, what follows from this.

An interesting semantic correspondence is that the Knights are dual to the Queens of their quadra, and the Kings to the Pages. The sums corresponding to the suits of duals are constant and equal to 5. The Knights, expressing the spirit of the quadra to the maximum degree, turn out to be at the head of their quadra, while the Kings are surrounded by their retinue.

## Appendix 6. Varieties of tests that could be used as auxiliaries in typing

Let us note that at present, until a full-fledged socionic test has been created, a whole battery of tests, methods and approaches may be used in the diagnostics of TIM.

1. **Ability tests.** Some tests originally created for the investigation of intellectual functions, knowledge and abilities correlate highly with personality factors — for example indices of speech fluency and motor rigidity, which correlate highly with factor U.I. (G) 23 (neurotic regression — mobilisation of energy).
    
2. **Tests of skills and proficiencies.** This group includes tests of visual-motor coordination, coordination of hand movements, precision, maze-running and the like.
    
3. **Perception tests.** This group covers a wide range of tests: from visual perception (completion of unfinished images) to olfactory perception (preference for odours).
    
4. **Questionnaires.** This is a group of tests constructed in the form of a survey about behaviour, tastes, habits and so on — for example questionnaires about state of health, observance of hygienic requirements, and so forth.
    
5. **Opinions.** To this group belong tests eliciting the subject's attitudes towards other people, norms of behaviour and morality, political views, and so on.
    
6. **Aesthetic tests.** This is a group of tests on preferences for musical works, paintings, drawings, poets, artists and the like.
    
7. **Projective tests**, or more strictly, tests of errors of perception. Here belong all kinds of formalised projective personality tests.
    
8. **Situational tests.** These presuppose the creation of a definite social situation. For example, one and the same task is performed alone and in front of the whole class, for individual credit and for team credit, in a situation of competition or of cooperation, and so on.
    
9. **Games.** In game situations the individual peculiarities of the subject's personality show themselves well. For this reason many games have been included among the objective tests.
    
10. **Physiological tests.** To these belong tests in which physiological indices are recorded: GSR, ECG, EEG, tremor and the like.
    
11. **Physical tests.** These are not always easy to distinguish from the physiological. Chest measurement, specific body weight, muscle turgor, the thickness of the fat fold and other indices should be assigned to the physical tests.
    
12. **Incidental observations.** These may be, for example, the number of indeterminate answers, the number of marks on the test form, neatness of handwriting, fidgeting, restlessness during the examination and other manifestations of the respondent.
    
13. **Menu-tests in a computer version:** a choice is made from a proposed set, and the quality of the choice is correlated with the type.
    
14. **Ordering tests:** these allow a person to reject nothing, but merely to distribute items by rank. After all, a person has everything in them.
    
15. **Paired-comparison tests.** Pairs of concepts, objects or situations are proposed. In each pair a ranking choice must be made. Diagnosis is carried out on the basis of the totality of all the paired choices.
    
16. **Unfinished-sentence test.** After the beginning of a phrase, a menu of choices may be offered.
    

- I would like to try myself in the field of…
- I like to read books on the subject of…
- In my free time I…
- My style of dress I would describe as…
- and so on.

17. **A pictorial thematic socionic test.** By analogy with the well-known Thematic Apperception Test (TAT), this may consist of a set of pictures presenting socionically significant situations.
    
18. **The "dancing men" test**, where instead of concepts one must choose between two schematic human figures representing aspectually significant postures and gestures.
    
19. **Concrete everyday and professional knowledge, skills, habits and abilities** which directly form the concrete actions aimed at achieving a practical result.
    
20. **Abstract fractal tests** (proposed by N. Prilepskaya). Presumably the TIM should be sensitive to the choice of typologically valid fractal structures. Deep investigations are required here.
    
21. **A test of colour-rhythmic structures** (proposed by Ya. Bondarenko). Ya. Bondarenko, a talented psychologist from Donetsk, advanced the hypothesis that at every moment in time conditions arise in the world, in accordance with the changing vibrational background, for the formation of structures corresponding to the character of the cosmic vibrations. He holds that a child's TIM is formed in the mother's womb under the influence of the corresponding cosmic vibrations, and that the adult bearer of the TIM will be sensitive to the corresponding colour and form.
    

## Appendix 7. A database of differentiating statements

Many years of testing experience — both my own and my observation of how other socionists do it — allow me to advance the supposition that the use of the questionnaires currently available serves to a considerable degree as a background and a cover for direct observation of the appearance and behaviour of the person being tested. Moreover, the expert often does not fully account for this to himself, believing consciously or subconsciously that he draws at least the basic information about the respondent's TIM precisely from the answers to the questions put to him.

In his famous book _Accentuated Personalities_, K. Leonhard says that firm rules on the basis of which a psychological type could be diagnosed unfortunately do not exist. And the celebrated psychologist continues: this is bound up with the fact that, when questions are put to a subject, one has to establish individually each time how the subject understands the question, since without that his answer cannot be correctly interpreted. And since socionic testing makes no provision for a procedure verifying the respondent's understanding of the question, his answer too takes on a probabilistic and hypothetical character.

Leonhard concludes that a subject's theoretical assertions can serve only for general orientation; proof of traits of character must come from concrete descriptions of situations in which the peculiarities of behaviour manifest themselves. He wishes to stress that this is almost the decisive point in the methodology of personality analysis [7].

Now, after the discovery of socionics, it can be said that we have come considerably closer to the formulation of firm rules on the basis of which a socionic type might be diagnosed; and we hope that psychology will make use of socionics' findings for the solution of its own theoretical and practical tasks.

In this appendix, within the framework of the problem of socionic diagnostics, I have collected several "test" sentences — blanks of a sort — and if interested parties are found, I am ready to continue the further joint elaboration of the corresponding test forms.
