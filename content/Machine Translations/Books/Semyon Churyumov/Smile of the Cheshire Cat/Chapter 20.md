## Instead of a Conclusion. SOCIONICS AS METHODOLOGY

> _Scientific methodology is a system of logical and heuristic means by which a scientist processes empirical data and forms a theory of the object of his research._

That socionics possesses methodological potential with respect to a number of humanities disciplines became clear once its concepts began to be used effectively in sociologists' recommendations to victims of the Chernobyl disaster, in the formation of working groups in business and in cosmonautics, in literary studies for the analysis of works of fiction, and in other subject areas — engineering, for example! There remains, however, the question of how universal the methodological potential of socionics is, and what the limits of its methodological competence are.

From its emergence around 1968, socionics was never integrated into the official science of the Soviet Union. Soviet ideology rejected Jung as an "accomplice" of the Nazis, and his works could be obtained from the library's restricted holdings only by special permission, granted to those who directed their efforts at the struggle against "bourgeois ideology." One could be given a prison term for reading Jung without authorisation. Yet people did possess Jung's principal work for our purposes, _Psychological Types_, and through acquaintances and in deep secrecy it could be obtained. One way or another, around 1975 I managed to read this book. Jung's typology made a strong impression on me, above all by its logic and persuasiveness. I had managed to read Freud earlier, but his ideas were oriented towards the clinic rather than towards normal everyday use, and in this they lost out. Although for us — "pre-perestroika people" — both were fresh and exciting. I enthusiastically retold to my acquaintances whatever I had managed to absorb from these two great psychologists; but now, knowing socionics and everything one can possibly know about typologies and contemporary psychology, I realise how much naivety there was in those accounts.

In 1986 Aushra Augustinavichute came to Kyiv for the second time at the invitation of progressive Kyiv psychologists (above all S. A. Bakhtiyarova), and I was lucky enough to get to a meeting with her. Aushra's lectures revolutionised my conceptions of human typology. That revolution had, admittedly, already been prepared by my acquaintance with the works of Freud and Jung.

Soon "perestroika" began, the harsh ideological control over "reliability" was lifted, and we began to acquaint ourselves with world achievements in psychology. It "turned out" that Jungian typology — or rather, a typology based on Jung's ideas — was widely disseminated in America; but by then, knowing socionics, we could see its deficiencies clearly. And despite the fact that official psychology gradually began to acknowledge Jung and even to take his position into account in his opposition to Freud, it refused to accept and recognise socionics as an independent and fully-fledged typological development, although the connection with and continuity from Jung are obvious here. This can be explained in part by the fact that a struggle of scientific schools exists in science; but the deeper cause is that the bearers of the official scientific paradigm consciously or subconsciously sense that socionics has a different methodological foundation, and for them that is a "limit thou shalt not pass." Not the least role here is played by the deep bureaucratic corruption of post-Soviet science both in Russia and, especially, in the countries of the "near abroad." In Ukraine the corrupt falsification of science has flowered luxuriantly, since it has now become altogether easy to acquire any dissertations and academic titles for money and through connections. This still does not mean that among the dissertations bought for money there are no real scientific achievements — it is simply that without a bribe they would not have been passed.

A somewhat contradictory situation arises: socionics in its constructions makes wide use of the methods of other sciences — systems theory (V. Yermak), structural combinatorics (V. Gulenko), geometrical configurations (G. Shulman, S. Churyumov and others), thermodynamic and quantum-mechanical analogies (Aushra Augustinavichute, A. Bukalov) and so on — and thereby demonstrates its methodological tolerance; yet other sciences, and above all psychology, deny socionics scientific status. Apparently the representatives of other sciences subconsciously feel that, in recognising socionics, they would also have to recognise the methodology standing behind it, and that might entail the necessity of revising many settled conceptions.

And although at the level of the subconscious such fears undoubtedly have the force of a categorical imperative, at the level of common sense they are infantile in character. The methodology underlying socionics has just as much right to exist as any other.

## The Search for Invariants and Symmetry

In classical mechanics, as in quantum mechanics, the following assertion holds: if a certain state is possible, then the mirror reflection of that state is also possible. This assertion is a priori, since it follows from the properties of the corresponding mathematical equations and not from physical experiments. A priori valid conclusions can also be drawn from other mathematical truths. For example, if a set of objects, phenomena or processes is discovered in reality which can be evaluated as an algebraic group, then one should expect from that set a phenomenology following from the properties of the corresponding group. This phenomenology may turn out to be extremely generalised, but it exists, and its existence follows from the properties of the mathematical category corresponding to it. In elementary particle physics, at any rate, this principle works. The discoveries of socionics allow us to suppose that this principle manifests itself in psychology, biology, sociology and other humanities. Incidentally, this principle can serve as the theoretical foundation of methodological structuralism.

Therefore, if energo-informational fields (or several fields) connected with the psyche exist, and if within them there are objects forming an algebraic group or some other mathematical structure, then from the totality of those objects one may expect a phenomenology following from the properties of the corresponding mathematical structure. And this means that substantial conclusions about such structures could be obtained as the result of calculations. It was in precisely this way that Aushra obtained the primary descriptions of the ARP.<sup>[1]</sup>

Between the structures of the TIMs there is the symmetry of the rotation group of the cube and its mirror reflections. Therefore, in particular, A. Afanasyev's constructions in his _Syntax of Love_ (see the chapter on the inevitability of methodology), unquestionably beautiful but not corresponding to the group transformations, may be assessed as a specific distortion of the truth — in essence they represent a poeticised profanation of socionic constructions. Their effectiveness (but not their truth!) is explained in the same way as the effectiveness of such constructions as the MMPI and Leonhard's accentuations: by the overlapping of the semantic fields of the individual categories. The ten categories of the MMPI, and following it of the SMIL, prove sufficient for assessing psychic states, giving recommendations and predicting changes in the personal space only because their semantic fields — which the ARP (the Augustinavichute–Reinin traits) structure in more detail — cover the entire semantic field of the TIMs. In Afanasyev's case, besides the overlapping of the semantic fields of the categories, a degenerate variant of the principle of fractality must also be taken into account: it is expressed in the fact that the distribution of functional accentuations within the structure of a TIM may be any whatsoever, while at the same time those variants which correspond to the group of cubic rotations and mirror reflections will be real, that is, will manifest themselves in the characters of particular people.

## The Principle of Fractality

This principle was proposed by A. V. Bukalov as an explanatory basis for the fact that structurally similar formations exist which thematically pertain to the psyche, to psychology and to sociology. Some aspects of this principle were examined in the chapter on diversity within the TIM.

Let us consider the principle of fractality (PF) in more detail. This principle manifests itself in the structural similarity of the whole and its parts. Another expression of it is the hierarchy of homologous structures. The principle is widely used in various spiritual teachings of the East. It can also be discovered in the Christian religion's assertion that God created man in His own image and likeness. Yet, strange as it may seem, European science before the 21st century was unable to formulate and trace this principle clearly either in biology, say, or in such humanities disciplines as psychology and sociology.

In Chinese medicine, however, it has long been known that the human body as a whole is projected onto its separate parts — onto the palms of the hands, onto the soles of the feet, onto the auricle of the ear, onto the iris of the eye (see Figs. 20.01, 20.02, 20.03) — which is precisely a manifestation of this universal principle in anatomical physiology. European medicine has the notion of the Zakharyin–Head reflex-projection zones, but this result is regarded as no more than an empirical observation — interesting, practically useful, but not rising to the level of a universal principle. In Chinese medicine, by contrast, the fractal character of the structures of the human body is traced at all levels of biological organisation. Not only is the whole body projected onto the palm or the sole, but it is further projected onto each finger, so that, for example, the terminal phalanx of each finger corresponds to the head.

The Chinese discovered energy meridians on the human body, the whole set of which represents a fractal substructure of the human organism. Each meridian corresponds to a definite anatomical and physiological system. Accordingly, there are meridians of the heart, the lungs, the liver and so on. But, entirely in accordance with the principle of fractality, on each meridian both all the organs and all the meridians are represented.

It certainly does not belong to the class of well-known symmetries such as axial or central symmetry. There are other frequently encountered symmetries, but their names are not so widely known — these are compatible-equal symmetry, or metamery, corresponding to the "coordinate" arrangement of floor tiles; and antisymmetry, where two figures are mirror-symmetrical but, for example, coloured in opposite colours. But there exist symmetries which few besides mathematicians would recognise as such. To this class belong all the functional symmetries defined by arbitrary mathematical functions. After all, the literal translation of the word _symmetry_ is _co-measurement_. Among the functional symmetries there is a subset of screw (helical) symmetries. They are distinguished by the fact that the elements of the initial set, along with a rotation through some angle, are additionally subjected to some further transformation. In this configuration the initial foursome is rotated through 90°, and in doing so each of its elements is regularly transformed into a new element of the rotated foursome. However, after the fourth transformation we return to the initial position. Of course, in ordinary screw motion this does not happen — you do not return to the initial position, but you find yourself exactly above it. Nevertheless, if the vertical component of the screw transformation is replaced by a transformation of the elements, then we obtain a screw transformation in which a return to the initial position is possible. The subtlety here, however, is that although we have returned to the initial position of the initial elements, the elements themselves, while apparently remaining the same, have nonetheless changed internally, since they contain within themselves the history of their transformation. Of course, purely mathematically this is not difficult to describe, but it is still interesting that the socion possesses such a not entirely ordinary, though fairly simple, symmetry.

Incidentally, in examining the square configuration presented above one may notice in it a manifestation of the principle of fractality: the socion divides into four quadras, each of which in turn divides into four TIMs. But let us not forget that each TIM constitutes a system consisting of four functional blocks. And, as was shown in the chapter on modelling the psyche, a further internal structural articulation of the functions is also possible.

One of the subtler manifestations of the PF is that functions can work in different thematic regimes. These regimes may be designated as generalised worldview schemas. I first heard of this — in a different terminology, admittedly — in 1987 from I. N. Kalinauskas, a remarkable director, psychologist and true Master. I personally consider him my Teacher, regardless of whether he considers me his pupil. In group trainings he used the concept of _temporhythm_, with which I was familiar from the works of K. S. Stanislavsky. However, what Kalinauskas proposed differed both structurally and substantively from what Stanislavsky worked with.

_Temporhythm_ is probably a very correct and precise term, but Kalinauskas's temporhythms may also be called generalised worldview forms. What is meant is four generalised characteristics of the perception of the external environment: 1) perception of the environment as a wholeness; 2) perception of the environment as a living and magical connectedness; 3) perception of the environment as a rigidly determined machine; 4) perception of the environment as a disintegrating and degrading chaos. Kalinauskas called these generalised forms, respectively, rhythm A, rhythm B, rhythm C and rhythm D. I first heard these categories in 1987 from Kalinauskas and associate them with his name. Of course, this foursome forms a rhythmic, or generalised-worldview, fractal.

As observations within the socionic paradigm show, the generalised psychic functions tune themselves to the perception of the world in accordance with one of the four forms indicated. It appears that such tuning may arise both as a result of upbringing or education and as a result of the uncritical assimilation of programmes circulating in society. Of course, this requires deep study.

It is clear that if this rhythmic fractal is superimposed on the functional field of the psyche, the possibilities for describing human phenomenology increase significantly. This fractal may also be called generalised-worldview, since it sets the most general contours of the personality's relation to the world. It is plausible that this fractal is not innate.

Closely related subject matter was developed by the remarkable researcher of depth psychology E. Litrovnik. He too speaks of four rhythms A, B, C and D, which may be linked with the work of each of the functions, but he invests them with a somewhat different sense. Rhythm A, according to Litrovnik, is roughly like the vision of an object from two or three sides, something like a three-dimensional drawing. Rhythm B is like a linear scale: more–less, drier–wetter, sweeter–sourer, and so on. Rhythm C is something like two-dimensional graphics: one can take into account branchings, intersections, limitations by area. Rhythm D he calls variable volume. It is not easy to understand what this means, but from his explanations it becomes clear that it resembles the ability to foresee non-obvious consequences. What is important is that, according to Litrovnik, each function, independently of the others, can work in one of these rhythms, which affects its specific productivity. This is a good theme for experimental research.

A. V. Bukalov approaches the problem of the differing productivity of the functions somewhat differently. He sees the solution to this problem in the fact that functions have different dimensionality: from 1 to 4. Moreover, according to Bukalov, dimensionality is rigidly attached to each function: the first and seventh functions (numbering after Gulenko) are four-dimensional, the second and eighth three-dimensional, the third and sixth two-dimensional, and the fourth and fifth one-dimensional. A four-dimensional function works at the level of clairvoyance, when the subject moves freely in the conceptual space of the object. A three-dimensional function makes it possible to manipulate the object freely, adapting it to current needs. A two-dimensional function allows one to distinguish certain parameters of the object quite confidently. A one-dimensional function works in a regime of rejection, non-acceptance, avoidance, denial of the object's subject matter, or of distancing from it. A. V. Bukalov also has a more detailed explication of the concept of dimensionality. He holds that a four-dimensional function processes the information flow by the parameters of time, situations, norms and selfhood. A three-dimensional function deals only with the last three parameters. A two-dimensional function deals only with norms and selfhood, and a one-dimensional one only with selfhood. It is interesting to consider how an experiment might be set up to verify the reality of these concepts.

On closer examination of the three proposed interpretations of functional modalities, one can see that their semantic fields have substantial intersections. In all three cases we see a hierarchy of functions by the criterion of the coverage of the object's semantics. The differences concern the details of how this coverage is accomplished. What has been said gives grounds for supposing that behind the three approaches considered to the assessment of the productivity of the functions there stands one and the same archetype. However, none of the explanations cited relies on the intrinsic nature of the socionic functions; each imposes on the function some external schema — which is entirely justified empirically, since quantitative differences in the work of the functions are indeed observed.

Let us try to assess the quantitative differences in the work of the functions as a function of their positional semantics. As was shown earlier, positional semantics is determined by the IA standing in the corresponding function in the model of Don Quixote, for whom, by virtue of the peculiarities of his model, the positional and aspectual symbols coincide. And since the positional semantics is the same for all types, all the features of the functions that depend on their position are identical for all types. Let us recall that the positional semantics of the PPF and the IVF is determined by I\i, that of the KZF and the RTF by L\l, that of the ANF and the ERF by S\s, and that of the KKF and the MMF by E\e.<sup>[2]</sup> Proceeding from this, let us assess the generalised quantitative productivity of the functions.

**I\i** — intuitive penetration into the essence of the object, deep understanding of its potential, free expansion of adjacent subject matter, the generation of enriched and improved images corresponding to the subject matter of the function's IA.

**L\l** — logical elaboration, technological execution, structuring of the object.

**S\s** — sensory perception, registration of the actual state of affairs, examination of the object from different sides.

**E\e** — emotional reaction, fixation of an attitude depending on the subjective set.

In these explications one can see gradations in the degree of freedom with which the function handles its material. Of course, quantitative productivity should not be confused here with the possible diversity of object forms within the framework of one or another IA. And nevertheless intuition, operating in the ideal plane, is presumably the freest in creating ideas, images and projects. Logic and technology are more limited, since they process an already given object or materialise a concrete variant of it; however, they are free in structural combinatorics. Sensorics merely perceives, registers or resists the object at hand, but it is nevertheless free to observe it from various points of view and in various modalities. As for emotionality, being limited by internal subjective sets, it merely reacts to the object or situation on the pattern acceptable–unacceptable. Thus, by the criterion of quantitative productivity, the IAs are arranged in the following order: **I\i > L\l > S\s > E\e**. If this is so, then the differing quantitative productivity of the functions observed in practice becomes comprehensible and socionically grounded. Of course, all the reasoning given above stands in need of deeper research, but as an initial position it is entirely plausible.

## Quantum-Mechanical Analogies and the Field Paradigm

Speaking of quantum-mechanical analogies: is it permissible in psychology to use concepts borrowed from other sciences? Well, presumably at the level of analogy or metaphor there is no sedition in this. However, I think that in the case of thermodynamic and quantum-mechanical concepts the matter stands more subtly and more fundamentally. Unfortunately, science has to date gathered too little empirical material concerning the energetic substrate of the psyche. Science has yet to discover this substrate, and here great tasks face the experimenters. Therefore at present it cannot be demonstratively asserted that psychic processes have precisely a quantum-mechanical character. But to suppose this and to develop the corresponding theoretical conceptions is entirely permissible, since the integral experience of humanity does not contradict such conceptions.

If we allow that at the basis of psychic processes there lies a certain energo-informational field (Volkov), then much in the psyche and in psychology receives a full-fledged grounding. In particular, the qualitative thermodynamic reasoning partly used by Aushra Augustinavichute in her attempt to substantiate Model "A" acquires sense. The hypothesis of an energo-informational field sheds light on the possibility of dichotomous quantisation, that is, of the polarisation of parts of the energo-informational substrate, in which the well-known qualities, processes and states of psychology are counterposed to one another. Psychology has accumulated a truly extensive body of material which literally begs for a quantum-mechanical and thermodynamic explanation. Incidentally, R. Wilson's book _Quantum Psychology_, which appeared in 1998, contains almost nothing "quantum" in it, and its title is no more than a play on words — although the book itself, irrespective of the idea of the quantisation of energo-informational fields, entirely deserves attention and contains valuable information for the education of the young. The theoretical challenge contained in the energetic component of the brain's work, with its alpha, beta, theta and other waves, so far remains without a "quantum" answer. Nor are there qualitative theoretical generalisations for such a striking empirical phenomenon as the Chinese meridians. And what about the polar states of consciousness (sleep–waking), the change of moods (joy–sorrow), the polarity of the psychic functions (sensorics–intuition, thinking–feeling), the polarities of temperaments, the polarities of personality characteristics — and what else besides might be found in psychology where polar structures declare themselves openly. The theme of polar structures is encountered in the works of many psychologists, but one of its best presentations can be seen in V. Levi's bestseller _The Art of Being Yourself_, in the chapter on the pendulums within us; in V. Petukhov's dissertation; and in the remarkable book by S. Bakhtiyarova [13], where the polar transitions of different psychic and psychological modalities are investigated.

In Western psychology such structures are partly given shape by structuralist approaches, but these are no more than conceptual packaging, not fundamental solutions. So, gentlemen experimenters, the matter rests with you! Prove the existence of energo-informational fields, or at least of something analogous — it is not obligatory to keep the name — and psychology will reach fundamentally new frontiers.

The reasoning given above is also applicable to what may be called fractal quantisation — the formation of psychological structures nested within one another many times over. But of course fractal quantisation is by no means limited to psychology; it permeates all the processes, phenomena and conglomerates of the cosmos. Such a phenomenon, if it were proved, could explain not only the formation of the polar-symmetrical structure of the psyche, but also many social processes and objects.

## The Methodological Potential of Socionics

Socionics is new knowledge, and it is natural that people should try to find ever new applications for this knowledge. This process is analogous to aromorphosis in the evolution of biological species and technical systems, but the range of adequacy in the application of new knowledge here may be very wide: from deep, essential, heuristic elaborations with an effective output into practice, into everyday life and activity, down to superficial metaphors possessing at best only aesthetic value, and at worst creating new delusions.

Simply postulating the possibility of applying socionics in some field of knowledge or activity at the level of "brilliant guesses" is, at the present stage of this science's development, no longer sufficient. What is required is research, proofs, comparative analysis of facts and similar scientific processing of the material of the corresponding field. Such work is being actively conducted by the staff of the International Institute of Socionics. As an example I shall cite just one work by the IIS staff, the Kyiv socionists O. G. Lemeshev and Yu. V. Lemesheva, "Notes on the Danger of Radioactive Damage," in which socionic methodology connected with pattern recognition is applied to such a concrete phenomenon as radioactive danger. As a result of socionic analysis, what happened at Chernobyl was recognised as an object characterised by intuitive-ethical introversion. And this is sufficient grounds for giving socionic recommendations to the relevant services and to the victims.

That socionics is becoming a powerful methodological instrument in the study of social phenomena can be seen from the remarkable articles by A. V. Bukalov and his colleagues [3, 8, 9, 10, 11 and others]. Article [3] gives examples of the predictive power of socionic methods, which make it possible to forecast, for instance, the development of political events with high accuracy.

Socionics is used as a methodology for solving psychological problems and problems of information processing, in sociological research, for analysis and recommendations in business and economics, in history, ethnography, pedagogy, medicine, linguistics, in literary, artistic, musical and other kinds of creative work, and in the analysis of religious, ideological and philosophical problems. Such a list would do honour to any science, but alas, neither philosophy, nor psychology, nor any other humanities discipline has so wide a range.

One may also assert that socionics can itself act as a methodology. This means that its propositions may become normative in social, historical, literary, linguistic, psychological and other research, as is clearly seen in the example of the works of scholars who profess socionics: A. V. Bukalov (the law of quadra succession in history and politics), V. V. Gulenko (the fine structure of evolutionary processes and group management), A. V. Prokhorova (analysis of the relations of characters in literary works, linguistic analysis of texts), and others.

The extraction of the methodological paradigm underlying socionics leads to strikingly simple and convincing initial assertions. The fundamental concept of the methodological paradigm of socionics is the notion of information metabolism, which may be decomposed into the notion of information and that of metabolism (exchange). The next axiomatic level is the repeated dichotomy of the information flow, which leads to a simple categorial basis satisfying the requirements of completeness, independence, consistency and minimality. Since the plausibility of this basis has a natural and obvious character, the theoretical constructions founded upon it are distinguished by high persuasiveness and can unquestionably be used for practical social technologies.

Let us consider in somewhat more detail the arguments in favour of the plausibility of the methodological basis of socionics. First of all, the very concept of plausibility. This is the only thing to which a scientific theory can lay claim in the modern world. To prove the absolute character of one's theoretical constructions, as two and a half millennia of the development of European philosophy and science have shown, is beyond anyone. Plausibility, for its part, is ensured by the presence of several factors. Above all it has an intuitive character — the scientist intuitively trusts, believes in, or inclines towards accepting certain assertions, constructions, hypotheses. But intuition must have sufficient grounds. Science has so far not formulated a criterion for what makes grounds sufficient: the same facts, assertions and suppositions may prove convincing for one person and not for another.

Only the entire totality of methodological factors makes plausibility well-founded. Experiment (the sensory factor), the internal connectedness of theoretical constructions (the logical factor), the external consistency of the material (the ethical factor) — all of this is necessary; but in any case the last word in the recognition of plausibility and its degree remains with intuition.

How, then, do the assertions of socionics look from this point of view? The fundamental significance of information metabolism — that is, the high degree to which personal phenomenology and social processes are determined by the peculiarities of the circulation of information in society and in the human psyche — is becoming ever more obvious, and correspondingly ever more plausible, to the majority of scholars working in the humanities. This leads, in particular, to an increase in the number of articles and papers in which questions connected with the role of information in psychological and social processes are raised to one degree or another, although such works often do not presuppose a knowledge of socionics. In such cases one may speak of a spontaneous rediscovery of what has already been discovered in socionics. Alas, such rediscoveries often have an intuitive-hypothetical character and do not rise to the level of full-fledged theoretical constructs.

The conception of information flows introduced in socionics can be convincingly presented in deductive form (see the chapter on the semantics of information aspects), and this, at least on the logical side, removes the problem of the completeness and consistency of the set of IAs. In any case this theme deserves full-fledged academic discussion rather than the silencing to which socionics is subjected in official science.

If, even simply on the basis of intuitive plausibility, one adds the axiomatic conception of eight functions which, depending on the TIM, may be linked with different information aspects, then the axiomatic basis of socionics may be considered complete. Of course, this is not obligatory. Theorists may, if they wish, work on this more thoroughly. Thanks to the simplicity of its axiomatic basis, socionics is capable of drawing conclusions distinguished by high universality. In particular, socionics makes it possible to give a complete classification of all the methods used in science.

In accordance with the socionic paradigm, all methods used in science and technology may be divided into intuitive, logical, sensory and ethical. Their extraverted and introverted variants may then be added. Possible combinations of these basic components must also be taken into account. Such a classification, entirely natural for socionics, may prove unfamiliar from the point of view of the paradigm established in science and technology. In that paradigm only sensory (observation, experiment, etc.) and logical (classification, deduction, proof, etc.) methods have their lawful place. Intuitive methods (induction, discovery, insight, invention and others), although they constitute the very essence of scientific creativity, are still marginal in character, since they yield with difficulty to logical description, and have only recently become an object of close attention from psychologists. As for ethical methods, such a combination of concepts may not be perceived at all. Generally speaking, ethics in science is spoken of, but as a kind of general moral requirement — that it would be right, that it would not be bad, to adhere to ethical norms in science; yet such a wish is far from corresponding to the real state of affairs. Socionics explains this by the fact that the TIM — the code — of science is intuitive-logical extraversion (ILE), which means that in its socionic model ethics is in a weak position. And from this, to put it mildly, follows the modest position of ethics in science.

## Rhythmic Laws

The various entities which appear from time to time in our world, having different terms of existence, display different evolutionary phenomenology. But common to all evolutionary processes are at least three elements: emergence, flourishing, extinction. In each concrete case a finer spectrum of phases of development can also be discovered. Different researchers have structured the processes of evolution differently. In K. Marx, for instance, the historical process is structured by the succession of socio-political formations, behind which stands the succession of relations of production, which in turn is determined by gradual changes in the means of production. In accordance with the theory of this remarkable philosopher one may distinguish six such formations: primitive communism, slaveholding, feudalism, capitalism, socialism, scientific communism. If socialism and communism are combined as phases of a single formation characterised by the socialisation of the means of production, five formations remain. One may also combine slaveholding and feudalism, since they can be treated as variants of the mass enslavement of the majority by a social minority, and moreover not all ethnoses had both of these formations. The Eastern Slavs, for example, passed straight from the primitive-communal order to feudalism with the gradual enslavement of some people by others. If these combinations are made, only four formations remain, which correspond entirely to the law of quadra succession proposed by A. V. Bukalov and V. V. Gulenko. If we recall Plato's theory of the state, there the state structure passes through four phases. These phases are by no means formal, since behind each of them stands a quite definite social mechanism. In the first phase, as Plato held, the state arises as the result of the efforts of sages, and at its foundation stands the power of law. But the state has to be defended, and then the military come to the fore, and the basis of power becomes force. When the state is secure, the accumulation of wealth gradually occurs, and power passes to the plutocrats, that is, to the richest, who have amassed their property by fair means and foul. And when the stratification of society by the criterion of wealth and poverty reaches a certain critical maximum, the people rise up and take power into their own hands — this is what Plato calls democracy. Despite the simplified character of Plato's schema, it describes the social process quite adequately at the level of almost ultimate theoretical generalisation. From the point of view of socionics, this process fits well into the schema of the law of quadra succession (A. V. Bukalov, V. V. Gulenko). Incidentally, this law accords well with the principle of fractality — the four generalised phases of the evolution of society form an evolutionary fractal.

The first quadra, headed by an intuitive-logical extravert, some sage Don Quixote, puts forward ideas concerning a just ordering of society and, to a first approximation, creates it. In this society an enlightened monarch rules and monarchical liberalism reigns.

But to some it seems that this society is a little dull, and that something more could be achieved. The ideologist of new, spiritual prospects turns out to be an ethical-intuitive extravert, some Hamlet, who paints before the mind's eye of his supporters astonishing prospects filled with spiritual feats — but for this one must close ranks and, sparing neither one's own strength nor that of others, march in orderly rows into the struggle for the new ideals and defeat one's enemies, since "if they do not surrender, they are destroyed." As a result a harsh dictatorship is established, under which an enormous number of dissenters find themselves under harsh pressure, up to and including dungeons and concentration camps. Many recall the old days when life was easy and free. So what if you paid a tithe — there was everything, and how merrily one lived, how many carnivals there were, and there was something in that for the sake of which it was worth living.

This may go on for a long time, but not for ever. New, pragmatically minded people appear, whom ideological uniformity and collective conformism do not satisfy, and who want individual freedom. And then a logical-intuitive extravert, some Jack London, points out such a possibility. He says that everyone can secure his own well-being himself; one need only try, work tirelessly, discover some Klondike, wash gold there and invest it in some profitable enterprise. Many do just that, and many succeed. But not all. Those who did not succeed now work for those who did. But they too have a chance, because they are free people: if they want, they work for this successful entrepreneur; if they want, for another. In this society individual freedom reigns, everyone is for himself, and nobody cares about anyone else. "What do I care about you all, and you about me," sings the hero of the film _The Last Inch_, based on the novel by James Aldridge. Many live for their own pleasure, and many for their own displeasure. Some recall the old days when things were hard but everyone was together, they felt each other's elbow, there was a goal ahead, and there was something in that for the sake of which it was worth living...

This may go on for a long time, but not for ever. People appear who understand the inhumanity of individual freedom. And then an intuitive-logical extravert,<sup>[3]</sup> some Huxley or Dostoyevsky, says that one must think not only of oneself but of other people, that people are, in the end, the most important thing in this world. It must be arranged so that all people can live with dignity, not only the most adroit, the most brazen and the most everything. Considerable wealth has been accumulated in society; technologies have been created such that one person can feed a thousand. He simply needs comfortable conditions, a well-arranged service sector, and every kind of entertainment made available, so that work does not seem hellish to him — and this is exactly what the thousand people he feeds will occupy themselves with. It seems that now everything is fine. Everyone is fed, clothed and shod, and there is so much entertainment that it begins to nauseate. Many seek oblivion in narcotics and other forms of comfortable suicide. Many recall the old days when things were hard but everything depended on you, and you felt yourself a human being, you achieved everything with your own hands, and there was something in that for the sake of which it was worth living.

And then an intuitive-logical extravert, some Don Quixote... And it all begins again. This is a very generalised and simplified schema, but on its basis ever more complex and subtle transformations and changes unfold in society. This is one of the first fractal harmonics of any social process.

## The Concept of Quantisation

The principle of quantisation may be formulated thus: _any entities in this world are the result of the quantisation of a primary field or its derivatives._ From this point of view, all polarised structures may be regarded as the result of the dichotomous quantisation of the substrate corresponding to them. In such quantisation the parts, components or layers of the substrate find themselves in a relation of opposition, but the connection between them is not broken. This is a good physical interpretation of the famous dialectical law of the unity of opposites, but it may also be considered its grounding.

What, then, is quantisation? This concept appeared in physics (Bohr) as the basis for explaining the discrete transitions of electrons between energy orbits without the possibility of a continuous transition between them. Subsequently this concept was extended to the mechanisms of the formation of elementary particles within a field. These particles, in another terminology, are regarded as resonances, or vibrational complications, of the initial substrate. Thus it became possible to speak, for example, of the photon as a quantum or resonance of the electromagnetic field. Analogous terminology is applied to other elementary objects as well. Generalising this physical concept and extending it to any natural phenomena, one may define quantisation as the spontaneous or self-generated configuring of a concrete field. It appears that the scenarios of such configuring may be different, and one of the simplest is the dichotomous scenario, in which the primary field substrate is stratified into parts that may be regarded as opposite, as counterposed to one another, or as poles. From the theoretical point of view, what is important is that despite the phenomenological rupture of the parts, they remain parts of a single whole, and the connection between these parts is not broken; but it acquires the character of a specific exchange, whose peculiarities must be determined separately in each subject area.

It is possible that one and the same field substrate is able to be quantised simultaneously according to several scenarios, or to be quantised repeatedly according to one and the same scenario, and also to be quantised in accordance with various combinations of various scenarios — which, incidentally, accords entirely with the principle of fractality. Among other quantisation scenarios one may name, for example, the Fibonacci scenario [15], in which the successive stages of a chain quantum process are related to one another as elements of the Fibonacci series, represented by the sequence of numbers 0, 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8... In this sequence each term, beginning with the third, equals the sum of the two preceding ones. Fibonacci arrived at his series as a result of solving the problem of the breeding of a pair of rabbits, symbolised in this series by the two ones after the zero. Despite the extreme pragmatism of such a problem and its apparent primitiveness, it is in essence a problem about the productive increase of a minimal set under given parameters of growth. It is not difficult to transfer this problem to the problem of the diversity of things and processes in the Cosmos. For this it is enough to pose the question as follows: "If the world is one, then how is the diversity of the things and processes observed in it to be explained?" One of the explanations of this may be precisely that the primary substrate underlying our world possesses the capacity for quantisation, that is, for a specific change of the initial structural configuration, characterised by ultimate simplicity, towards ever more complex forms.

Closely connected with the Fibonacci series is the concept of the "golden section," whose numerical expression is the limit of the ratio of two successive terms of the Fibonacci series. This ratio tends to the number 1.618..., and this specific constant appears repeatedly in various practical applications connected with productive growth [18]. The theme of the "golden section," or "golden proportion," developed out of the simple geometrical problem "of the division of a segment in mean and extreme ratio," apparently formulated by Euclid. Behind the complicated verbal name of this problem there stands a simple geometrical sense: to find the magnitudes of two adjacent segments _a_ and _b_ whose ratio equals the ratio of their sum to the larger of them. Mathematically this is expressed by the formula a/b = (a+b)/a. By introducing an exponent on the right-hand side, this formula is easily generalised into an infinite series of formulas of productive growth: a/b = ((a+b)/a)^n [18]. This formula allows one to speak of scenarios of the "golden section," a particular case of which, for n = 1, is examined in the work of Yu. N. Kovalev [16]. It is not hard to see that the limiting scenario in this set, at n = 0, will be precisely dichotomous quantisation, since in this case the primary substrate will be divided in half: a : b = 1, that is, the equality a = b will hold. Of course, here we are dealing with an abstract division into two equal halves; but, as follows from various philosophical considerations, a division into polar, antisymmetrical halves is possible — halves which are equal in their opposition, which preserve their unity through the initial substrate, but which are nonetheless counterposed to one another as qualitatively different opposites. Yet these are opposites whose semantics belongs to a single thematic set. In this sense such polarities are complementary, that is, they complete one another into a single systemic whole.

The general formula of the dichotomous, or polar, scenario is the expression P = 2^n, where the number of poles is successively doubled. This, incidentally, corresponds to the geometrical schema once proposed by the ancient Greek logician Porphyry — the famous "ladder of Porphyry" (see Fig. 20.05). For all the apparent metaphysicality of the reasoning given above, it unambiguously reflects the empirical fact that plant and animal organisms develop precisely according to the dichotomous scenario. One single parental cell divides into two parts, as a result of which two new cells appear. It is obvious that even these first two cells are not identical, since one contains the male and the other the female chromosome, completing themselves into the double helix.

## The Socion

At the level of philosophical and sociological abstraction, the socion is the deep law of the functioning of society. The socion is the abstract structure of society; it is one of the projections of that motor which sets society in motion and controls its pulse. This law could be expressed by a structural model, which would make it sufficiently graphic, but such a model is not so simple to depict. Let us try to touch lightly on a description of this structural model.

In accordance with the socionic paradigm, one ought to speak not of information in general but of informational-field flows. What is called information in cybernetics is merely the introverted-logical flow (in-lg). This flow is launched from the second function of _Il_ structures. When this flow is launched, it immediately begins to suppress the extraverted-intuitive flow (ex-it), blocking the independent generation of information by the _lS_ structures under its control — or rather, forcing this information to move along a definite channel promoted by the _Ei_ structures. Simultaneously the _lI_ structures launch the extraverted-intuitive flow (ex-it) in the opposite direction and suppress the extraverted-intuitive (ex-it) flow of the _Il_ structures under their control. When the _l_-flow of _Il_ reaches _Ei_, since the latter has the corresponding suggestive channel, it in turn launches from its first function an _i_-flow, which is received by _Se_ through its suggestive function, and so on. Analogously, the _I_-flow of _lI_ reaches _sL_, which begins the practical use of the ideas embodied in constructions, thereby launching an _s_-flow, which will be received by _Li_.

Simultaneously, in the opposite direction along each active flow there moves a reactive flow of the demonstration of readiness on the part of the receivers, and a provoking flow on the part of the controlled structures. As a result there arises a central screw antisymmetry (which, incidentally, is also expressed in the group-theoretic structure of the socion), having an adequate graphical projection. One can go further and use the metaphor of screw thrust, created by the screw structure of the information flows within the socion. If all this corresponds to reality, then such thrust is a generalised image of the driving force of social processes.

Since the conception of information flows belongs to the level of high abstractions, their temporal attachment to concrete processes in society may have both a diachronic and a synchronic character. And at the level of real functioning, all these flows and informational impulses may arise simultaneously or in any sequence, creating a complex picture of informational interactions.

## Socionics as a Basis for Teaching Methods

Socionics constitutes an excellent methodological basis on which methods for teaching children and adults may be built, with the aim of forming in them all manner of life conceptions of a practical and instrumental character.

Relying on socionics as a methodological means, all the instrumental skills and abilities which may or must be formed in a child so that he may successfully adapt to life can be classified in accordance with the information aspects adopted in socionics. They may be divided into eight classes: S (extraverted sensorics), s (introverted sensorics), I (extraverted intuition), i (introverted intuition), L (extraverted logic), l (introverted logic), E (extraverted emotioning), e (introverted emotioning).

In accordance with what has been said above and the designations adopted, the psychological qualities of group **S** include: the ability to change a situation in accordance with one's plans and intentions, the ability to withstand the pressure of the external environment, will, personal strength, persistence, tenacity, endurance, the ability to defend one's interests, to carry through what one has begun, to expand the zone of one's influence (expansion), and others.

The psychological qualities of group **s** include: the ability to restore and maintain a positive bodily well-being, to adapt to the surrounding environment using the resources available in it, to organise cosiness, to create comfort and the aesthetic objects connected with it.

The psychological qualities of group **I** include: the ability to perceive intuitively the sense of phenomena, situations, events and texts, to assess the potential possibilities of objects and people, to understand in the broadest sense of the word. The psychological qualities of group **i** include: the ability to perceive an object in development, to orient oneself in temporal categories — in the past (taking experience into account, respect for traditions), in the present (manipulation of time: for example, being on time, doing something regularly), in the future (the formation of ideals, goal-setting, planning).

The psychological qualities of group **L** include: the ability to process physical material, to change the state of physical objects in accordance with an existing project, to carry out a technological process directed at obtaining a final product constituting a definite life resource, to change the position of objects and arrange them in space, to think and act technologically.

The psychological qualities of group **l** include: the ability to transmit, gather, accumulate and classify information about objects from written and oral sources, to reason, define, draw logical conclusions and construct proofs, to establish logical connections and existing relations between objects, to establish rules and adhere to them, to find quantitative parameters of their external characteristics — weight, size, distance and so on.

The psychological qualities of group **E** include: the ability to react emotionally to situations, events, phenomena and objects, to transmit the energy of emotional states to others, to support and inspire other people emotionally, to evaluate, to choose.

The psychological qualities of group **e** include: the ability to feel the attitude of other people to oneself and to one another, to evaluate these relations and actively to establish them.

## Socionics and Biology

Socionics makes it possible to take a fresh look at a whole range of psychological and biological concepts.

For example, let us turn our attention to the concept of instinct, long established in psychology and psychophysiology. First of all let us note that the concept of instinct is not unambiguous in science. Here we shall have in mind innate, or unconditioned, reflexes. Observation of animals and people shows that there exist both separate reactions and whole behavioural blocks which a living being is capable of carrying out without any learning. The programmes of such behaviour are innate, are transmitted by inheritance, and may be connected with the notion of species memory.

Each species of living being has its own set of such instinctive programmes, realised in the form of concrete actions. There is, however, a certain number of instincts common to many animal species. In the human young — the infant — psychophysiologists count twenty-nine innate reflex actions. For example, the reflex of sucking at the breast. When the mother takes the child in her arms and brings the breast to his lips, the hungry child very precisely seizes the nipple with his lips and begins to suck. Yet this is far from a simple act: the child must combine sucking, swallowing the milk and breathing. From the outside all this looks like a clearly formed rhythmic sequence of actions. The expediency of this reflex is obvious. Another example of an instinct in the infant is the grasping reflex, which manifests itself in his gripping firmly with his little fingers objects that fall into his palm. He can be lifted into the air this way if one offers him, say, one's index fingers to grasp. The child clutches at them so firmly that his little body is able to hold itself suspended indefinitely. For a human being such an adaptation does not seem necessary, but it can be understood as an animal atavism preserved from distant (some three million years ago!) simian ancestors, when the ability to hold on to the fur on the mother's chest or back was essential for survival.

It is held that the number of instinctive reflexes is specific to each animal species, but psychophysiology long ago discovered the possibility of distributing the whole possible diversity of innate reflexes among a small number of groups. This, incidentally, may be regarded as one of the manifestations of the structuralist approach in biology. However, different authors do this differently. Thus P. V. Simonov [16] distinguishes three groups of instinctive reflexes: vital reflexes ensuring the physical survival of the individual (alimentary, drinking, sleep-regulating, defensive and others); role, or zoosocial, reflexes (sexual, parental, territorial, hierarchical, and that of emotional resonance — behaviour motivated by signals of the state of another individual); and reflexes of self-development (exploratory, imitative, playful and others).

V. I. Garbuzov [17] develops a conception of seven human instincts: I — self-preservation, II — procreation, III — altruism, IV — exploration, V — dominance, VI — freedom, VII — preservation of dignity. In his book this theme is well worked out and well received, and can undoubtedly be applied with benefit by people for the upbringing of others and for work on themselves. But the same questions remain: "How do such lists arise? Do they possess completeness?"

We shall not give a full survey of the approaches to the classification of instincts here; these may be found in the specialist literature. All such classifications reflect to one degree or another the achievements of biology in the cognition of reality and correspond to the scientific interests of their authors. But one way or another, they are all the result of the invention of a suitable construct, the result of the scientist's inductive train of thought, and they contain the component of subjectivity that is inevitable in such a case. It is, however, legitimate to raise the question of the existence of a single basis for all such classifications, one that maximally eliminates subjectivity in the division of instincts into groups. Such a basis may be provided by the natural structure of the psychic functions discovered by socionics.

Proceeding from this idea, one may assert that there must exist exactly eight groups of innate reflexes, which is what instincts in fact are. Each such group reflects the tuning and work of the psyche with definite sides of reality; and since socionics discovers eight generalised aspects of the world, it is natural to suppose on this basis that the generalised reactions to the impact of the different sides of the world upon a living being must also be eight in number. ## Literature and Art as a Source of Psychologically Significant Information

Psychologists can hardly be reproached for having conducted psychological research in the way they did. They did it as best they could. They projected onto their research everything that was known to them in their time, including the philosophical views contemporary to them and, as we now understand, their own personal problems. In the course of broad psychoanalytic practice it turned out that an analyst who has not undergone special preparation inevitably projects his own complexes and problems onto the patient when working with him. In this connection a practice was adopted prescribing that a beginning psychoanalyst who has completed the corresponding educational institution should undergo a whole series of psychological trainings which reduce the danger of a deformation of the psychoanalytic procedure.

At present it is time for psychologists to think about the possibility of raising the level of objectivity of their research. One of the ways of solving this problem is the necessity of reckoning with the integral experience of humanity, which is recorded both in so-called secret knowledge and in such open and accessible sources as literature and works of art.

## Prospects and Programmes

In socionics one can now speak not only of a minimum programme but also of a maximum programme. It is clear that the minimum programme is the more modest, and may be reduced to the necessity of recognising socionics as a full-fledged and independent scientific discipline.

That socionic information enjoys interest from all strata of the population is confirmed by the growing demand for articles, books and journals with socionic content. Besides this, it is unofficially known that many "official" scholars are interested in socionics but cannot use it in their work precisely because it is not officially recognised, and their superiors forbid the use of what is not officially recognised.

The maximum programme could be formulated as THE RECOGNITION OF THE SOCIONIC PARADIGM AS THE UNIVERSAL METHODOLOGICAL FOUNDATION OF ALL THE HUMANITIES — which would, incidentally, be an entirely natural acknowledgement of the colossal contribution to psychology made by C. G. Jung and Aushra Augustinavichute.

It is easy to imagine that there will be people who cannot, will not, or will not find it possible to agree with such possibilities for socionics. There may be many reasons for this, including "defence of one's own uniform." It is not excluded that many such people, instead of discussing such questions in an academic mode with the use of logical argumentation, will resort to debating tricks, up to and including accusations that the author is psychically and mentally deficient, as well as to the use of administrative measures.

That socionics has the right to lay claim to a universal methodological role was shown in the articles "Socionics as Methodology" and "Nothing New Under the Sun," and also in A. V. Bukalov's programmatic article "Socionics — a New Approach to Understanding Man and Society" [12].

If such recognition were to take place, one important conclusion would follow from it — one which is not fully, or not at all, recognised or even realised by the majority of socionists. This conclusion is that socionics has its grounds within itself and therefore has no need to seek grounds in other scientific disciplines.

In particular, it has no need of a systems-theoretic grounding, since, on the basis of the postulate of the universality of the socionic paradigm, matters should stand precisely the other way round: systems theory itself must be derived from socionics.

It is not difficult to sketch certain moments of such a derivation. Let us note first of all that any scientific discipline relies on a certain set of concepts fundamental to it, and on the corresponding terms and meta-terms by means of which the subject and method of the given science are described and its connection with its grounding is maintained.

The basic concepts of systems theory are: SYSTEM, STATE, TRANSITION FROM ONE STATE TO ANOTHER, INPUT, OUTPUT, INFORMATION, CONTROL, and others. The concepts of THEORY and INFORMATION may in this case be regarded as meta-concepts, since the concept of THEORY, being general-scientific, is used in systems theory externally and only for the organisation of the material of this scientific discipline. With the concept of INFORMATION matters stand more complexly, since it may be directly introduced and defined within systems theory; yet it is broader than the limitations imposed upon it by that theory. And in general the concept of INFORMATION is a meta-concept for any human cognition, since it precedes cognition, and cognition may be reduced to the obtaining of information.

In socionics, however, in its methodological hypostasis, the concepts of INFORMATION and INFORMATION ASPECT are primary. Here, unlike in systems theory, INFORMATION is postulated first, together with its ASPECTS and the immediately adjoining concept of INFORMATION METABOLISM, and only then does everything else appear.

Let us now consider each of the concepts of systems theory from the point of view of socionics. The concept of SYSTEM is connected with the information aspect l (introverted logic); it appears within the depths of this aspect, as of its archetype, since this aspect points to the ordering of objects in space, and since in the DESCRIPTION (l) of a SYSTEM (l) time can be reduced to spatial relations by depicting it as one of the coordinate axes. But the point, of course, is not the formal modelling of time by spatial symbols, but the existence of the corresponding archetype.

In exactly the same way the contribution of the other aspects to the formation of the concepts of systems theory may be partially explicated. Thus, for example, the concept of THEORY constitutes a STRUCTURED DESCRIPTION (l) of POTENTIAL POSSIBILITIES (I), and this is its archetype. Real systems, however, unlike their descriptions, are characterised by the CHANGE OF STATES IN TIME (aspect i), by the presence of STATES (E), by a CAUSE (I) of the change of states, by the presence of MATERIAL ELEMENTS (L) and of CONNECTIONS BETWEEN THEM (e), by a definite ENERGY (E), and also by the LAWS OF THEIR OWN EXISTENCE and SOURCES OF GOAL-SETTING (I). The implicit definition of a system contained in this paragraph, without laying claim to completeness or finality, gives an idea of the complexity of this concept, which is connected with all the socionic IAs. On the basis of what has been said, one may suppose that the concept of system is the most complex of all abstract concepts — precisely because it is connected with the interaction of all the IAs.

The concept of CONTROL can apparently be presented as the result of the joint manifestation of two IAs — S and l. The aspect S by itself gives only the energetic-volitional component, the "motor" of the impact, as it were; and since the concept of CONTROL includes a change of trajectory in phase space, projections of the aspect l must be added for a sufficient description of it. Let us note that since two combinations are possible in the sequence of the aspects S and l, namely Sl and lS, one may speak of two forms of control — strategic (Sl) and tactical (lS).

Another important concept of systems theory is that of FEEDBACK, which in a first approximation may be reduced to the aspect e. However, this aspect alone is insufficient for describing so important and even complex a concept. This is not simply a connection. This connection is loaded with REFLECTION, with the MUTUAL MAPPING of two elements; and this connection is not simply an abstract concept but really manifests itself in the CHANGE (i and I) of the BEHAVIOUR (L) of one element depending on the STATE (E) of another.

Let us note that in this case the concept of CHANGE, which in pure form may be linked with the single aspect i, turns out to be connected with the aspect I as well, since this is a change depending on a certain content, on SENSE (I), and one which itself generates sense.

If we now distinguish within a system, as is done in the works of V. D. Yermak, the PROGRAMME and EXECUTIVE BLOCKS, then these too may be reduced to projections of definite IAs. The PROGRAMME BLOCK is connected with the aspects l and I, since it contains PRESCRIPTIONS (l) relying on a certain SENSE (I) connected with the ACHIEVEMENT OF A GOAL (I). Since two combinations are possible in the sequence of the influence of the aspects I and l, one may speak of two varieties of programming — strategic (Il) and tactical (lI). Analogous reasoning applies to the EXECUTIVE BLOCK as well.

Of course, one may ask why one should reduce the well-proven and practically tested concepts of systems theory to socionic aspects. The answer here may be not one but several.

First, we wish to show the potential possibilities of socionics as a methodology. Second, in science there always remains the problem of grounds and grounding, and the choice of a well-grounded paradigm is, from this point of view, an optimising step. Third, since socionic terminology may be laid at the basis of the description of any concepts, one may speak of terminological unification. Fourth, it is always useful to look at a problem from another angle. And fifth, socionic terminology makes it possible to discover a fine spectrum within concepts that are quite settled and no longer subjected to further analysis.

In connection with what has been set out above, one may speak of a PANSOCIONIC approach to all phenomena and processes in this world. And here, as always, there are pluses and minuses. Of course, the reckless reduction of complex phenomena infinitely rich in content to the dry schema which socionics can at times propose is hardly likely to bring useful results. However, the conscious application of the conceptual apparatus of socionics to the problems of contemporary science will undoubtedly help to discover new shades of settled meanings and will lead to the appearance of new solutions to old problems with a concrete technological output.

The reproach of the apriorism of irresponsible theoretical fantasising, sometimes encountered in scientific criticism, cannot bear on socionics as a whole — this is a science that uses the entire array of empirical data of psychology, sociology, history and so on. But of course individual intuitive "flights" may occur in socionics, just as in any other science. ### G. A. Shulman's reaction to the analysis and criticism of his configuration

Judging by the article "A Reply to Curzon", written by G. A. Shulman and circulated among socionists, he took great offence at me for attempting to make sense of his works and understand the course of his thought. Do I really have no right to that? I used no abusive words, as he does; I used no irony; I simply discussed the information contained in his articles, and did so in exactly the same way as when reading any other author. G. A. apparently considers that I do not understand him and do not hear him, and that over many years. Of course I could readdress that very statement to him and say that it is he who does not understand and does not hear me. For those who know socionics there is nothing surprising in this — two mutually uncomprehending types have come together. Of course I do not understand why he calls me Curzon. I appear to have no relation whatever to Curzon, and there is no analogy in my surname. I cannot, for instance, tell whether this is a variety of insult or some variety of humour. In any case his article approaches in style a lampoon, or rather a libel.

And yet it is interesting: how could I have understood him had I not thought over and analysed his articles? After all, I read and analyse not only his articles but the articles and books of many other socionists. With some I agree more, with others less. And that is comprehensible. I, for example, disagree with V. V. Gulenko and V. D. Ermak on many points, and I say so constantly, and I am ready to discuss contentious questions in an academic mode — but, needless to say, without any manipulative tricks: without offence-taking, threats, pressure, blackmail, mockery, profanation, defamation and the like.

However, judging by the article, G. A. Shulman considers that I am engaged in vilifying him. He writes that the vilification began fifteen years ago, with the appearance of the Periodic System of the Socion (PSS), and continues to this day — with varying assiduity, under various sauces (from "But this is nonsense!" to "And what for?"). G. A. goes on to write that a special place among all the vilifiers is occupied by the head of the methodology department of the International Institute of Socionics, Semyon Ivanovich Churyumov. No references to the corresponding places in my articles and papers are given, apparently because I never used the phrases quoted about "nonsense" and about "what for". I find it interesting to make sense of the peculiarities of people's thinking, and I try to do so professionally. In the end we are socionists and are trying to make sense of people. I do not understand why G. A. Shulman tries to be ironic at my expense, why he attempts to cast doubt on my ability to think and to formulate my thoughts. I do not understand why, about my attempts to make sense of G. A. Shulman's theoretical constructions, it is necessary to write that the Periodic System of the Socion just will not let Semyon Ivanovich sleep in peace — will not let him at all — and that he has been demolishing the poor thing at no fewer than three socionics conferences. For me G. A. Shulman's Periodic System really does represent a special interest, but simply because, to my mind, more logical and methodological errors have been committed in this theoretical construct than in the works of other socionists, while at the same time it is a beautiful and plausible construct whose logical defects and errors are not obvious. And these errors must be analysed and explained so that they do not hinder the development of the new science. The errors themselves deserve deep and close attention. I always have been and remain open to a conscientious discussion of any theoretical construction. I have always built my argumentation in detail and with care, and I should like people to talk to me in an academic style rather than in the language of libels.

Churyumov had, among the 1,420 sources of information he used in writing his doctoral dissertation, not merely indicated two collections of hikers' songs but conscientiously listed all the songs in those collections — then, oho, what a bibliography would have resulted! Frightening to think of. And those collections he too knows, for he is a sinner and loves hikers' songs; a great many songs are gathered there. What have hikers' songs to do with it? I catch only that some insinuation is contained here, but why a person writes precisely in this way is beyond me. Possibly some doubt is being expressed about the quality of my doctoral dissertation, but what its substance is I do not understand.

From the style of "Curzon" one gets the impression that G. A. Shulman does not admit the thought that he might be mistaken, and that he perceives academic criticism as a wish to defame — at any price.

At the 8th socionics conference in Irpen I found, as it seems to me, an exceptionally mild form of conveying my understanding of G. A. Shulman's work: I applied his method to the construction of analogous constructs, from which it could follow that no such construction is absolute, and hence that one may doubt the absoluteness of the PSS as well. G. A. Shulman assessed this as a striving to defame. In my book I have devoted sufficient attention to G. A. Shulman's constructions (but of course not only to his) simply because I am a logical type and do not let logical errors pass. All the more since this is my direct duty as head of the methodology department of the IIS. In other respects I regard G. A. Shulman with respect, considering him a talented poet, translator, journalist, editor, orator and educator, and in some measure I understand his feelings; but I need arguments, not attempts to manipulate through taking offence. There is nothing personal in my analysis of G. A. Shulman's works, and if anyone finds errors in my argumentation I am ready to discuss any topic, as I have done more than once. There is no "crusade", no vilification and no striving to defame a talented person; there is only logical argumentation. I by no means insist that I understand everything correctly, and when G. A. Shulman says that my article abounds, to put it mildly, in inaccuracies, I study his text attentively. G. A. Shulman considers that my article utterly rejects (or refutes, crosses out, disavows etc.) the basic propositions and conclusions he arrived at as a result of sixteen years of research in socionics. I fully allow that this may be dramatic; but if the construction of G. A. Shulman's under examination has turned out to be an artefact — a beautiful one, outwardly plausible and actively defended — then one must have the courage to renounce one's "favourite delusions", assuming of course that I am right. Unfortunately G. A. has chosen the path of taking offence and of ethical dramatisation instead of calm and careful discussion.

In any case this discussion shows how differently two representatives of two socionic types understand one and the same words; and if G. A. Shulman retains confidence in his own rightness, that is his right — but I too am entitled to my own view of the problem. I probably have many errors, but I should like to be convinced of them rather than have open and disguised abuse used against me. For example, G. A. Shulman openly mocks the "Churyumov Square", holding forth on the absurdity of this "article of manufacture". A person's TIM is not something indecent, forbidden and not to be mentioned. In any case, socionists determine and name the TIMs of actors, writers, scholars, politicians and generally anyone they wish.

"Curzon" ends with a list of categories in which G. A. Shulman assesses my abilities to work with theoretical texts. They are: "1. Aggressive-ignorative voluntarism; 2. The method of snatched fragments; 3. Global trivialism; 4. Concrete-conceptual primitivism (in naming the working methods of the co-contacts under survey)." This at least is comprehensible and witty. There is, however, an essential difference in the motivation of the generalising terms used by Shulman and by Churyumov. For the generalised designation of Shulman's methodological complex, Churyumov used methodological categories which Shulman himself insistently, over many years, in many articles and papers, used as key ones, in his opinion, for work with socionic material. These are the category of beauty as a criterion of truth, the principle of relative reliability, and the principle of the error of the measuring instrument. This is his working terminology, and therefore there can be nothing offensive in designating Shulman's methodological position by these terms as _instrumental-aesthetic relativism_. That is, there is no question here of pinning on labels, as G. A. assesses it, but only of using an adequate term. Shulman, on the other hand, ascribes to Churyumov methodological terms which the latter has never used as normative instruments.
