# Section 4

## PSI-Socionics — Socionics with a Psychological Face?

_(based on the book by S. Filimonov and I. Malsky)_

> _Across the wild steppes of Transbaikalia, where gold is dug in the mountains..._ _(Russian folk song)_

**Editor:** The socionic work of S. Filimonov and I. Malsky is distinguished by the fact that the authors expanded the presentation of the "canonical" material which most socionists give "first priority." They did this by adding to it a significant volume of information from books on psychological topics — in particular, on ego psychology — as well as through innovations of their own. They motivated this expansion by arguing that without such additions socionics looks too isolated from the unquestionably valuable psychological material accumulated by science over roughly two hundred years. This is probably not a bad idea, allowing a socionics instructor to significantly increase the volume of his lectures and to talk about things that in purely socionic courses are taken for granted or touched on only in passing comments.

This authorial stance gives them the opportunity to declare their course an independent educational unit, which they designated Ψ-socionics, apparently meaning psychological socionics. And while Ψ-socionics looks like an authorial innovation with an understandable desire to improve the canonical core of socionics through their own understanding, and to make it more accessible — at least as it seems to them — to a wide circle of readers varying in interests and level of psychological preparation, "psychological socionics" looks like a more serious bid to create a socionics with a "psychological face," integrating the key concepts of ego psychology.

The book includes large anthology-style insertions from books by Burns (the "Self"-concept), Rudhyar (The Experience of Change and Order), Berne (Diagram of the Personality), Kalinauskas (Self-Awareness and the Ego), several large fragments from Jung, and other, unnamed authors.

Personally, I believe that a socionist should know and respect psychology, but socionics has no need to "curry favor" with psychology or to try to "improve" its concepts to fit those already existing in psychology. Socionics does not oppose psychology and is not opposed to it — it grew out of it and is an organic part of it. However, socionics contains within itself the potential of a unifying theory, which is precisely what psychology currently lacks, having turned out over the entire course of its development to be merely a multitude of empirical findings that can successfully be applied for practical recommendations, but behind which there stands no single cause-and-effect explanation.

The editor, having read the text of the book, believes the authors made great efforts to make their material understandable to the "wide circle." However, in many cases their ideas — being either the authors' own improvements, or derivatives of what exists in modern psychology (which is, incidentally, very heterogeneous in its scientific and terminological style), or of what is present in well-known spiritual teachings — take on a metaphysical character. This means these ideas lose their direct credibility for the "wide circle" and appeal to the authors' subjective certainty and to unprovable entities. The introduction of these ideas is predominantly emotional in character and relies on NLP techniques for working with the reader's and listener's consciousness. Which may well be effective, but requires ethical and legal clarifications connected with intervention in the recipient's subjective world.

At the beginning of the book the authors report that they strive "not to weary the well-disposed reader with constructions of the type '...psychic processes and functions, being emergent properties of brain systems at various levels of organization, are, by virtue of emergent determinism, capable of subordinating to their tasks the functions of individual components of brain micro- and macrosystems...'" and that therefore "in certain places" they "had to sin against precision of definitions for the sake of greater accessibility of the essence of the matter." Of course, for their text this is an exaggeration, and in socionics no one uses such combinations of words. Therefore, in my view, there was no point in simplifying the socionic language and speaking of "empty-shell" selves, "Floating islands and the territory of consciousness," "little engines and carriages," or "crosses of functions." Perhaps it is pretty, but within a text claiming fundamentality and scientific reliability it looks like populist decoration. All the same, the greater part of the text requires precise statements that are scientifically complete. Instead of raising the neophyte to their level — of which, as is evident from his remarks, he is quite capable — the authors play "guru and dimwit" with him (from the joke: "one day a guru met a dimwit..."), which looks rather absurd, like a kind of baby talk. As a result the text turned out uneven: complete, clearly formulated theoretical passages alternate with an imitation of childish babble as the neophyte's characteristic. Of course, all this is the authors' business — I have merely expressed my emotional impressions on the matter, and possibly the socionic interference of the TIMs of the authors and the editor plays a role here.

Here is an example of a well-written excerpt from the book, good and comprehensible to a modern intellectual: "If one may suppose 'foundations' of some general kind at the source of the human unconscious — 'foundations' supplying psychic contents other than a person's everyday awareness of the stream of existential data of sensory experience — it seems probable that we should today find ideas, images, modes of thinking at least relatively identical in various cultural regions." But it turns out this sentence does not belong to the authors — it is from the anthology insertion "Archetypes" by Jung. There are quite a few such passages in the book, but most of them do not belong to the authors.

I would not at all wish to criticize the authors — that is a thankless task and it would look quite different. I would like to sort out the particularities of their methodological complex and their understanding of socionics, since they are not the only ones proposing "refinements" of the socionic paradigm, which is presented quite distinctly in the works of Aušra Augustinavičiūtė. Therefore part of my comments relates to general methodology, and part specifically to the exposition of socionic concepts. In addition, as an editor of scientific texts, I sometimes pay attention to authorial style — but this, of course, is a trifle, and one need not take it into account. Nevertheless, the editor reserves the right to note grammatical and stylistic errors. Possibly in some places the editor becomes too ironic and sharp, but that depends on the particularities of the material. The editor apologizes in advance in every possible way and asks that his comments be regarded as friendly remarks in the hallway conversations of conferences, and he is ready for academic discussion of any problems connected with socionics.

To speak frankly, I did not like the book. What exactly is wrong with the book, in my view, I will discuss in the editorial comments. As an editor I consider it raw and therefore published hastily. It resembles raw tape recordings of "field" conversations having the character of more or less random improvisations. There are many drawn-out metaphysical monologues on the essence of man and, in particular, on the nature of the "self," including Buddhist reminiscences about "emptiness" — which, of course, is not necessarily bad. There are many long discussions with no direct relation to socionics, such as "the movements of the 'self' across the territory of consciousness according to the laws of association" and the like. Here is a small fragment of such reasoning: "The question is as follows: when a movement of the 'self' across the territory of consciousness from one content to another occurs in us, what kind of journey is this? It is the movement of the 'self' across various fragments consisting of the products of sensation and intuition. At some moment I have a perception; from the perception I may jump to some image, if the function of intuition has fired; from the image I may pass to a concept, I think about it, ...again a perception, some representation may be evoked — that is, a chain of elements. That is, in consciousness there is constant movement along such chains..."

I personally perceive such text as rather muddled and, in any case, poorly edited. Many pages are filled with "conversations" of this kind. Apparently the authors believe that such "chains" promote a better understanding of socionics by the "ordinary person."

The book contains many superficial and banal discussions, probably reflecting the authors' informational growth, and undoubtedly possessing biographical value, which may also be used for psychological research. In any case, this side of the authorial personality interests the editor, and he tries to go into all the details connected with the author's methodological complex.

Nevertheless, not everything in the book is bad. It should be noted that an unquestionable merit of the authors is the attempt to identify physiognomic markers of TIM. This is a good beginning of a large and valuable work, but it cannot be brought to full completion without competent reliance on the achievements of modern psychology and methodology. And, of course, there are many beautiful literary passages. And quite a few plausible, credible and realistic judgments. But, alas, if for every barrel of honey there is even a spoonful of tar — or something worse — then who needs such honey?

What they write about men and women is good.

What follows is the editor's correspondence dialogue with Borisova, the editor of "Psi-socionics," who wrote a short introduction to the book, and with the author, who in this case is collective — that is, Filimonov and Malsky, whose percentage contributions to the concept of psi-socionics are unknown to me, and so I will treat what they wrote as a joint position.

## Dialogue with Borisova, the Book's Editor

**Borisova:** Over the roughly 30 years of its existence, socionics has traveled a certain path of development — and also of adaptation to the current demands of users. What is the difference and, in my view, the value of "old" socionics compared with the works of recent years? Modern socionics is regarded as an applied psychological typology that helps solve questions of people's compatibility, career guidance, and so on; and the main problem preventing socionics from becoming a simple tool in the user's hands is the problem of identifying the socionic type. However, the "old" socionists regarded this science not only as a tool but also as a conception helping to clarify and understand various psychological questions (the structure and functioning of the human psyche), social ones (the specialization of types within the socion), and even philosophical ones. Therefore works written in the 1990s contain, if one may put it this way, more reverence toward the subject of socionics, more respect for the complexity of the human psyche, and more indications of the unknown and unknowable within it.

**Editor:** I do not agree with Borisova that socionics should be divided into old and new. New generations will come, and what now looks new will become old. But socionics has an outer layer oriented toward quick practical use, and a deep core that directly touches the very foundations of the universe. And this core requires deep comprehension, regardless of how the practical road-testing of its layers most accessible to superficial comprehension proceeds. As for the underlined words: with the existence of the unknown in socionics I agree, and I think it is a matter of time and technique. As for unknowability, that is the usual agnostic illusion, connected with the difficulties of cognition and often with an inability or unwillingness to change one's methodological positions. Attempts to "squeeze" modern socionics into the frame designated by Borisova — that of an applied psychological typology — are one of the forms of failing to understand its fundamentality, and of the desire to adapt it to the horizon of the psychologist-philistine who does not wish to part with the mastered and dogmatized space of the established image of psychology.

**Borisova:** Let me note certain moments in this book that please me. The meaning of the socionic type is examined in great detail — why it is needed, how it arises, and what it actually is. Thus the socionic model of the psyche does not "hang in the air" but has a grounding beneath it (and moreover an extra-socionic grounding, one that does not close the system "in on itself").

**Editor:** Very well said. However, the present existence of two and a half dozen paradigmatic deviations from the classical version of socionics makes the situation considerably more complex. This situation shows that more than one explanation and grounding of socionic concepts is possible, but the question always remains how close such explanations and groundings are to the truth. Therefore a careful comparison of the existing versions of socionics is necessary in search of objective truth.

Unfortunately, the authors have a whole series of methodological inaccuracies that significantly reduce trust in their results. One also senses insufficient familiarity with the works of other authors, thanks to whom much in socionics has long since ceased to "hang in the air" and has a fundamental grounding.

**Borisova:** What seems important to me is the examination of the functionality of the socionic functions — not simply "how it works" but also "why this or that function is needed"; and also the manifestation of the psychotype in the deep, true values of the personality (hence the thought that to understand the socionic type one must come into contact with these values).

**Editor:** Here Borisova introduces a term new to socionics — "the functionality of the socionic functions" — which, as is clear from her text, she considers to be the totality of a function's content and its purpose. However, the content and the purpose of a function constitute a single whole, and their separation is, from the standpoint of scientific methodology, inadmissible. In Jung this moment was by no means overlooked; it constitutes the very essence of his discovery, though he never fully reflected on it, leaving much at the level of empirical fact. What is at issue here are the two inseparable semantics of each socionic function: positional and aspectual. Aušra developed this theme to a constructive level, specifying the positional semantics of the functions explicitly. From the text of "psi-socionics" it turns out that the authors did not understand this and filled the resulting gap with considerations of their own. Which are, undoubtedly, of interest — but more from the standpoint of human delusions.

**Borisova:** The book is written in a "cyclical" form and in the form of a dialogue, which implies repetitions and clarifications of unclear moments. And this is indeed so — its language is simple and accessible to understanding, yet it is not "popular" in the way many other books on socionics are. Unlike them, "Ψ-socionics" presupposes a much greater depth of mining the useful minerals of knowledge and understanding.

**Editor:** Knowing socionics, one may suppose that a text written by the bearer of one TIM will not always be comprehensible to the bearer of another. Personally, I perceive the structure of Filimonov and Malsky's book as unjustifiably overcomplicated. I even got the impression that with the reference to cyclicity they are merely masking a desire to avoid the hard work needed to eliminate the multiple repetitions that make the book's text not merely redundant but loose and chaotic. What does cyclicity have to do with it if, for example, the book many times repeats the same kind of reasoning about extraversion and introversion? In the practice of editing scientific literature this is, at best, assessed as inexperience, an inability to build a conceptual-terminological sequence, or as the preliminary character of draft work when there is not enough time to bring it up to marketable quality. And, at worst, it is either carelessness or elementary laziness about finishing the work in order to bring it to the editorial standard. As for the language, it is, in my view, weighed down with concepts from other branches of psychology — quite effective in training workshops but lacking convincing theoretical grounding. The cause of many of the presumed shortcomings of the authors' work is insufficient command of modern scientific methodology, as well as limited familiarity with the socionic literature.

**Borisova:** There are moments in the book that seem somewhat outdated, since they were later developed in more detail (for example, the semantics of the aspects), or, on the contrary, remained "overboard" from the main socionic base — and not necessarily deservedly (for example, methods of investigating intertype relations). But I do not think this harms the book; at worst, one may regard these elements as having historical value. And such details as, for example, a numbering of the functions in the model different from the one used in modern works should not trouble the attentive reader.

**Editor:** Here one senses limited familiarity with the socionic literature, where solutions to many of the problems the authors try to solve in their own way have long existed. As for the numbering of the functions in the model, that is of course a trifle, since the functions in themselves have no numbering, and each author introduces one according to his own understanding of Model A. Moreover, all authorial innovations in understanding Model A should be regarded as an authorial model of the socionic type, which can naturally be designated as Model "F."

## Dialogue with the Authors — Editorial-Methodological Part

**Authors:** The leitmotif of the proposed material is the attainment of an understanding of surrounding reality, of the other person and of oneself; the search for confidence and creative self-expression in the nature and regularities of one's own perception, thinking, and behavior.

**Editor:** In this seemingly quite harmless and understandable phrase, the reader is immediately designated as a "subject who does not understand reality, lacks confidence, and is creatively limited" — this is a covert "Ericksonian" suggestion; such are the laws of NLP. At the same time, the authors position themselves as a source of understanding and confidence, ready to enlighten the naive neophyte. The book is indeed constructed as a dialogue between a master and a neophyte — these are the authors' own terms. From the standpoint of literary form this is a device well known since the days of Galileo, but each author's choice of specific roles is his own and to some extent characterizes him. Clearly the authors' self-assessment and assessment of the situation show through here. Unfortunately, the authors sometimes lose ethical tact and begin to gravitate toward the image of the Guru — in that ironic sense in which the word is used to assess the now widespread phenomenon of the preaching of spiritual teachings by people who have only lightly touched them. The authors are undoubtedly experienced socionists who have successfully conducted many classes and groups, and have of course experienced the feeling of being a "first teacher," which brings deep satisfaction but at the same time contains a danger connected with ambition — a danger into which self-made preachers of spiritual teachings and disseminators of socionics fall so easily. I hope the authors have avoided this trap.

**Authors:** Since the book is addressed not to a community of narrow specialists but to a wide circle of readers varying in interests and level of psychological preparation, we have tried to simplify the language as much as possible, to make it accessible and similar to the everyday speech in which most questions of interest to the reader are discussed in ordinary life.

**Editor:** For "ordinary" people, around four dozen books on socionics have already been written — including in the form of dialogues between the author and a possible reader. If the authors intend to explain to people for the 41st time the same thing they have heard from others, then their book is probably somewhat overdue. It will be interesting to see whether they can say something that was not in the other popular books. Besides, there is always the danger that simplifying the language and eliminating scientific terminology may turn into vulgarization and the transformation of the fine and sensitive organism of a theory into a mechanistic conglomerate of approximations. It is hardly the case that a perfected and harmonious theory should stoop to the level of consciousness of the intellectual philistine — who, incidentally, is often quite prepared to make the necessary effort and rise to the level of a full-fledged science in order to become a professional. This is doubly expedient: the intellectual realizes his ambition and becomes a master, while science has no need to twist its own arms and stoop to the level of baby talk.

**Authors:** We also ask the reader to take into account that limited space does not allow us to present detailed proofs of the proposed conception. We count on his good disposition and, for our part, understand perfectly that our very first task is to present the material as clearly as possible.

**Editor:** The book is rather voluminous — do detailed proofs of their conception really require significantly more space? While reading the book I got the impression that the exposition of the authors' ideas could have been more compact. For example, by removing the redundant repetitions.

**Authors:** Most authors demonstrate a clear ignorance of already developed ideas and theories, offering as a kind of revelation regularities of psychic activity discovered by the authors personally in the process of analyzing their individual experience; creating new concepts and abstract models that complicate the already tangled terminology.

**Editor:** What majority is the author speaking of? The authors of books on socionics? Among them there are not only graphomaniacs but also people sufficiently familiar with psychology and in confident command of the socionic minimum. Yes, many of them have their own understanding of socionics — but do Filimonov and Malsky not wish to offer the reader yet one more understanding of what Aušra Augustinavičiūtė proposed to us?

**Authors:** Man is, on the one hand, a biological being, an animal belonging to the class of mammals, distinguished by upright posture and the development of the hands, and possessing a highly developed brain. On the other hand, man is a social being. This is an important attribute, since social life and social relations have changed and subordinated to themselves man's biological, bodily organization. The unrepeatability of the ensemble of qualities of the bodily and psychic organization of a specific person as a representative of the human species is reflected in the concept of the individuum.

**Editor:** This question and these definitions have already been discussed in the socionic literature. But in this excerpt there is a subtle semantic inaccuracy which I would not want to pass by. Read the underlined part of the sentence closely. It can be understood to mean that man once existed separately from social life, and that social life appeared from somewhere unknown and forcibly deformed his body. Yet it is clear that man and his social life are inseparable and evolved as a single whole. This almost imperceptible error may be understood as a symptom of the authors' intellectual style: they do not fully hold on to dialectical meanings, which — given their intention to simplify the exposition of their conception — may turn into over-simplification.

For reference, let us recall the basic concepts relating to the human being as the editor understands them:

- **human being** — the general name of representatives of the species Homo sapiens;
- **individual** — a single specimen of Homo sapiens;
- **individuality** — the unity of the physical, physiological, psychic, and psychological qualities of a person in their unique combination;
- **personality** — individuality adapted to society;
- **"self" ("I")** — a symbol of subjective self-identification at any level of the existence of individuality, and also the theoretical projection of such self-identification;
- **ego** — the objective equivalent of the "self," fixed as the core of the personality or of the individuality, depending on the theoretical positions of the scholar.

**Authors:** There exist different points of view on the relation of biological and social factors in the development of the personality. We will treat the biological moments (meaning innate properties) as the given conditions of personality development, which do not unconditionally determine its psychological traits, but act, on the one hand, as factors structuring the individual's interaction with the surrounding environment, and, on the other hand, as forms and modes of his existence. In this the personality is understood not as a passive result of external influence; it develops in the process of its own activity and the organization of individual experience.

**Editor:** This is written very precisely and very scientifically, and will undoubtedly be well received by the intellectual reader. Alas, to understand socionics one must have a sufficient level of mental development, and "socionics for the feeble-minded" inevitably degenerates into a simplified set of semi-jargon and jargon clichés.

**Authors:** For especially fastidious readers who demand clear formulations from the very beginning of the exposition, let us define the concept of personality as follows: the personality may be described from the standpoint of the motives and strivings that make up the content of its "personal world" — that is, a unique system of personal (individual) meanings, individually distinctive ways of ordering external impressions and internal experiences.

**Editor:** An especially fastidious reader — for example, myself — will not be satisfied by such a definition, simply because it does not satisfy the rules of definition. First, the genus to which the concept of personality belongs is not indicated. The format of a definition — "Personality is..." — is not maintained. And most importantly, this definition contains one of the standard logical errors — "idem per idem," that is, "the same through the same," or a "circle": here personality is defined through the personal world and personal meanings — that is, through itself. So the fastidious readers remain unsatisfied, and a doubt about the authors' logical competence creeps in on them.

**Authors:** In psychology there are numerous attempts to single out the structure of the personality. Correspondingly, many existing psychological conceptions claim to describe structural features, although in practice they deal with what may be called the "shell" — the particularities of behavior, perception, thinking, and other directly observable manifestations of the personality.

**Editor:** The fastidious readers are again unsatisfied: no definition of the structure of the personality has been given, since each scholar may develop his own views on the structure of the personality and use his own definitions. Therefore the authors' critical remarks about existing psychological conceptions turn out to be superficial.

**Authors:** The study of the structure of the personality appears extremely productive, since it stands in the same relation to the shell as cause to effect. At the same time, the shell too is of no small applied interest, if one focuses attention not on the laws organizing the psychic life of the individuum but on concrete, particular manifestations.

**Editor:** The authors intend to study something they can name but cannot define. It is also unclear why the structure is opposed to the shell — does the shell not enter, or can it not enter, into the structure? Perhaps the authors identify the structure of the personality with the core of the personality, but then that is what should be said. These particularities of the authors' thinking arouse in the fastidious reader the suspicion that the authors' thinking belongs to the category of the fuzzy. And such people cannot be trusted in theory. Though it is not excluded that they are remarkable practitioners. Well, we shall see...

**Authors:** The goal we pursue in writing this book may be designated as the identification, classification, and description of the various structures of the personality, and of their determining role in human behavior, motivation, and perception of the surrounding world, of the other person, and of oneself.

**Editor:** From this quotation we learn (indirectly) that, it turns out, there exist "various structures of the personality," and consequently, the fastidious readers conclude, there are several of them. That is, at least more than one. Now one wants all the more to learn what the structure of the personality is, and how the authors manage to criticize others regarding something they cannot define.

**Authors:** Ψ is a letter of the Greek alphabet (psi). According to legend, it symbolizes the trident of the sea god Poseidon, whose cult was widespread in Ancient Greece. It was believed that with his trident Poseidon tore the earth and, ripping deeply into its body, created bays and straits. The trident itself symbolizes the division of the world into three spheres — the earthly, the heavenly, and the spiritual — and is the union of three primary elements: air, water, and earth.

**Editor:** Aha — it appears the authors have mythological thinking, and that is somewhat different from what a rationally thinking scientist requires. The fastidious readers fear they are going to be told myths and fairy tales. In any case, they have not been given a definition of the structure of the personality, though the authors have demonstrated decent erudition regarding the Greek alphabet.

**Authors:** As is well known, the term "psychology" is of ancient Greek origin.

**Editor:** Here we go... It is known that this word is composed of Greek roots, but its origin is European, since it was proposed not in Ancient Greece but in Europe in the 16th century, and its inventor was a philosopher, in 1590. The fastidious readers note the possibility of a certain confusion in the authors' minds. A socionist has the right to suppose that the authors are probably not logical types but ethical ones.

**Authors:** ...after psychology became a science, this word came to be used much more often. According to one version, the use of the Greek letter Ψ to denote the term "psychology" was first employed by students of philosophical institutes to speed up the note-taking of psychology lectures.

**Editor:** We learn much that is new and interesting about the history of the use of the letter psi — whereas at the very beginning the authors complained that the volume of the book did not allow them to unfold a full-fledged grounding of their conception. The fastidious readers wonder what all this might mean...

**Authors:** Socionics was born at the junction of three sciences: psychology, sociology, and informatics.

**Editor:** This sounds beautiful and scientific, but it is not quite true. Or rather, there is something in it, but it is written with a kind of semantic skew. There simply was no such concrete junction and intersection of these sciences. Anyone who remembers the history of the discovery of socionics knows that Aušra Augustinavičiūtė, being an economist, was head of a department of the family. She was specially interested in the typology of the human being, and when the authorities in the country permitted the reading of Jung's "Psychological Types," she, having read this work, intuitively penetrated the meaning of Jung's typology and on this basis reconstructed a possible model of Jung. What she built is not Jung's model, since Jung did not use such terminology, but his thought was close to it. Therefore it is more correct to call this model the "AJ" model — Augustinavičiūtė–Jung. So the psychological component is plainly present. As for the informational idea, Aušra took it not from informatics but borrowed it from the Polish psychiatrist Kępiński, who specialized in schizophrenia and who, apparently by analogy with physiological metabolism, and not being a specialist in informatics, began to use the expression "informational metabolism" approximately and in a simplified way, though with originality. Moreover, he used this term not at all in the way Aušra came to. He employed the word in a very narrow sense to designate a certain intellectual function responsible for the assimilation of concrete information connected with schizophrenia. Aušra transformed this term into a scientific category with an extremely broad meaning. Thus IM in Kępiński's mouth and in Aušra's are homonyms — they merely sound the same, but different concepts stand behind them. Unfortunately, "official science" has to this day not grasped the scale and significance of this concept. As for the sociological contribution to Aušra's discovery, it played only the role of background. In any case, Aušra nowhere cites the research of, say, Shibutani, Mead, or Strawson. So Aušra Augustinavičiūtė's discovery belongs entirely to her extraordinary genius — though sociology and informatics could draw much from it. And there is no need to "put the cart before the horse."

The fastidious readers here too discover the approximateness and a certain optionality of the authorial text. Editors call this phenomenon "diluting a text to increase its volume" and resolutely cross out such places, which raises the target informativeness of the text and does not lead to its worsening.

**Authors:** Using the concept of informational metabolism introduced by A. Kępiński, A. Augustinavičiūtė proposed structural models of the psychotypes.

**Editor:** The fastidious readers perceive this assertion within the frame of the saying "elderberry in the garden, but an uncle in Kyiv" [i.e., a non sequitur]. Apparently the authors do not understand that the structural models of the psychotypes proceeded directly from Jung and in no way from Kępiński. And it happened in this order: Jung's schema → the "AJ" model → Model A → the realization that Model A represents a generalized schema of IM in the human psyche. As for Kępiński, Aušra, having rethought his term (not even his idea) to the scale of a universal process, crowned the typology she had created with the concept of IM, transforming it from one particular case among many typologies into a fundamental theory with enormous methodological potential. The authors' assertion is wrong in substance: Aušra created the models of TIM not at all on the basis of the concept of IM, but proceeding from Jung's key ideas about the positioning of functions in the psyche and the basic set of four generalized psychic functions. It was only afterward, after building the model, that she named the corresponding types "types of informational metabolism." Out of ignorance or carelessness the authors draw together meanings that are in reality not directly connected with each other.

**Authors:** Thanks to this, it proved possible to describe a significant part of the processes of perception and behavior of a person as the bearer of a specific type of informational metabolism (TIM — a more precise variant of the term "psychotype"), as well as his strong and weak sides.

**Editor:** After the previous inaccuracy, this assertion is almost correct. The point is that, looking back, one may say that Jung's types were of course also TIMs — only Jung did not know it at the time, and if he had been told, he might not have understood or accepted it: the times were different. Aušra, having built the models of all the psychological types without at all relying on the concept of IM, was able in this connection to rethink Kępiński's attractive term, which had become heuristic for her. This is an example of the work of the intuition of the brilliant discoverer of socionics — like a flash of lightning that drew together in Aušra's consciousness two concepts which until then no one had perceived as related to each other. In any case, it is interesting that the authors understand this differently. As for the term "psychotype," its use is incorrect without an indication of which typology is meant — in different typologies one and the same person may belong to different psychotypes.

**Authors:** Thanks to the models of the psychotypes it became possible to describe and predict both the behavior of the personality in standard situations and interpersonal (intertype) relations.

**Editor:** Of course, here one must write "socionic psychotypes" or, better, "socionic types" — though TIM, as a more precise variant of the term "socionic psychotype," is preferable.

**Authors:** Almost immediately the enormous practical importance of socionics became evident. For while classifications of characters, however incomplete, do exist, the possibility of precisely describing and predicting human interrelations is absent in traditional psychology. It proved possible to solve problems of the compatibility of partners in marriage, in the work collective, in business, and purposefully to form stable, cohesive groups for solving various tasks in any sphere of human activity.

**Editor:** Here we are dealing with Jung's theoretical notions of the interaction of the conscious and unconscious attitudes. These notions have never been tested in laboratory experiment, but they may be regarded as a hypothesis. Behind this reasoning of Jung's one may discern a formula built according to the scheme: "C(ra)—U(oa)" — that is, "if the conscious is subordinated to the ruling attitude, then the unconscious is subordinated to the opposite attitude." Such a formula makes a plausible impression, though it bears a merely speculative character, since it has never been proven either in Jungian analytical psychology or in Freudian psychoanalysis. The well-grounded notion available in socionics — that both in the zone of the conscious and in the zone of the unconscious there exist both extraverted and introverted functions — renders such formulas doubtful.

Furthermore, Jung speaks of the "compensatory activity of the unconscious." But of what does such activity consist? To compensate means to make good a loss. In a broader sense, compensation may be understood as the counterbalancing of any deviation from the optimum. One of the forms of universal compensation is the oscillatory process, when a deviation from the neutral position in one direction is counterbalanced by a deviation of the same magnitude in the other. In physiology, one may assign to such processes, for example, the cycle "performance of work — compensation of the expended effort." Psychic activity is connected with the large cyclic process of the alternation of waking and sleep. And if waking corresponds to a significant degree to the zone of consciousness, then sleep for the most part belongs to the subconscious and the unconscious. But can one consider sleep merely a compensation of activity during waking? Apparently not, since, according to the available notions, sleep is not reducible merely to the restoration of working capacity. Some part of it constitutes, for example, the ordering of the impressions received during waking, which may be regarded as a continuation — not a compensation — of daytime activity.

**Editor:** Of course, this is a typical metaphysical misunderstanding. Such a thing can be asserted only within the frame of subjectivistically oriented philosophies. **Authors (quotation from Jung):** The mechanism is understandable: interacting at the current moment with this object, we unconsciously perceive (by means of the sense organs) information which is the "trigger" for our rich unconscious experience, in which an informational message is formed, in turn issued into consciousness in the form of symbols, associations, signs.

**Editor:** Here the authors offer, as a cause-and-effect explanation of the different vertness of the functions in the block, their invention "of the necessity of accomplishing a cycle." By a cycle they mean the alternation of the directedness of attention outward–inward. Apparently the authors consider that attention rhythmically changes directedness — otherwise where would the cycle come from? In both extraverts and introverts the cyclicity itself is identical, but in extraverts, in the authors' opinion, only the outward directedness of attention is made conscious, and in introverts only the inward. This is a pretty explanation — however, its truth is improbable. In psychology there are no data on the existence of such cycles. It is known that the directedness of attention may be voluntary, involuntary, and post-voluntary. In the voluntary regime the subject himself directs attention. In the involuntary, the directedness of attention is determined by the object. In the post-voluntary, attention is forcibly held by an impression. Clearly the voluntary regime cannot be chosen for the role of a cyclic process, by reason of the conscious relation to it. The involuntary depends on the object, and since the appearance of the object is not controlled by the subject, this regime too is not cyclic. The same can be said of the post-voluntary regime. For example, a tourist's pitching of a tent is an extraverted process. And regardless of who does it — extravert or introvert — it cannot be done in an unconscious state. Just as one cannot try to remember something — an introverted process — without consciously directing attention to the recollection.

Instead of using the concept of positional semantics, which determines the content of the functions' work, and aspectual semantics, which determines the material with which a function works, the authors introduced the notion of the psychological role which the psychic function plays. Let us recall how the generalized process of information processing in the psyche unfolds. First of all, what is essential here is that the environment's influence on the psyche has a dual character: load-bearing and resource-bearing. The load side of the environment's influence is perceived by extraverted sensing and manifests itself in volitional withstanding of external pressure. This pressure is evaluated by the function of introverted attitude as undesirable: unpleasant, don't want. Here we see that a quite understandable and causally conditioned change of the subject's functional activity takes place. Next, extraverted intuition switches on as the function capable of providing an understanding of the situation of external (extraverted) pressure and, on this basis, of building an adequate model of the external environment. This model implicitly contains programs of possible behavior as well, but their formation and refinement into algorithms of behavior is accomplished by the thinking function, which in the model may be positionally semanticized as the communicative-probing function (CPF), whose contact with the environment is directed at confirming the adequacy or inadequacy of this stage of adaptation to the environment.

The resource potential of the environment is evaluated by introverted sensing. At this level it acts as the capacity to discriminate the consumer properties of the resource. The evaluation of the result of its work is performed by extraverted emotionality — and above all this is the positive emotion as confirmation of the resource's correspondence to the need: pleasant, like it, want it. Of course, if the resource proves defective, the emotional reaction will be negative; but the initial meaning of a resource is connected with the satisfaction of a need — that is, with its positive realization, which stands in correspondence with the positive emotional reaction. After this, introverted intuition switches on, connected with the awareness of the subject's inner world, which provides the inclusion of the resource into the internal system of subjectivity. Introverted intuition is, among other things, connected with the perception of time, and provides the allotting of time for the consumption of the resource and its technological obtaining — for which extraverted thinking, or technology, is responsible. Clearly, even the plucking of fruit from a tree must, at the level of socionic generalization, be regarded as technology, as activity. This part of information processing is provided by the realization-technological function — extraverted and rational. Here the editor cannot give a more extended explanation, and therefore refers the interested to [2]. The proposed variant of a natural-scientific explanation of the change of functional vertness and -nality, relying on a small number of primary assumptions-axioms, reflects the evolutionary processes of the adaptation of the living to the environment.

The authors clearly want to say something good about socionics, but do it somehow altogether artlessly... "demonstrated... a different development" — not quite literary; the authors do not take into account the compatibility of words. The authors wrote "intra psychic" [as two words] — most likely this is an error; if they mean what happens inside the psyche, i.e., "intrapsychic," then it is written as one word — though spell-checkers have not entered this word and count it an error — rather than "inside psychic processes." But since the authors' thinking differs strongly from mine, I am not 100% sure here. Of course, this is a trifle. I am after something else — the distinction between the psychic and the psychological, since in this place these two terms come into too close contact. "A description of the mechanism of internal psychological processes" is not right, since what is inside the psyche is psychic, while the description of a type's behavior is psychological. In general, this praise looks childishly naive — it does not sit well with a scientific context.

**Authors:** But what interests us is the search for the model most closely approximating the phenomenon of the psychological type. On the one hand, we rely on common sense, on the logic of constructions. In particular, socionic psychology as such relied on Jung. The ideology and content of socionics were built up relying on Jung and on existing psychological knowledge and conceptions.

**Editor:** This is the authors about themselves. O sancta simplicitas! O holy simplicity! How well the authors think of themselves. Here is common sense, and the logic of constructions (there is such a thing, it turns out), and direct reliance on Jung, and — most importantly — reliance on existing psychological knowledge-conceptions. And here is the result: the ideology and content of psi-socionics in all its splendor. The only thing that let the authors down was their frankly weak preparation in methodology. It is like taking wood of precious species and trying, with a blunt axe, to make Vietnamese beads out of it. The result may prove unpredictable.

But what is it, then, that does not satisfy the authors in Model A?

**Neophyte:** If I have understood correctly, from all that has been said it follows that a person who wants to master socionics as a method cannot and probably should not use the literature written by representatives of the various schools?

**Authors:** This literature undoubtedly fulfills its popularizing task. That is, anyone interested in questions of the psychological arrangement of the human being may read such literature with great benefit to himself — if he is going to occupy himself only with his own development, his transformation and self-investigation.

**Editor:** True, for the "ordinary person," in the absence of fish even a crayfish is a fish — but it would still be better if he read Aušra instead of the "gruel for the poor folk" that used to be cooked in certain almshouses in the old days and differed little from swill. Alas, transformation may lead to deformity, and self-investigation may prove ineffective.

**Authors:** If, however, he wants to master this method and apply it to render help to other people, then the question arises how reliable, interconnected, and reflective of the observed phenomena the material he has taken will be. Because, as we said, socionic psychology is a rather powerful instrument of influence, of suggestion. Therefore harm may also be done here.

**Editor:** In the underlined reasoning the authors reach the level of methodology and touch on three methodological parameters: reliability, interconnectedness, the capacity to reflect observed phenomena. Reliability has a direct relation to the problem of truth. On this score every philosophical school has its own notion, and for now we will not discuss the problem of truth. In the practical sense, reliability is provided by proof, which consists of arguments and testimonies. Arguments, in their turn, rely on facts and logical coherence. On the other hand, material may be interconnected but not true, as is the case in fairy tales, myths, or in mystifications and false testimony. And, conversely, a text may be incoherent, clumsy, ragged — yet carry an important message. Thus interconnectedness does not guarantee reliability. As for the capacity to reflect observed phenomena, this condition is necessary but not sufficient, because one and the same phenomenology may be explained in many ways. As a result, the ascertainment of theoretical truth proves to be by no means a simple process. This is one of the causes of the appearance of a significant number of different readings of the socionic texts of Aušra Augustinavičiūtė by the various socionic schools. The authors' text analyzed by the editor also belongs to this category. And the editor is trying to establish how reliable and coherent this text is, and how well it reflects the observed phenomena. After the work performed, I can say with great confidence that the coherence of this text is far from 100%. As for reliability — much work remains to be done on that.

**Authors:** Speaking of these roles (the psychological functions), we shall describe their properties independently of which particular psychic functions occupy these roles in the concrete type. That is, we shall describe not the concrete type but the model of the type.

**Editor:** Clumsily written, but the authors' intention is understandable — and yet it is realized imprecisely. And this is because the authors evidently did not understand the most important thing in Jung's discovery — the principle of the positioning of functions in the psyche. The point is that Jung himself did not fully understand his discovery and never fully reflected on it. Official psychologists did not understand it and to this day do not. In his rich clinical practice Jung noticed, detected, discovered that each of the generalized psychic functions — sensing, intuition, thinking, or emoting — may in different people occupy different places: namely, leading, auxiliary, or subordinate. These concepts express the empirical positional semantics of the psychic functions. This was the result of empirical observation. The remarkable discovery of Aušra Augustinavičiūtė was that she went further and presented the positioning of the functions explicitly as a technological chain in which each function occupies a strictly defined place. This discovery was embodied in the "AJ" model. This was no longer an empirical but an intuitive-constructive stage in the understanding of positioning. The place in the technological chain of IM may also be understood as what the function does. But since a function performs its work not in general — that is, without any connection with the others — but within the technological chain, one may speak of the semantics of positioning. Such positioning is called in psychology quasi-spatial, since the subject is not physical but semantic spaces. However, by force of the semantic parallelism of language and reality, such positioning is filled with meaning, since it is connected with sequence. Alongside the positional, each function has an aspectual semantics, which answers the question "with what does the function work; what does it process?" The authors expressed this thought very approximately and, certainly, very foggily, by opposing psychic and psychological functions — with psychic functions able, in the authors' thought, to act in the role of the psychological ones. Do the authors really not sense the semantic and functional pile-up here?

Let us sort out the difference between these terms. The psychological is what is connected with the manifestations of the personality, with its behavior in society. And the psychic is what is connected with the individual, what is built into physiology. The eye is a physiological organ, and vision is a psychic function. Reading, in which vision is essentially used, but which is connected with a conscious relation to the content of a text tied to social life, constitutes a psychological function. Psychic functions provide the psychological phenomenology, but it is not reducible to them. Thus the psychic acts as an intermediary between physiology and psychology. But one must always remember the dialectical rule: the higher cannot be reduced to the lower. In our case this means: the psychological is not reducible to the psychic; the psychic is not reducible to the physiological. So one must work with these terms carefully. Hence the authors' expression "...there are eight psychological functions, that is, these are the roles in the psyche which the psychic functions may occupy" is perceived very foggily. There is no such usage of these terms in psychology, and it might even have become a discovery of the authors — "psychic functions in the role of psychological ones" — but for that one must know psychology considerably more deeply than the authors demonstrate. To say of psychic functions that they play roles, instead of saying that they perform certain work and issue a certain product — is that not too "theatrical" and romantic? The functions themselves, as superstructured over physiological organs, are psychic; but, relying on them, the personality unfolds its psychological phenomenology. The distinction of the concepts of positional and aspectual semantics at once puts everything in its place and does not require the introduction of the not very comprehensible terminology connected with the psychological roles of the functions.

Note also the phrase "That is, we shall describe not the concrete type but the model of the type." What is wrong here? Here the authors permit themselves carelessness in the shaping of the thought. Speaking of the "concrete type," the authors of course mean its model; and behind "the model of the type" here hides the description of the structure of Model A.

**Authors:** We are now speaking of the psychic functions — that is, the functions of sensation, intuition, feeling, and thinking in their extra- and intro-versions. In this, when their informational component is emphasized — that is, the information which the functions consume and issue — they are called aspects of informational metabolism (exchange), or simply aspects.

**Editor:** With the authors it turns out that the very same objects — the psychic functions — are both themselves and, "when their informational component is emphasized," aspects of informational metabolism. But they also become psychological functions when they perform social roles. This is an example of muddle. First: Aušra spoke of informational aspects (IA), not of aspects of informational metabolism. These are different concepts. The concept of IA simultaneously points to the highly generalized parameters of real objects, to the signal codes of these parameters in various media, and to the classes of these objects used by the subject to create his own model of the external environment. Informational metabolism, on the other hand, is the processing of information in the subject's psychic structures. Metabolism as a process may have its own parameters and aspects — such as speed, density, efficiency coefficient, etc. — but that is an entirely different class of concepts. IM is directly connected with aspectual information, which within the IA correlates with concrete phenomena and objects. It is with these that the socionic functions deal — not with the IAs themselves. Thus, for example, when one speaks of eating a plate of soup, no one, of course, is going to eat the plate itself. The term "IA" is homonymous and points simultaneously both to the signal representation of the generalized parameters of objective entities and to their informational representation in the subject's psychic structures as classifying categories.

The IAs are objective entities that are realized in the world in the form of concrete phenomena and objects, and do not depend on human consciousness. In this, IA and SF [socionic function] are different entities, whose non-distinction and mixing in the socionic context is inadmissible.

The difference between the editor's theory and the authors' consists in this: with the editor, the psychic function remains itself but has two parameters: the positional (it determines what the function does, occupying a concrete place in the structure of the psyche) and the aspectual (what information the function processes). With the authors, the psychic function has no positional semantics but performs "psychological" roles — which, apparently, it could do while located in any place of the structure. This is a rather strange use of the concept of role. Though a role is a variety of functioning, and hence a role in the structure of the model is a synonym of function. If one now performs a synonymic substitution in the authors' definitions, it turns out that a psychic function performs the function of a psychological function. And here the question arises: what sort of construction is this? Why should the psychic functions not do their own work, and the psychological ones theirs? However, one may try to seek arguments in favor of a double, multi-level assignment of the functions. Thus the leg, being an anatomical organ, performs the function of standing and locomotion. These are functions belonging to the category of extraverted thinking — that is, they are elements of a potential technological process. However, a psychological function the legs by themselves cannot perform — it is performed, as an element of the social structure, by the personality. Analogously, a psychic function cannot perform a psychological role, by definition, since that is the prerogative of the personality.

So which functions are in the socionic model — psychic or psychological? To somehow motivate the psychological character of such roles, the authors choose names for them that partly imitate people's psychological behavior: dominant, auxiliary, ignoring, blind spot, creative, suggestive, LPS, mobilizational. However, if a person dominates in some situation, that belongs to the psychological level. Whereas the dominance of a function in the psychic structure belongs to psychic phenomenology. The same applies to such hypothetical properties of the SF as the capacity to help other functions, to ignore something, to fail to notice something, to be an instrument of creativity, or to let through suggestive signals. We see that anthropomorphic terminology can suggest a psychological understanding of the work of the psychic functions.

**Authors:** Let us begin with the psychic function of sensation.

**Neophyte:** Is that random, or is there some sequence?

**Authors:** No — one may consider the sequence of the aspects in an entirely arbitrary order. I hold to that order of the psychic functions which seems to me to reflect best the structure and development of the psyche.

**Editor:** Since the authors have not built a system of the aspects, they can speak of an absence of order in their set. "Seems" is a reflection of the authors' failure to work through this subject matter.

## Conclusion

THERE IS NO NEED TO REINVENT LOGIC AND PSYCHOLOGY — IT IS BETTER TO USE CORRECTLY WHAT ALREADY EXISTS.

THERE IS NO NEED TO COMPROMISE SOCIONICS WITH HOME-GROWN INVENTIONS IN A SPHERE WHERE THE SOCIONICS INSTRUCTOR IS NOT A PROFESSIONAL.

THE PRESENT TEXT IS A VIVID EXAMPLE OF THE AMATEUR DEVELOPMENT OF A PSYCHOLOGICAL THEME BY INTELLIGENT PEOPLE WHO ARE NOT PROFESSIONALS IN THE SUBJECT AREA OF PSYCHOLOGY. THE RESULT IS A MASS OF ORIGINAL THOUGHTS DISTORTING THE ESSENCE OF THE SUBJECT OF INVESTIGATION — WHAT IN PHILOSOPHY IS ASSIGNED TO THE CATEGORY OF METAPHYSICS. THE BOOK MAY BE OF INTEREST TO RESEARCHERS OF THE METHODOLOGICAL COMPLEXES OF NON-PROFESSIONAL SCIENTISTS.

THE BOOK IS NOT RECOMMENDED FOR NEOPHYTES.

**Authors (to the Neophyte):** So you too have stepped onto the path of socionic speculations. Many beginning socionists break lances over this question.

The authors underscore their resolve in this matter with a reference to the famous phrase of Abraham Lincoln: "This path is not our path; our path is another path."
