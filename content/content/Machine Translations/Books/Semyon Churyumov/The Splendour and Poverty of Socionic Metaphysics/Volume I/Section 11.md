Below follows the original text of some of Evgeny Litrovnik's "STUPID STORIES" with my embedded semi-comments. These comments should not be regarded as criticism of any kind — they are nothing more than a literary genre: a game of schizo-ping-pong with the author. The story contains spelling errors, but there is no point in correcting them — they are its ornaments.

Litrovnik's text is, in literary-stylistic terms, utterly brilliant; its semantic part is built on the logic of the absurd (LA), while the stylistics belong to the category of schizoid plaiting, as, for example, in Mark Twain's story "My Grandfather's Old Ram." The genre of the story is logico-psychological fantasy, at whose root lies four-dimensional schizo-intuition. In parentheses a logical commentary is occasionally given, itself humorous in character, since formal logic bears no relation whatsoever to this very amusing and at the same time substantive story. BOM — an abbreviation introduced by the author meaning "meaninglessly mediated multitude" — is precisely the author's genre. The author's subconscious knows that he is about to work with logical nonsense, but his conscious mind has not yet grasped this. We nevertheless emphasize that we are dealing with logical nonsense — but from the intuitive point of view it is a texture filled not with one but with many meanings, and an intuitive will manage to understand a great deal over and above the logic. As, for example, in the paintings of Salvador Dalí. All of Litrovnik's "STUPID STORIES" deserve deep analysis, since in them, in a specific form, his genuinely profound views and developments in socionics are set out, often more than outweighing what most socionics authors have proposed within normative syntax. However, the form in which Litrovnik's ideas are set out creates difficulties for their perception, understanding, and adaptation to science in general and to psychology in particular. Unlike most socionists, Litrovnik has professional training in invention and rationalization, which to a considerable degree ensures his understanding of the methodological foundations of science.

The name of this variety of socionics — "ZK-esque" — comes from the fact that Litrovnik introduced into the assessment of the manifestation of psychic radicals in Model A, besides the percentage of the functional maximum, special quantities: Z (zet), K (ka), X (iks). Z is when the functional maximum turns out to be below 0%. K is when the functional maximum goes beyond 100%. X is the management of variable volumes. If a functional radical is assessed as ZK, the person turns out to be beyond social control — "couldn't care less about anything," as the author puts it. ZK is such an original feature of Litrovnik's theoretical constructions that it fully justifies putting this symbol into the name of his variety of understanding socionics. The author himself calls the behavioral parameters he has found _rhythms_.

The key element of Litrovnik's methodological complex, besides his knowledge of the theory and practice of invention, is the rapid intuitive identification of concepts from different subject areas and the use of one terminological series to service a subject area for which it was not originally intended. In itself this is an interesting phenomenon of reinterpreting terminology, of filling it with meanings different from its original use. But since the new specification is not given, only presumed, this leads to interference between the old and new semantic fields, which makes rational understanding of the text difficult. One may say that Litrovnik, intuitively identifying basic concepts from very different systems of categorizing reality, violates one of the requirements of a rational approach to studying the world — the requirement of justified semantic specification of the concepts used.
