# Section 8. Nebykova's Neosocionics

> _Socionics has long outgrown the short trousers of preliminary searching and irresponsible intuitive insights. It is high time the authors of socionics books answered — before the socionics community, before methodology, and before the trusting reader — for what they offer. It is high time to rely on scientific methodology, rather than to take as truth everything that, for whatever reason, appears on one's inner screen, or that one is able to pronounce using the unique possibilities of the Russian language._ _(From the reflections of Pfafik the dog)_

**Svetlana Ivanovna NEBYKOVA** Candidate of Philological Sciences, psychologist, socionist. Has practiced socionics since 1992. Her research interests are the development of an original theoretical concept, born of twenty years of experience working in secondary schools, as well as the modeling of human psychological manifestations and the effective application of socionics to conflict resolution, personnel selection, and career choice.

**Editor:** While giving due credit to the professional education of the author of the book _Neosocionics_, and presuming in advance the author's competence in her field of professional interest, the editor — having thoroughly acquainted himself with the book's contents — expresses his extreme astonishment at the factual mismatch between the supposed theoretical results and the book's pretentious title. The book is 95% a muddled retelling of what has long been known in socionics, and 5% hypotheses to the effect that the socionic type — the TIM — "in reality" (whatever that means) consists of two TIMs. The author formulates this differently, saying that the type consists of two models, thereby identifying the type with its model. From a methodological standpoint this is entirely unforgivable, and it instantly turns the supposed scientific text into science fiction — which, of course, can also be interesting. But then, so as not to mislead the inexperienced reader, one should say so outright. The author, however, is apparently unfamiliar with the methodological requirement to state precisely the status of what one intends to do. It would have been perfectly fine had the author declared that she was proposing a new hypothesis and offered a justification for it. Yet nowhere in the text is there any sign that she harbours even the slightest doubt about the infallibility of her intuitive revelations.

Evidently the author also fails to understand that by releasing into the world, under the stamp of absolute truth, results that are not in fact true, she compromises socionics: should her book be read by a professional psychologist, sociologist, or philosopher, he will be outraged by the author's low level of psychological and methodological culture. And he will say — and I have heard it more than once — "all sorts of people write these things." Freedom of speech guarantees the author the opportunity to publish her works; but that same freedom of speech allows the editor to say everything he thinks about the author's work. Unfortunately, everything in the author's book that is true is banal — a retelling, needed by no one, of what has long been known — while everything that is not banal turns out, on inspection, to be untrue. Still, let me note the author's positive traits: she is industrious and observant — her tenacious eye catches many external typological features — but, regrettably, this part of her work has remained a half-finished product.

Frankly, one could have declined to react to this work at all, since it contains almost no positive results. But I have spent many years editing books and analyzing the content of socionics works from a methodological standpoint, and I am simply interested in figuring out how the author constructs her thought, and then telling others about it, so that they may take note of the methodological defects of the author's work and take them into account in their own research. This is especially useful for young scholars who may not suspect the existence of methodological requirements for scientific research and are already prepared to present the world with the results of their own insights. The fact is that socionic concepts strike many beginners as exceptionally simple, and new generations of socionists joyfully — sometimes "with great fanfare" — rush to bless the world with their discoveries, without troubling to learn what their predecessors did, and which rakes they stepped on.

I am therefore, after all, grateful to the author for the opportunity she has given me to analyze her intuitive revelations and what she apparently considers something like arguments.

I acquired Nebykova's book last of all — on 23 September 2009, at the 25th socionics conference — which is why I placed the article on Neosocionics closer to the end, in order of the material's arrival; though perhaps it would have looked more striking nearer the beginning. On reflection, however, I decided to leave it where it landed. Let readers first become acquainted with milder variants of assessment of research texts, since most of those texts are, after all, written from positions of a higher level of familiarity with methodology, and in each work I try to preserve the "rational kernel" and find as much of the positive as possible. Unfortunately, in this work I was unable to find even a minimum of useful information, apart from certain physiognomic observations — and even those somewhat fragmentary. Therefore, offering the author every possible apology, I place below an exceptionally harsh commentary — above all as a lesson to future researchers, and, as a certain well-known literary hero would say, "not for gain, but solely _par la grandecha de la terra_." And, I will add from myself: for the greatness of SOCIONICS. Nothing personal…

**Advertising blurb:** This book will prove unusual and will even astonish many readers, for it proposes a new paradigm of socionic knowledge based on the original idea of the duality of type. The author offers an original and productive approach to socionics, viewing the personality type as a structure consisting of two psychological models: the basic and the programmatic. The manifestation of these models correlates with temperament types and is reflected in a person's appearance and behavior. As a result, the informational portrait of the personality becomes richer, more detailed, more dynamic, and intertype relations are supplemented with curious nuances.

**Editor:** Indeed, this book differs greatly from everything I have had occasion to read before. It astonished me with its exceptional virginity with respect to methodology — and this after twenty years during which the journal _Socionics, Mentology and Personality Psychology_ published a multitude of articles on methodological matters. Apparently all of them passed the author by. Perhaps for that reason — or perhaps for others — the author's central idea, that the socionic type consists of two socionic types, is presented as a truth requiring almost no argumentation. And yet all that was needed was the merest trifle: to declare her idea as a hypothesis, gather statistics, and provide the reader with a base of arguments.

Of course, the first question is: "Why NEO?" It would be easy to understand had the author titled her book _Sketches Toward a Portrait of Socionics_ or, better still, _How I Understand Socionics_ — but _Neosocionics_? Is it really so utterly NEO? For that, there must be weighty grounds. Still, let us take things in order…

Yes, there are correct passages in Nebykova's book — mostly copied from others, it is true — but even they are so diluted with poorly substantiated claims that the book's value is reduced to zero, and the author's undertaking turns out not to exceed the bounds of graphomania. A beginner in socionics will only be misled by this book; to an experienced socionist it is simply of no use.

What follows is the editor's correspondence dialogue with the author.

---

**Author:** _Psychological modeling is the next step in the development of typology._

**Editor:** And what, pray, was socionics doing before Nebykova? What kind of modeling? Was it not psychological? Does the author truly believe she has discovered a new kind of modeling in socionics, and that what she is about to offer the socionics community therefore deserves the epithet NEO? Note also that the author gives no definition of the concept of "psychological modeling" as she understands it, so this self-designation is unserious and pretentious. Moreover, psychology used modeling as a general scientific method even before socionics. So what kind of modeling was psychology engaged in?

**Author:** _The book examines the human type as a structure consisting of two psychological models: the basic and the programmatic. They are connected with the individual's external appearance and behavioral characteristics. Manifestations of the basic and programmatic models correlate with temperament types. The temperament type of the basic model may coincide with that of the programmatic model, in which case the individual is perceived as the bearer of a single, vividly expressed temperament. When they do not coincide, one of the temperaments predominates in different periods of life; the second temperament is, as it were, muted, though the overall picture is mixed — or rather, integrated._

**Editor:** This passage, perfectly normal in literary terms, raises a whole series of methodological questions. If a person has a type, what — from the author's point of view — does modeling it mean? If the author considers a model to be a depiction of the structure of psychic functions, then is not such a model, however complex, still something unified — namely a model, say, "N" (for Nebykova)? Just a model consisting of two parts… Why must a type be modeled by two models? And if two, why not three, four, and so on? Of course, anyone who knows socionics can guess, or at least suppose, that the two socionic Model-A's are meant. The author is confident that she has discovered what escaped the attention of socionics' discoverer, Aušra Augustinavičiūtė. That is entirely possible — but weighty proofs are needed.

In that case Nebykova would have to admit that the socionic type of a person rests not on 8 generalized functions but on 16, since each socionic model involves 8 functions. It has by now been proven [UChK] that there exist exactly 8 generalized psychic functions, and the socionic type represents their differing accentuation.

But let us not rush ahead…

Note also that the author gives no definition of "type," apparently considering the concept self-evident. Yet if the author lays claim to NEO, such clarifications matter — the more so since it is precisely the type that is the object of the author's innovations. The author begins with the phrase: "The book examines the human type as a structure consisting of two psychological models: the basic and the programmatic." That is, what matters to the author is only that the type must consist of two psychological models. Does the author realize the jargon-like quality of this expression — the semantic carelessness in the use of words and concepts? Does the author appreciate that she is identifying type with model? Alas, the difference between these concepts is the same as between an object and the word that designates it. Type has ontological status; a model, informational status. A type is what the researcher discovers in reality; a model is what the researcher uses to reconstruct the type in thought. Does the author know that psychologists now have more than three hundred typologies at their disposal? Which one is she talking about? One can guess, of course, that the socionic typology is meant — but how can one speak of models before saying which types one intends to model? Thus the author's claim that a type consists of two psychological models may be understood to mean that the psychological type considered in socionics consists of two socionic types. The logical incorrectness of such a claim is obvious. It is equally obvious that the author does not grasp certain semantic shades of the concepts she uses. And this casts a shadow of distrust over all her ideas, conclusions, and results. But let us continue examining the author's text.

**Author:** _The proposed approach makes it possible, when determining a specific person's type, to take into account the range of his real capabilities in professional activity and in communication._

**Editor:** Does Model A not take into account the range of a person's real capabilities in professional activity and communication? If the author believes it does not, that should have been substantiated. What can the author do with her model that Model A cannot? A point-by-point list would be in order.

**Author:** _On the basis of psychological modeling, a detailed and profound analysis has been carried out of the activities of enterprises, firms, and agencies working in the service sector. The results of the analysis are presented in tables reflecting the typological composition of specific firms._

**Editor:** So the author thinks of her work — but does it correspond to reality? I note in passing that when, as an editor, I encounter word combinations like "detailed and profound analysis," I suggest the author strike one of the "epithets," since here they coincide in meaning. Can an analysis be profound but not detailed, or detailed but not profound? Fine, one may class this among minor quibbles — but trifles of this sort cast a shadow on the character of the author's thinking, and consequently on the reliability of her text.

**Author:** _The appendix presents 184 photographs illustrating the manifestation of typological characteristics in the appearance of bearers of various psychological models. The main features of appearance are compiled into a table, which is also given in the appendix._

**Editor:** Apparently the author believes that in the photographs presented, everyone will be able to see the signs of the two socionic models she speaks of. Well then, we shall see…

**Author:** _Socionics studies the socion — all of humanity. Its task is to describe 16 personality types and 16 types of relations between them._

**Editor:** Here is an example of a most frivolous phrase: simultaneously banal and wrong. Aušra never identified the socion with all of humanity. Nebykova's statement must therefore be counted an innovation — in which case proofs are required. Or else it testifies to the author's poor acquaintance with the works of Aušra Augustinavičiūtė, in which case her statement must be classed as a gross error — which once again casts a shadow over all of Nebykova's further reasoning.

The second half of the quotation likewise demonstrates the author's idiosyncratic understanding of socionics — at the very least an exceedingly simplified one, not to say primitive. Can Nebykova really be unacquainted with the history of socionics, not knowing that socionics models the structure of the psyche, and only then, having completed that task and theoretically constructed the complete set of 16 models, does it describe those models according to definite rules and discover the existence of relations between the types?

**Author:** _In C. G. Jung's book "Psychological Types" 8 personality types are described. Jung based this division on 8 psychic functions: extraverted thinking, introverted thinking, feeling (extraverted and introverted), sensation (extraverted and introverted), and two intuitive functions, extraverted and introverted. To these eight functions correspond eight personality types with the same names: the extraverted thinking type, the introverted thinking type, the extraverted feeling type, the introverted feeling type, the extraverted sensation type, the introverted sensation type, the extraverted intuitive type, the introverted intuitive type._

**Editor:** Here the author briefly and impermissibly simplistically describes what, in her opinion, Jung did. This description in no way helps the reader; rather, it misleads him. Jung spoke not of eight functions but of only four. Eight functions are Aušra's discovery. The extraverted and introverted attitudes, in Jung's scheme, do not double the number of functions, since they pertain to the type as a whole, not to the functions. So here we are dealing with the author's conjectures — which likewise cannot be counted among the book's merits or its cognitive novelty.

**Author:** _It is important here to stress the terms "extraverted" and "introverted": for Jung the main thing is the extraverted or introverted attitude of consciousness._

**Editor:** That is how the author understands Jung. For Jung, however, all the concepts underlying his typology were important — though of course he assigned an equally important role in describing the type to extraversion and introversion. What matters is that this was his discovery: before him no one had distinguished these concepts, and certainly no one had used these terms for the purpose. But to claim that extraversion and introversion are the "main" concepts for describing the type is, at the very least, a misunderstanding.

**Author:** _With the extraverted attitude of consciousness the individual's attention is directed toward objective circumstances; with the introverted attitude it is directed not toward the object but toward subjective factors._

**Editor:** Does the author really think that introverts do not "direct their attention" toward objective circumstances and toward the object? And that extraverts, correspondingly, ignore subjective factors? This is nothing more than a widely circulated myth arising from a distortion of what Jung actually said on the matter (see the section on Jung). The author, however, accepts it as self-evident. What does "the individual's attention is directed toward objective circumstances" mean? For example: putting on a raincoat and taking an umbrella because it is raining outside; preparing for winter because it will be cold; renovating because plaster is falling from the walls and ceiling; planting a vegetable garden because vegetables have become expensive; tidying the apartment because guests are coming; cooking borscht because the necessary ingredients are at hand. Can all this — and, of course, not only this — not fall within the sphere of interest of both extraverts and introverts? But perhaps the author means something else entirely? Then it should be described explicitly.

And what are "subjective factors"? For example: I don't like short dresses; I want to become an actress; in the mornings I exercise in the wushu style; I cannot help writing poetry; I prefer the company of cultivated people; I love my husband; I hate bureaucrats. Here too it is hard to imagine any — let alone large — differences of principle between the types in question.

**Author:** _Such an understanding of extraversion diverges from the practice, accepted in contemporary psychology, of testing these characteristics with a predominant orientation toward the subject's degree of sociability._

**Editor:** Yes, they diverge, and this has been described repeatedly and comprehensively in the socionics literature. What new thing does the author wish to say about it? In socionics it is known that version (extraversion/introversion) is one of the differentiating features, and in importance it is on a par with all the others. If someone has come to a different conclusion, it must be proven logically, not accepted as self-evident. It is high time for authors in socionics to be demonstrative rather than dwelling at the level of brilliant guesses.

**Author:** _If these two approaches to extraversion and introversion are represented as intersecting logical circles, a significant part of the circles will turn out to be shared, but 20–25% will be non-coincidences. The non-coincidences mean that not all sociable people are extraverts and not all reserved individuals are introverts._

**Editor:** This question has been discussed many times in socionics, and socionists know — though not fully — how the true understanding of extraversion and introversion, restored in socionics, differs from the several understandings of these terms in official psychology. But let us return to the author's logical circles. What is she talking about? Approaches, it seems. But what does an intersection of approaches mean? It means that in some respects they coincide and in others they do not. The first approach, as the author has framed it, proceeds from the TIM-bearer's orientation toward objective circumstances or subjective factors, while the second assesses the difference between these types, as the author writes, by degree of sociability. Ponder the definitions offered here. These are two different approaches between which, in the terms proposed, no intersection can be found. This means that the "logical circles" the author speaks of do not intersect. I hardly know what one could object here, but let me try to "play for the author." Perhaps she slipped, intuitively, from comparing approaches to comparing the results each of them might yield. Then, comparing the sets of people selected according to each approach, we might find that the TIMs of the people in those sets partly coincide and partly do not. There our circles could indeed do their work — but that, from the standpoint of logic, is an entirely different situation. The author, unnoticed by herself, substituted the thesis, thereby violating the very first axiom of logic — the axiom of identity. And, of course, behind the estimate of 20–25% there ought to stand statistical research, but the author gives no indication of any. In private conversation such errors might somehow be compensated; in a text claiming scientific status and theoretical discoveries they are impermissible.

For some psychologists (Eysenck, for example) extraverts coincide entirely with the sociable and introverts with the reserved — but there, after all, this is not the only criterion used.

**Author:** _The situation here is more complex and deeper, although it is true that most extraverts are sociable, whereas most introverts strive to limit their communication in time and in the number of participants. Nevertheless, there are reserved extraverts and sociable introverts. They can be distinguished only by the attitude of consciousness toward objective circumstances or subjective factors._

**Editor:** The situation here is indeed "more complex and deeper" (a pleonasm again — the joint use of words close in contextual meaning), since the author continues to insist that extraversion and introversion "can be distinguished only by the attitude of consciousness toward objective circumstances or subjective factors." This is as much an exaggeration as the unambiguous linkage of version with sociability and reserve. Had the author remembered her intersecting circles, she would not have been so categorical.

Having constructed the intersecting circles, she would have seen that both extraverts and introverts are interested in both objective circumstances and subjective factors. Let us note that this is yet another sign of the author's insufficiently deep understanding of the basic categories of socionics. The author would unquestionably do well to work through a logic textbook and stop committing logical errors.

**Author:** _Naturally, Jung had in mind only the predominance in the individual of the extraverted or introverted attitude: both the extravert and the introvert may, in certain circumstances, behave contrary to their characteristic attitude of consciousness._

**Editor:** When the author writes "naturally," it looks like a gentle pat on the great psychologist's shoulder. It is as if the author has so thoroughly inhabited Jung's mode of thought that she can decide on his behalf how exactly he regarded the concept of version. Naturally, this cannot "go without saying." What is required here is a precise reference to the corresponding passage in Jung's works.

Both the theory and the practice of socionics, however, indicate that version, while having two poles, characterizes the personality as a whole and not its individual acts. Therefore an extravert is always an extravert, whatever acts he performs — for every personality performs any act in its own way. Both extraverts and introverts can be heroes and cowards, law-abiding citizens and swindlers; they can keep faith and betray, suffer and rejoice, be closed and open, swear like troopers or be exquisitely polite, and so on. Version characterizes the personality as a whole, not its individual aspects. Recall the principle of the integrity of personality, so vividly presented in the works of Adler.

**Author:** _The significance of Jung's typology can hardly be overestimated: at the beginning of the 20th century he arrived at the processes of energy distribution across a person's psychic functions, which determine his leading mode of perceiving information about the surrounding world._

**Editor:** Banal praise of Jung from an under-qualified author looks rather vulgar. But the motivation of this praise — that "he arrived at the processes of energy distribution across a person's psychic functions" — is yet another invention of the author's, unfounded and only misleading to the reader. In science, the question of the energetic side of psychic processes was first posed by Freud; he was also the first to identify such blocks of personality structure as ego, id, and superego — and he did so at the beginning of that same 20th century. Thus the author once again demonstrates the incompetence, imprecision, and groundlessness of her reasoning.

It is equally untrue that Jung "arrived at… the leading mode of perceiving information about the surrounding world," since Jung did not use the concept of "information" as a scientific category in his typology. Nor is it clear where the author gets the notion that "the processes of energy distribution across a person's psychic functions determine his leading mode of perceiving information about the surrounding world." Jung said nothing of the kind, and the causal connection of energy with information, from the standpoint of their mutual transformation, has never been seriously investigated by anyone. The concept of information first came to be considered in cybernetics after the works of Shannon and Wiener — but there information was always opposed to energy and matter.

**Author:** _It is no accident that E. Medtner, editor of the Russian edition of C. G. Jung's "Psychological Types," writes that Jung's theory embraces the psychic domain from various sides but is subordinated to "a single intention (the self-regulation of the psyche as an energetic phenomenon)" [Jung, p. 12]._

**Editor:** The point is not at all whether the editor of the Russian edition of Jung said something by accident or not, but that Jung was by no means the first to attempt to solve problems connected with the energetic side of the psyche. In scientific psychology these problems were first formulated by Freud. In fairness, let us note that to Freud's concept of libido as sexual energy Jung — criticizing Freud — opposed his own understanding of libido as a universal energy capable of realization in any human manifestation. Let us add too that Freud's "energetic ideas" were criticized by many other psychologists as well.

**Author:** _A. Augustinavičiūtė took the next step — from eight psychological types to 16, supplementing C. G. Jung's classification with the concepts of rationality/irrationality._

**Editor:** Read that statement closely — "took the next step"… Aušra transformed Jung's typology, unfolding its potential; she by no means confined herself to adding a supplementary differentiating feature! Aušra opened a new scientific direction, though she of course relied on Jung's typology in doing so.

**Author:** _A. Augustinavičiūtė's greatest discovery is her graphical model, which makes it possible to see through which psychic function a person of a given socionic type receives information about the surrounding world, through which function he realizes himself in accordance with the information received, which function is leading in his communication, which is the point of pain, through which function he is most suggestible, and so on — 8 positions in all, 4 of which enter the mental ring (conditionally conscious) and 4 the vital ring (conditionally unconscious). The place of each function in the graphical model determines its energy supply._

**Editor:** One can fully agree with the author that the model of the psyche created by Aušra Augustinavičiūtė is a very great discovery; however, the explication that follows this solemn declaration shows how peculiarly the author understands Model A. It is not a coherent conception of the model of the psyche but a collection of "vivid" yet unsubstantiated details, seen with "the fresh gaze of a child." Yet this gaze is not only "freshly naïve" — it is also inaccurate: in this description one senses a simplified understanding of the functioning of the psyche. The author evidently does not grasp the problem of the semantics of the psychic functions.

**Author:** _The main differences between people are determined by rationality/irrationality._

**Editor:** A few paragraphs earlier Nebykova considered extraversion/introversion the main distinction; now it is rationality/irrationality — evidently not understanding that all the Augustinavičiūtė–Reinin differentiating features are equally important. And again this style of searching for the "main" feature…

**Author:** _A classical description of the rational and irrational types of appearance was given by A. P. Chekhov in the story "The Beauties."_

**Editor:** It is unclear why Chekhov's description should be called classical — because he belongs to the classics? Or because his description conforms to some socionic canon known to Nebykova? Let us pass over the non-typological details and see what Nebykova draws attention to.

**Chekhov:** _"…Sitting down at the table, I glanced at the face of the girl who handed me a glass, and suddenly felt as though a breeze had swept through my soul and blown away all the day's impressions with their tedium and dust. I saw the enchanting features of the most beautiful of the faces I had ever met in waking life or dreamed of in sleep. Before me stood a beauty, and I understood it at first sight, as one understands lightning. The little Armenian girl's beauty an artist would have called classical and severe. It was precisely that beauty whose contemplation, God knows whence, instills in you the certainty that you are seeing regular features; that the hair, eyes, nose, mouth, neck, bosom, and all the movements of the young body have merged together into a single, whole, harmonious chord in which nature has not erred by the smallest line; it seems to you somehow that an ideally beautiful woman must have precisely such a nose as Masha's, straight with a slight arch, such large dark eyes, such long lashes, such a languid gaze; that her black hair and brows suit the tender white color of her forehead and neck as green reeds suit a quiet stream; Masha's white neck and her young bosom are weakly developed, but to sculpt them, it seems to you, one would need an immense creative talent."_

**Editor:** Well, what can one say about the literary merits of the great writer? They are universally known. Chekhov impeccably conveys his impression of what he saw, and in the passage quoted he uses the word "classical" in relation to beauty. But note that he readdresses this epithet to an artist, and of course he does not have in mind rationality or irrationality — which Nebykova tries to see in this description. What, then, is the inexperienced reader supposed to take away? Presumably, that if beauty is classical, it is rational. But alas, this does not correspond to reality. Both rationals and irrationals can be "classically" beautiful. There are no grounds for discriminating against any type whatsoever in regard to beauty.

**Author:** _The girl Chekhov met in the steppe was, by the type of her appearance, a rational._

**Editor:** The author invites you to accept the great writer's description as the classical standard of a rational's appearance. But what grounds are there for this? Earlier Nebykova wrote: "Externally, rationality manifests itself in greater regularity of facial features and in lesser mobility of the face, as well as in lesser fluidity (a certain discreteness) of movements." Clearly, such claims must be proven, not served up as laws of nature of unknown provenance. What does "greater regularity of facial features" mean? Compared to what? To an irrational? Where are the methods for measuring regularity? Where are the statistics? Why does the author not say these are her suppositions?

**Chekhov:** _"…She was still a young girl of seventeen or eighteen, dressed in Russian costume, bareheaded, with a little mantle thrown carelessly over one shoulder… Before I could give myself an account of what I was seeing, I was suddenly seized by the feeling I had once experienced in the Armenian village. The girl was a remarkable beauty, and neither I nor those who were looking at her with me had any doubt of it. If one were to describe her appearance piece by piece, as is customary, the only truly lovely thing about her was her thick, wavy fair hair, loosed and tied on her head with a black ribbon; everything else was either irregular or very ordinary. Whether from a particular manner of coquetry or from short-sightedness, her eyes were narrowed, her nose was irresolutely upturned, her mouth was small, her profile weakly and flaccidly drawn, her shoulders narrow for her years — and yet the girl gave the impression of a real beauty, and looking at her I could convince myself that a Russian face, in order to seem lovely, has no need of strict regularity of features; more than that — had the girl's upturned nose been replaced by another, regular and plastically impeccable like the little Armenian's, it seems her face would have lost all its charm…_

_It was a butterfly beauty, which suits waltzing, flitting about the garden, laughter and gaiety, and which does not accord with serious thought, sorrow, and repose; and it seems that one need only let a good wind run along the platform, or the rain fall, for the fragile body suddenly to fade and the capricious beauty to scatter like pollen from a flower."_

**Author:** _In this example we see the description of the appearance of a girl belonging to the irrational type._

**Editor:** Such is the author's opinion, and she invites the reader to take on faith that everything stands exactly as it appears to the author. But this is a very irrational position — it is hard even to imagine how one can normatively use a fictional excerpt, however beautiful the literary text, as a theoretical construct. Note that Chekhov assigns the second girl, too, to the class of beauties — though, as follows from Nebykova's hints, as an irrational she ought not to qualify.

**Author:** _Extraverts have a more confident gaze than introverts; they radiate energy and usually do not slouch._

**Editor:** Reader! Now you will effortlessly distinguish an extravert from an introvert. Look at a person, and if you notice that his gaze is "more" confident — not simply confident, but "more confident" — and moreover he "radiates energy," and moreover he usually does not slouch, then he is an extravert. If even one feature fails to match this set, he is an introvert. Where do such recommendations come from? From the author's description one can at least draw the unambiguous conclusion that all introverts, without exception, are round-shouldered.

**Author:** _Rationality is connected, as Jung already noted, with the psychic functions of thinking and feeling._

**Editor:** It is astonishing how carelessly and approximately the author builds her thought. First, Jung did not "note" this at all — his intellectual process here was different. His understanding of rationality proceeds from the semantic closeness of the concept of thinking and rational behavior. Indeed, one can find semantic shades bringing together reasonableness and rationality; strictly speaking, the second word may be regarded as a translation of the first into Latin. And since feeling, in Jung's view, is used by the subject to process information alongside thinking, both these functions fall into the category of rationality.

In the sentence under consideration, moreover, the particle "already" is used out of place. Here it shifts the emphasis from a simple statement of fact about Jung's completed scientific research to a temporal marker that is inessential in this case.

But the especially unpleasant moment in this utterance is the incorrect attribution of the concept of rationality as a particular parameter of the psychic functions of thinking and feeling. By all appearances, the author does not understand that rationality is not a separate aspect of these functions but their abstract generalization.

**Author:** _A. Augustinavičiūtė gave them other names: logic and ethics. These functions can be extraverted and introverted._

**Editor:** Unquestionably, Aušra Augustinavičiūtė's renaming of Jung's terms proved an unfortunate idea, but we must be understanding toward the difficulties of the theoretical work Aušra had to perform: she had to travel unbeaten paths, where it was not so easy to avoid certain imprecisions. **Author:** _Extraverted logic is denoted by an unshaded little square. Hence one of its names: white logic. Other names for this function: abstract, theoretical logic. Introverted logic is denoted by a shaded little square (…) — black logic, business logic, investigative logic._

**Editor:** Here we are most likely dealing with an authorial slip and an oversight by the publisher's editor and proofreaders: it is exactly the other way around — introverted logic is denoted by the unshaded square, extraverted by the shaded one. Far worse, however, is that the author uncritically absorbs and accordingly retells to the reader an approximate semantics of the functions in question, remote from precision. Could someone explain to me, for instance, how theoretical logic differs from investigative logic? Yet many socionists think this way, misled by the not-always-successful elaborations of Aušra Augustinavičiūtė's followers. Introverted thinking is what takes place inside the psyche, its result being an algorithm. Extraverted thinking is what is realized outwardly in the form of a technological process — what is done with one's hands. Strange as it may seem, this understanding does not come easily to everyone. But Nebykova, after all, lays claim to NEO — so why does she so readily transmit a defective semantics without, apparently, realizing it?

And of course, the inclusion of the words "black" and "white" in scientific terms introduces semantic overtones inadequate in this case, associated with good and evil, as in the combinations "black" and "white" magic.

**Author:** _Externally, logical types differ from ethical types by the relative development of the upper part of the face: their eyes have a more intelligent expression than those of ethical types, and their brows are often mobile. **Editor:** Fine, the author mixed up the terms — it happens: she wrote "extraverted" instead of "introverted." Errors of this kind greatly reduce trust in the author's text. Unless, of course, this is the author's invention… but then arguments are required.

**Author:** _The eyes of ethical types are emotionally expressive; the forehead is often low, sometimes narrow; the mouth is as a rule large, beautifully outlined, mobile._

**Editor:** This set of features, though incomplete, looks quite plausible. One senses that the author has decent powers of observation — so this work should be brought to a quality conclusion, not rushed into print as a half-finished product.

**Author:** _The psychic function Jung called "sensation" received the name of sensorics. **Author:** _This scheme comprises 4 blocks: EGO (I) — "I know and can"; SUPEREGO (the social I) — "I must"; SUPERID (the social IT) — "I want"; ID (IT) — "I am able."_

**Editor:** Obviously the author borrowed this construct, so there is nothing "NEO" in it. But why SUPERID should be the social IT is nowhere explained — as though there had not been a hundred years of ego psychology from Freud to Aušra Augustinavičiūtė, as though all this were NEO. Evidently the author has no notion of the semantics of the functions and blocks, and does not understand that this semantics must be introduced with justification, not assigned by "intuitive-volitional" decision.

**Author:** _The EGO and SUPEREGO blocks enter the mental ring (the ring of awareness — conditionally); the SUPERID and ID blocks enter the vital ring (unconscious — likewise conditionally: some processes in the vital blocks may be conscious, while some in the mental-ring blocks are not always conscious)._

**Editor:** So far nothing new, apart from additions false at their root — that the mental ring is "the ring of awareness, conditionally," and the vital ring "unconscious, also conditionally." Where does the author get this information? Did she really establish these two facts in a laboratory experiment? In any case Freud, who at first held the ego to be fully conscious and the id unconscious, was later forced to conclude that the ego is not fully conscious and that a considerable part of it is rooted in the unconscious. He also at first held the superego to be fully unconscious, but later inclined to the view that something of the superego does reach consciousness. This reminder is meant to show that things are not so simple with "awareness," and that consciousness itself was and remains a phenomenon inaccessible to scientific study. Hence the "assignments" the author makes as to what shall be conscious and what not look frivolous. As for the maximally generalized psychic functions, no presence of awareness is presupposed there at all. And in any case, drawing conclusions here about their degree of consciousness offhand, at the level of intuitive guesses, is unbecoming for a scholar. Some intellectual or experimental work must be done.

**Author:** _Extraverted sensorics heightens the subject's attention to the world of things. Thanks to his first function he is well oriented in the quality of things and their aesthetic value. **Editor:** This paragraph is a vivid specimen of disinformation — from the standpoint of classical socionics, of course. One gets the impression that the author understands the word "sensorics" somehow differently from everyone else, differently from what is "customary in socionics circles." Sensorics is the function of the subject's perception of the object; it is the very perception of the world of things — and to say that it heightens attention, when at the level of maximal generalization this function is inseparably bound up with attention, is like saying something along the lines of "food increases appetite," "association with people heightens attention to people," "walking improves the ability to keep one's balance," and so on. This is not merely banality — it is obtrusive superfluity. It is the quintessence of graphomania.

**Author:** _The question of terminology stands very acutely in socionics. The names of types by their leading psychic functions, accepted in the specialized socionics literature, are too cumbersome and not comprehensible to everyone. For example, the designation "ethical-sensory extravert" says nothing to the uninitiated._

**Editor:** Yes, the question of terminology in socionics is acute — but not because the functional terms are cumbersome and "incomprehensible to the uninitiated." Socionics is a science, and for the "uninitiated" to find his way in it he must study it and become initiated. These names are meant to indicate with utmost precision the psychological essence of the given type. It is another matter that Aušra Augustinavičiūtė's replacement of Jung's terms "thinking" and "feeling" with "logic" and "ethics," though she gave her reasons, proved unfortunate. In making the substitution Aušra proceeded from the premise that the concept of "thinking" characterizes bearers of all types equally, whereas "logic" points to individual typological differences — "each has his own logic," as it were. But in Jung the positioning of the functions presupposed a link above all with the type, not with character — character presupposing the acquisition of individual differences through learning, including the acquisition of logical and ethical knowledge and habits. Hence it is precisely the position of thinking and feeling in the structure of the psyche, and not acquired knowledge of the techniques of thinking (to which logic belongs), that determines typological differences.

**Author:** _A. Augustinavičiūtė assigned the socionic types pseudonyms drawn from the names of famous historical figures, writers, poets, actors, and literary characters. But this system, for all its one indisputable merit — its effect on the consultee's figurative perception of information — suffers from a whole series of shortcomings. Let us name a few: the influence on the conception of the socionic type of the individual traits of the person chosen as the pseudonym; disagreements among socionists in determining the socionic types of the pseudonyms themselves; the complexity of the psychic characteristics of well-known figures of politics, science, and art; the impossibility of selecting pseudonyms equally well known to representatives of different strata of the population (thus the pseudonym Dreiser, as a rule, means nothing to the young)._

**Editor:** These questions have been discussed many times in the socionics literature, and no consensus was ever reached on them: representatives of different types held to their own arguments. Yet the author, touching on this theme, advances arguments already advanced by others; there is nothing new in her position. As for the use of eponymous terms in science, they are widely employed, though not perceived identically by everyone: recall newtons and megapascals. Incidentally, 1 pascal = 1 newton per square meter. An ethical sensibility may be jarred by this. Still, it is well known that regular practical use of eponymous terms removes possible emotional interference, turning them into habitual working instruments. That is exactly what happened in socionics. The overwhelming majority of people have no neurotic reactions to such names for their types as Napoleon, Robespierre, Stirlitz, and others. Amusing situations do sometimes arise in connection with the pseudonyms Aušra Augustinavičiūtė chose. Someone holds that F. Dostoevsky was not a Dostoevsky but a Hamlet, while the real Dostoevsky was Don Quixote. Others conclude that Napoleon Bonaparte was not a Napoleon but a Zhukov. Someone wants to replace the author's pseudonyms proposed by Aušra Augustinavičiūtė with his own — say, Sherlock Holmes instead of Stirlitz, or Alexander of Macedon or Caesar instead of Napoleon. Clearly a wide field opens up here for individual creativity, and representatives of certain types will not miss such a splendid chance to distinguish themselves. Aušra Augustinavičiūtė's eponymous type names have stood the test of time. No negative consequences have ensued. These names have attained the level of terms and have fully justified their instrumental purpose. Their use adds motivation to the study and dissemination of socionics and has, of course, contributed to its popularization. As for names like Dreiser, of whom the young may not know — let the young raise their cultural level; and many young people promptly try to learn more about the remarkable American writer. This theme is discussed in more detail in [UChK].

**Author:** _Names by leading character trait are also used. Thus the socionic type — the ethical-sensory extravert — was given the pseudonym Hugo. By leading character trait he received the names: Organizer (jocular variant: Master of Ceremonies), the Fervent One, the Enthusiast._

**Editor:** The author evidently does not understand that a leading character trait must be scientifically substantiated, not assigned on the basis of personal impressions. The very variants of "leading traits" she cites — Master of Ceremonies, the Fervent One, the Enthusiast — point to the ambiguity of the concept. As for "organizer" as a leading trait, that capacity is possessed at the very least by bearers of such types as Zhukov, Napoleon, and Stirlitz. The inadequacy of such a name is, in this case, obvious. A comparative table of type names was given above, and the reader can judge for himself their approximateness and scientific incorrectness.

**Author:** _All 16 socionic types are divided into 4 quadras._

**Editor:** Nothing new so far — the author repeats what has long been known.

**Author:** _Having constructed each of the 16 models (by the rules for constructing Model A) and correlated the psychic functions with their role in the given position, we obtain a fairly complete picture of each of the listed types; but before doing so I wish to introduce into the context of the description the concept of "role in the quadra."_

**Editor:** The concept of "role in the quadra" was introduced by Gulenko — so one should raise one's cultural level and cite one's predecessors. As for "a fairly complete picture of each of the listed types," this expression contains a subtle defect that evidently escapes the author's attention. The point is that socionics gives a complete, not a "fairly complete," picture of each type, since all information about the type (about the type — not about the character or the person!) is contained in Model A; though of course one must know how to extract it — that is what socionic theory is for.

As for the "rules for constructing the model," an author laying claim to NEO ought to have explained where they came from — but for that one must first realize that this needs explaining.

**Author:** _I shall begin with a brief summary of a newspaper article, unfortunately lost by me in a sea of papers. It concerned the formation of an ancient cavalry force that did not yet have a clear structure. Nevertheless, in this force the mounted warriors were grouped in fours, and each of the four horsemen had his own role in the arena of military operations. First rode the Hero. His task was to charge the enemy without a thought for danger, drawing the others after him. Behind him rode the Ram. The Ram's task was to protect the Hero's back, to cover him, to guard him. Third came the Harlequin, or Distractor. His task was to tease the enemy, confuse him, throw him off balance. The Harlequin could overtake the Hero or fall behind, circle to the left or right. Witty, quick, on a light horse, he "wound up" the enemy, imposed his own rhythm, knocked him out of his stride. The fourth horseman was the Sensitive One, or Human Antenna._

**Editor:** Now you know the author's key source of inspiration — the structure of an ancient cavalry unit with a Hero, a Harlequin, a Ram, and an Antenna. What words they knew in the ancient cavalry! — all from different cultural infrastructures, which probably never stood side by side even in the Italian comedy. No, the author relies not on the whole experience of human psychology standing behind the discoveries of Jung and Aušra Augustinavičiūtė, but on a newspaper article. Not a bad article — genuinely interesting. But if tomorrow an article appears about a brigade of four cooks — first the soup-man, second the meat-man (specialist in meat dishes), third the salad-man, fourth the pastry-man — there will be a new basis for revising the classical conceptions of socionics.

**Author:** _This very system of interacting horsemen is a good image that can be used to describe the functional interrelations within a quadra. Naturally, the designations of the horsemen are ambiguous. I therefore replace them with participles: the Leading, the Guarding, the Interacting, and the Perceiving._ (emphasis the editor's)

**Editor:** The horsemen make a fine example. But it can have only an oblique relation to socionics. The horsemen's roles are examples of sociotypes, and the correspondence between TIMs and sociotypes is not one-to-one: people of one TIM can be different sociotypes, and conversely, people of one sociotype can have different TIMs. Moreover, imposing military semantics on other kinds of social activity is a typical example of covert manipulation of consciousness. Why, for instance, replace or substitute the division of scientists into generators, selectors, developers, inspirers, implementers, etc., with heroes, rams, and harlequins? As a metaphor in a poetic comparison it will do, but at the level of organizational technology it seems pointless — though observations of this kind could be useful in developing the semantic spectra of sociotypes.

But let us look more closely at the semantics of the "participles" the author has chosen. Note at once that as soon as these participles are capitalized they turn into nouns, since they designate agents.

The Leading — this concept cannot be reduced merely to that of the Leader. A television presenter ["ведущий"], for example, lacks some of the Leader's parameters. Close to the concept of the Leading are: Toastmaster, Boss, Commander, Ataman, Gang-boss, Guide, Seeing-eye, Manager, Pastor, Teacher, Pilot, Boatswain, Head, Elder, Sergeant-major, Chairman, Tour guide, Compère…

The Guarding — the Protecting, Guard, Overseer, Escort, Bodyguard, Physician…

The Interacting — Negotiator, Diplomat, Politician.

The Perceiving — Pupil, Listener, Watcher, Scout, Scholar…

Surveying the semantic fields of the roles Nebykova has chosen, one can see that they do not cover the whole set of social roles, which makes the author's chosen set of roles, at the very least, limited. This means the author will have to make numerous stretches to maintain the logical coherence of her reasoning.

**Author:** _Naturally, any person is capable of being the Leading, the Guarding, the Interacting, and the Perceiving; but here we are speaking of the principal trait of each of the four, corresponding to his functional role in the quadra._

**Editor:** It does not go without saying. Not everyone can be a leader, and so on. At the very least this would have to be proven. In science there is almost nothing that goes without saying.

**Author:** _It is easy to notice that these 4 roles correspond to the four temperament types: the Leading is the choleric, the Guarding the phlegmatic, the Interacting the sanguine, and the Perceiving the melancholic._

**Editor:** An example of a stretch — no such one-to-one correspondence exists. And the author's offhand claim that it is easy to notice does not correspond to reality.

**Author:** _What does the change of terminology give us? The point is that the psychological terms denoting temperament type are oriented toward the person as an object of study, observation, psychological diagnosis. By changing the terminology we lend the description of the temperaments functionality: attention shifts from the question "what kind?" to the question "what for?"_

**Editor:** Here we are dealing with an error which, judging by what has already been written above, is characteristic of the author. The attempt to bind temperament rigidly to sociotype may be assigned to the "childhood disease of hasty identification." A thought flashes through the author's mind, and without troubling herself with serious research she at once takes it for final truth. The same happens with the attempt to bind the TIM to the sociotype. Temperament is broader than any sociotype, since it is a basic, biological characteristic of individuality. It is connected with the evolutionary adaptation of the species _Homo sapiens_, whereas the sociotype appears much later — within the framework of the individual's socialization.

**Author:** _Classical descriptions of the types consider the positive and negative features of each temperament. The sanguine temperament is recognized as the best, since it rests on a strong type of nervous activity and a balance of the processes of excitation and inhibition._

**Editor:** Apples and oranges… What classical descriptions of types is the author talking about? Socionic ones? Then what do temperaments have to do with it? In psychology, evaluations of "better or worse" are not applied to types. The classical temperaments are the result of natural selection, and each has advantages and disadvantages relative to the others — which is why all the temperaments have survived in the human population.

**Author:** _By changing the terminology we thereby come to understand what each temperament type is for. But that is not all: we now look at bearers of the different temperaments not as dwelling in isolation but as participants in interaction. Under this approach the bearer of each temperament type is, on his own, incredibly vulnerable: the choleric does not reckon with danger and does not catch the subtlest signals that allow the future to be foreseen; the phlegmatic, for all his reliability, is late to react; the sanguine is changeable and loves to be the center of attention; the melancholic is anxious, weak, and timorous. **Editor:** This excerpt might have been considered a development of the temperament theme, an attempt at an activity-based profiling of it — but superficial observations are not enough for that; and besides, the interrelations of the temperaments have been investigated and described in the psychological literature in fair detail. The author, however, starts from zero, as on a desert island — as though there had been no I. P. Pavlov, no Teplov–Nebylitsyn institute, no research by Simonov, no classical works by Strelau and many others. And so the author has decided to change the terminology. If I understand rightly, we must now call cholerics "the Leading," and so on down the list. Yes, when a dilettante-graphomaniac takes up a matter, expect surprises.

**Author:** _Let us transfer this approach to the contemporary situation of business partnership. Suppose several people decide to found a firm. **Author:** _I have studied the management structures of factories and travel agencies, observed the administrations of schools, universities, and education departments. **Editor:** Here is an example of a person who studied management structure and then for some reason decided that LEADER should be replaced by LEADING. **Editor:** The author senses the defects of her terminology and tries to neutralize its negative impression on the reader by proposing that we "not notice the full range of the terms' meanings."

**Editor:** Can the author, holder of a philology degree, really not understand that by choosing participles as terms she has thereby nominalized them — that is, converted them, as linguists say, into nouns — and that they now denote agents?

**Author:** _The choleric temperament characterizes the Leading one and permits the assumption that the individual has the model of the Organizer, Marshal, Politician, or Administrator. **Editor:** From the very start the author bound temperament unambiguously to the quadral roles she herself assigned, and fell into the trap of hasty identification. She is completely sure of her rightness and seems incapable of understanding where her error lies.

**Author:** _It is precisely the temperamental characteristics, being basic to the personality, that are determined most easily. Tradition helps here, as do psychological tests approved and corrected by long practice of use._

**Editor:** Psychologists long ago concluded that pure temperaments are rare, and proposed a formula for combined temperament summing the percentage shares of each: T = Ch% + S% + Ph% + M%, where the letters correspond to the first letters of the temperaments' names. So the author's optimism about the ease of diagnosing the TIM via temperament lacks sufficient grounds. Nevertheless, Gulenko's result is quite effective.

**Author:** _Thus the parallels drawn by socionists between temperament types and socionic types do not allow continuity to be established between psychology and socionics, hindering dialogue between representatives of these sciences._

**Editor:** The author apparently believes that before her all socionists erred in establishing this or that correspondence between TIMs and temperaments, and that she alone has found the truth here. So far, however, she has presented no sufficiently weighty arguments for it.

The author does not understand the relation between socionics and psychology. Socionics is a science, since it possesses a generalizing theory, whereas psychology, for some 200 years now, still remains a collection of empirically found recipes — in most cases, admittedly, very effective ones. Psychologists to this day cannot agree among themselves on the justification of the classical temperaments. Suffice it to recall the promising experimental research of I. P. Pavlov, who never managed fully to think through the possible conclusions from the three parameters of the nervous system he discovered: strength, mobility, stability. For if these three parameters are independent, there should be 8 temperaments, not 4. It appears he fitted his conclusions to a pre-set number. Eight temperaments were discovered by Dutch researchers, though on a different theoretical basis. The most reliable at present is the physiological justification of the nature of the temperaments carried out by Simonov. But the question remains: who, after all, is right — and what is Truth in psychology?

**Author:** _On the interconnection of external and psychological characteristics C. G. Jung wrote: "The inexplicable unity of the living being is the reason why a physical feature is not merely physical and a mental one not merely mental, for the continuity of nature knows nothing of those incompatibilities and ruptures which the human mind is forced to overcome in order to know anything at all. The separation of body from soul is an artificial operation, a discrimination which undoubtedly rests more on the peculiarity of the cognizing mind than on the essence of things. In reality the mutual penetration of bodily and mental features is so deep that from the properties of the body we can not only draw far-reaching conclusions about the qualities of the soul, but from mental peculiarities we can also judge the corresponding bodily forms… The safer path is the one from outer to inner, from the known to the unknown, from the body to the soul" [Jung, pp. 11–12]._

**Editor:** This is a good quotation from Jung. It shows that the great Jung understood the complexity of the problem and proposed no "obvious" solutions. And, however strange it may seem to inexperienced psychologists, the hint is offered by philosophical elaborations in methodology. In dialectical philosophy it has long been known that the higher forms of the motion of matter are not reducible to the lower. Hence the long-established conclusion that psychic phenomena are not reducible to physiological ones, however close the connection between them. As for the continuity of nature, here Jung is mistaken: nature contains leaps, quantum transitions from some states to others. Body and soul are different entities: the soul is not reducible to the body.

But when the great psychologist says that "from the properties of the body we can not only draw far-reaching conclusions about the qualities of the soul, but from mental peculiarities we can also judge the corresponding bodily forms," he comes right up to informational processes — though in his time the concept of information was not yet a scientific category. The theory of informational metabolism makes it possible to understand that the correspondence between the physiological and the psychological is informational in nature. The informational modulations circulating in the substrate of the soul leave their traces on the physical body. They are, however, not isomorphic but only homeomorphic to the processes of the soul. A good analogy here is the footprints a person leaves on the ground: these prints are connected with the person and can tell much about him, but they are not identical with him. Analogously, psychic processes leave their traces in the parameters of the body: the qualities of the musculoskeletal system, speed of reactions, facial expression, physiognomy, plasticity of movement, gesticulation, timbre of voice, and so on.

**Author:** _At the current level, genetic research allows conclusions to be drawn about the psychological, behavioral, and bodily particularities of a specific person. **Editor:** There is no such tendency. There are individual socionists who hold differing opinions on the matter.

**Editor:** Bukalov's position is easy to understand: he is against hasty identifications of physiognomic and other external features with the TIM. We know — we've been through it. **Author:** _(on Filatova) At the same time, on p. 55, where portraits of Keepers are given, the faces in the middle horizontal row do indeed have the features of ethical types and introverts, yet they can hardly be assigned to the sensory types. **Editor:** The author criticizes a colleague's opinion — but what can she set against it? Alas, only an opinion…

**Author:** _As a result, the absence of reliance, in determining the socionic type, on temperament type and on features of appearance, including physiognomic ones, turns typing into a rather subjective process. As for subjectivity in typing, it cannot be avoided in the way the author indicates: any number of people can err jointly. Thus, for example, Aušra once judged Pushkin a Napoleon, and ever since, most socionists have held to that opinion. But recently T. N. Prokofieva adduced a considerable number of arguments for Pushkin being a Huxley — which is far more plausible, though not everyone agrees.

In adolescence a strengthening of the EGO block begins, with its program-potential (PPF) and communicative-probing (CPF) functions. **Author:** _Children from 7 to 11 are typologically transparent; they are more harmonious, since they proceed mainly along the basic model. **Editor:** If the guess expressed in the previous comment about the shift of accents from SUPERID to EGO is correct, then the author's observation described above acquires a completely different meaning and becomes valuable empirical evidence of that transition from childhood programs to adult ones.

**Author:** _The presence of precisely two models in a person's psychic structure is confirmed not only by changes in appearance and behavior: the coincidence of any features in both models sharply intensifies those qualities, while non-coincidence makes both appearance and behavior smoother._

**Editor:** Regular age-related changes in appearance and behavior are not, purely logically, obliged to entail a change of TIM — otherwise any change of appearance, such as the transition from nursery age to kindergarten age, from kindergarten to school, and so on, as well as gain or loss of weight, illness, or muscle growth from athletic training, would have to entail a change of TIM. On the other hand a change of TIM, purely logically, must entail a change of appearance and behavior, since the TIM embraces all spheres of individual phenomenology, including physiognomy, facial expression, plasticity, and kinesics. If this is right, then in the process of maturation the TIM would have to change first, and this would entail the change of appearance and behavior. From a theoretical standpoint, this is an exceptionally interesting problem: how, in the course of evolution, could such a mechanism have formed? Why can a person not have one TIM throughout his life — or, conversely, why must a person have only one TIM? How many TIMs can a person have in all? If a person can have several TIMs, can they be present simultaneously, or only successively in different age or biographical periods? The simultaneous action of two TIMs — would that not be a variant of schizophrenia? These questions are considered in more detail in [UChK, ch. 5]. But if everything is explained by a change of programmatic accents within a single TIM, the hypothesis of two types within one becomes unnecessary.

**Author:** _If both of a child's models, basic and programmatic, are rational, he is a "rational squared." He has a sharp, geometrically regular profile; his face is not very mobile. **Author:** _In first place here I would put tests for determining temperament type, above all two tests by H. **Editor:** It is not so at all! It is known of Eysenck that, being a profound and principled scientist, he attempted to give an explicit definition of extraversion–introversion through a series of more concrete concepts subordinated to them hierarchically and representing their semantic spectrum. As a result he matched extraversion with the following series of more concrete concepts subordinate to it: sociability, liveliness, activity, persistence, and striving for success. Hence in his questionnaire one meets such items as "Do you consider yourself a cheerful person?", "Are you a talkative person?", or "Are you able to give free rein to your feelings and have a rollicking good time in noisy company?" Accordingly, a positive answer to these questions is supposed to mean you are an extravert, a negative one that you are an introvert. Socionics operates with a conceptual toolkit that makes strictly grounded descriptions of personality types possible. True, this does not mean all the type descriptions found in socionics books are accurate — those are the growing pains of a new science. Nevertheless there exists an invariant of the descriptions, concentrated in the socionic models, and it gives grounds to disagree with Eysenck's understanding of extraversion–introversion. Thus, for example, all the ethical introverts — Dumas, Yesenin, Dreiser, Dostoevsky — are talkative enough to answer the relevant question positively. Dumas, by definition the sensory-ethical introvert, is certainly cheerful, whatever blurry meaning is put into that word. Dumas, "introvert though he is," is sociable and lively. People of this type are able "to have a rollicking good time in noisy company." But to the same degree, some extraverts cannot be characterized as especially cheerful either — for example the logical-sensory extravert Stirlitz (Ls), Zhukov (Sl), or Don Quixote (Il). Here, in fact, the logical blurriness of the concept of cheerfulness becomes visible. So there is no getting by without careful explication of psychological concepts. And to deny socionic introverts a striving for success — the tongue simply refuses; though success must not be understood, say, merely as a career in business. From what has been said it is clear that Nebykova misleads her listeners about Eysenck's understanding of extraversion–introversion — it is precisely in him that "extraversion correlates with sociability and introversion with reserve."

Back at the Vilnius conference in 1991 I advanced the hypothesis that every TIM contains the radicals of all 16 TIMs. This is directly visible in Model A if one attends to all the pairs of adjacent functions forming blocks. But does this entitle us to say that in a specific person's behavior each of the 16 TIMs can manifest at full scale? By no means. Each of the 16 radicals stands in a strictly fixed position in Model A, and that position deforms the radical to such a degree that in its place it remains strictly subordinated to the whole structure of the model, to the integral functioning of the TIM expressed by the radical of the EGO block. An extravert never behaves like an introvert — though an inexperienced or unprofessional observer may on occasion see features of introversion in his behavior. This is because these two socionic categories, exactly like all the rest, are understood by many socionists too straightforwardly and simplistically. A logical type never behaves like an ethical one, and vice versa. An intuitive never behaves like a sensory type, and vice versa. An irrational never behaves like a rational, and vice versa. And so on. Not everyone can accept such assertions — but only because not everyone fully understands the principle of the wholeness and unity of the personality.

Incidentally, this principle was first formulated, and consistently applied in his work, by Adler — never understood by Freud. One and the same act, identical in form, is performed by each person strictly individually; but the outside observer is not given to trace all the nuances of such an act. Of course, besides typological parameters it contains many others. But even the purely typological side of an act is by no means as unambiguous as it may seem at a superficial glance. This is because behind every socionic category stands an archetype — an entity at once basic and virtual, and only with difficulty, and never fully, yielding to description in words.
